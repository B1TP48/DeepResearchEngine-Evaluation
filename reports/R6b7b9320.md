# How Revolutions Remake States and Societies

The French, Russian, and Iranian revolutions arose from different combinations of state crisis, social grievance, and contested political legitimacy—not from economic hardship alone. The French Revolution of 1789 began amid fiscal paralysis and acute subsistence pressures; Russia’s revolutions in 1917 unfolded under the strain of World War I, with February’s overthrow of the monarchy distinct from October’s Bolshevik seizure of power ([Lynn Hunt, “The Global Financial Origins of 1789”](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.7591/9780801467479-004/html); [“The revolutions of 1917–1918”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-russia/revolutions-of-19171918/858CA1E87FF489B55546B959E5A12138); [Oxford Bibliographies, “Russian Revolutions and Civil War, 1917–1921”](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62360/reference-article-abstract/554546917)). Iran’s 1979 revolution followed rapid oil-funded development, unevenly perceived gains, and political exclusion, as diverse opponents of the Shah rallied around overlapping social and ideological claims ([Stanford Iranian Studies, “Governance and Development in Iran”](https://iranian-studies.stanford.edu/publications/governance-and-development-iran); [Nikki Keddie, “Iranian Revolutions in Comparative Perspective”](https://psi203.cankaya.edu.tr/uploads/files/Keddie%2C%20Iranian%20Revolutions%20in%20Comp%20Perspective%20%281983%29.pdf)). In all three cases, mass mobilization and weakened incumbent authority opened the way to new institutions, but the organizations best able to claim and exercise power shaped the settlement. The resulting orders challenged inherited hierarchies yet created new exclusions and inequalities. A useful theory therefore treats revolution as a contingent sequence—pressure, state crisis, organized challenge, and institutional consolidation—rather than a predictable result of grievance.

## Fiscal paralysis, war, and uneven modernization produced distinct crises

France’s pre-revolutionary crisis joined a monarchy unable to secure agreement on tax reform with acute hardship in the subsistence crisis of 1788–89. The fiscal struggle became a dispute over privilege and representation, while rising food prices sharpened the immediate costs of political paralysis for poorer households. Grievances recorded in a parish *cahier* linked unequal access to offices and status with the interests of the “most numerous and useful class,” although a single cahier cannot represent every community ([A Parish Cahier](https://revolution.chnm.org/d/368/); [Cambridge, “The Outbreak of the French Revolution”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/new-cambridge-modern-history/outbreak-of-the-french-revolution/92840DCB4E4B0FBAC47456AC3C842E57)). The scale of a longer-term decline in French living standards remains disputed: one reassessment finds the short-term subsistence crisis clear but calls a sustained crisis “debatable at best.” This makes it more accurate to connect 1789 to interacting fiscal, political, and social conflicts than to a single, established story of general impoverishment ([Jean-Laurent Rosenthal, *The Fruits of Revolution* excerpt](https://beckassets.blob.core.windows.net/product/readingsample/328281/9780521392204_excerpt_001.pdf); [Bossenga, “Origins of the French Revolution”](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1478-0542.2007.00448.x)).

Russia faced a more direct interaction between wartime breakdown and pre-existing demands. World War I worsened shortages and transport disruptions; in Petrograd, snow slowed trains and chronic flour-supply problems contributed to bakery closures. Beyond the cities, peasants’ demands for land reflected local conditions: land quality and the historical prevalence of serfdom help explain regional variation in unrest. Archival work on rural participants cautions against reducing the revolution to urban workers and national party politics ([Cambridge, “War and Revolution, 1914–1917”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-russia/war-and-revolution-19141917/B88B924B180BD9DF0330677F573277B8); [“‘Good Land and Freedom’”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/slavic-review/article/good-land-and-freedom-for-former-serfs-determinants-of-peasant-unrest-in-european-russia-marchoctober-1917/5F6760AA2A5501EB7B7B6166995D3DE9); [Sarah Badcock, “The Russian Revolution: Broadening Understandings of 1917”](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1478-0542.2007.00485.x)). Ideologically, the year held competing claims: the February revolution’s language of liberty and democracy differed from the Bolsheviks’ socialist program. Lenin’s April Theses linked peace and land to transferring power to workers and poor peasants, but Bolsheviks were then a minority in most workers’ soviets ([Lenin, “The Tasks of the Proletariat in the Present Revolution”](https://scalar.usc.edu/works/usm-open-source-history-text-the-world-at-war-world-history-1914-1945/media/Lenin%20April%20Theses.pdf)).

Iran’s pre-revolutionary tension was not simply economic decline. Oil-funded growth expanded schooling, consumption, urbanization, and migration, while inflation, housing costs, shortages, and disruption of livelihoods intensified anxieties about who benefited. One study reports that schooling had tripled since the White Revolution began and nearly half the population was under 16 on the eve of the revolution; it also emphasizes that improved living standards coexisted with inequality and dislocation ([Relli Shechter, “Royal Developmentalism”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00220094251375940)). Professionals, salaried groups, workers, rural classes, clergy, and bazaar merchants did not share a single grievance, but many perceived that political institutions failed to accommodate new expectations or distribute oil-boom gains fairly ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Classes in the Pahlavi Period”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/class-system-vi/); [Stanford Iranian Studies, “Governance and Development in Iran”](https://iranian-studies.stanford.edu/publications/governance-and-development-iran)). The coalition’s liberal, leftist, nationalist, and religious currents could unite against autocracy without agreeing on what should replace it. The comparison thus points to a common mechanism—grievances gaining force when interpreted as evidence of illegitimate rule—but not to one common social cause.

## Mobilizing networks and state defections turned crisis into regime change

None of the three revolutions began under a single command structure. In France, representative deputies, local municipal bodies, National Guards, clubs, and popular crowds all shaped events. The Third Estate declared itself a National Assembly, while the Paris uprising and the spread of municipal revolutions created new local authorities; rural mobilization and the October march to Versailles brought popular demands, including bread, directly into the political crisis ([Cambridge University Press chronology](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805213/68100/frontmatter/9780521368100_frontmatter.pdf); [National Endowment for the Humanities, “The Storming of the Bastille Led to Democracy but Not for Long”](https://www.neh.gov/article/storming-bastille-led-democracy-not-long)). Political clubs widened participation but were not a centralized party machine: Jacobin clubs were locally autonomous and could diverge from Paris ([Charles Walton, “Clubs, parties, factions”](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/87578/)).

Russia’s February uprising began with demonstrations by women textile workers and housewives, spread into strikes and broader protest, and became decisive when garrison soldiers mutinied. The Petrograd Soviet and a Duma-derived Provisional Government then embodied competing claims to authority. February ended the monarchy, not the contest over who would govern. Over the following months, Bolshevik influence grew; in October, the party used the Soviet’s Military Revolutionary Committee and links to the garrison to seize key positions in Petrograd ([“The revolutions of 1917–1918”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-russia/revolutions-of-19171918/858CA1E87FF489B55546B959E5A12138); [“Politics and the Russian Army”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/F6C453E81BB01743DC8105EAB90911E4/9780511615719c3_p64-137_CBO.pdf/army_and_the_revolution_1917.pdf); [“Living the Revolution”](https://www.routledge.com/rsc/downloads/Social_Revolutions_final.pdf)). This distinction matters: spontaneous protest and socialist organizing both contributed, but the later transfer of power relied on more concentrated coordination.

In Iran, clerical, mosque, shrine, bazaar, labor, student, and political networks connected local participation to national opposition. The cycle of protest and mourning after the Qom killings helped demonstrations spread between cities, while oil-worker strikes added disruptive force. Khomeini’s leadership and clerical networks increasingly out-organized secular parties, even though the revolutionary coalition remained ideologically diverse ([Farhad Kazemi, “Politics and Culture in Iran”](https://cdn.isr.umich.edu/pubFiles/historicPublications/Politics_5427_.PDF); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Khomeini i. Life”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/khomeini-i-life/); [“Linkages of oil and politics: oil strikes and dual power in the Iranian revolution”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0023656X.2019.1537018)). The Shah’s departure did not by itself settle the struggle: the military’s declaration of neutrality marked the collapse of the monarchy’s coercive position, while revolutionary committees, Khomeini’s council, the provisional government, and workers’ councils competed for authority ([“Constitutionalism and the Republic”](https://escholarship.org/content/qt45p2n8dd/qt45p2n8dd_noSplash_d0cd8a4bfc6b1f23bdcb0a5dde0d47ea.pdf?t=sfseaz); [“The Showras in the Iranian Revolution”](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004440395/BP000010.pdf)).

International forces shaped each trajectory but did not act as a universal cause. France’s assistance to the American Revolution contributed to its financial burdens, but the evidence does not establish that this alone caused the 1789 rupture. Afterward, émigré organizing, the Pillnitz Declaration, and war with European powers helped intensify revolutionary emergency and radicalization; internal counterrevolution, including the Vendée rebellion, was a distinct part of that struggle ([U.S. Department of State, “U.S. Debt and Foreign Loans, 1775–1795”](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1784-1800/loans/n); [German History in Documents and Images, “Emperor Leopold II and King Frederick William II Meet in Pillnitz”](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/the-holy-roman-empire-1648-1815/emperor-leopold-ii-and-king-frederick-william-ii-meet-in-pillnitz-on-august-25-1791-undated-oil-on-canvas.pdf); [EBSCO, “The Vendee Rebellion and Its Place in the History of European Regional Antirevolutionary and Counterrevolutionary Movements”](https://openurl.ebsco.com/contentitem/doi%3A10.14258/izvasu%282022%293-06?id=ebsco%3Adoi%3A10.14258%2Fizvasu%282022%293-06&sid=ebsco%3Aplink%3Acrawler)). World War I was integral to Russia’s crisis. Germany facilitated Lenin’s return, but claims about German financing remain contested; subsequent Allied intervention aided White forces without defeating the Bolsheviks ([Rex A. Wade, “The Great War, Revolution and the Struggle Over Peace: Russia, 1917”](https://doi.org/10.1080/09546545.2017.1402732); [Semion Lyandres, “The Bolsheviks’ ‘German Gold’ Revisited”](https://carlbeckpapers.pitt.edu/ojs/index.php/cbp/article/view/63); [Cambridge University Press, “Revolution and Intervention, 1917–1920”](https://doi.org/10.1017/9780511844126.010)). In Iran, the memory of the 1953 U.S.- and British-backed coup shaped suspicion of foreign interference, but the evidence does not show that foreign governments engineered the 1979 revolution. U.S. officials debated intervention, while post-revolutionary CIA proposals to weaken the new regime document intent, not demonstrated success; Soviet influence on the revolution itself appears limited ([National Security Archive, “1979 Iran Hostage Crisis Recalled”](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/iran/2019-11-04/1979-iran-hostage-crisis-recalled); [U.S. Department of State, FRUS, “Memorandum … Direct CIA Contact with Shahpour Bakhtiar,” December 11, 1979](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v11p1/d93); [Cambridge University Press, “‘A Priest does not consider the toppling of the Shah as an option’”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/iranian-studies/article/priest-does-not-consider-the-toppling-of-the-shah-as-an-option-the-kgb-and-the-revolution-in-iran/AA4B1304FF407BAA401A17F5BDCA9851)).

## New constitutions replaced old sovereignty, but in different ways

France dismantled the estate-based order but cycled through competing constitutional settlements. The 1791 Constitution established a constitutional monarchy and national sovereignty; the monarchy was abolished in 1792. The 1793 Constitution was never implemented, the 1795 Constitution created a Directory, and the 1799 Constitution concentrated executive power in the Consulate ([Assemblée nationale, “La première Constitution écrite française”](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/histoire-et-patrimoine/revolution-francaise/la-premiere-constitution-ecrite-francaise); [Élysée, “La Constitution du 24 juin 1793”](https://www.elysee.fr/la-presidence/la-constitution-du-24-juin-1793); [Bibliothèque nationale de France, “Constitution (1795)”](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb16684534b); [Assemblée nationale, “La Constitution de l’an VIII : Le Consulat”](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/histoire-et-patrimoine/consulat-et-premier-empire)). The removal of feudal privilege proceeded in stages, with definitive abolition in 1793. Even as political arrangements changed, the department system remained and was reorganized under appointed prefects; the Civil Code of 1804 unified much of the legal order ([Assemblée nationale, “Nuit du 4 août : Abolition des privilèges”](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/histoire-et-patrimoine/revolution-francaise/nuit-du-4-aout-abolition-des-privileges); [Assemblée nationale, “Consulat : centralisation et création du corps préfectoral” and “La codification juridique”](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/histoire-et-patrimoine/consulat-et-premier-empire)). France therefore combined constitutional volatility with more durable administrative and legal consolidation.

Russia’s new rulers made a sharper formal break: the Soviet government abolished the existing general courts and declared a republic of workers’, soldiers’, and peasants’ Soviets. The 1918 Constitution vested supreme authority in Soviet congresses, but the Bolshevik-led government dissolved the elected Constituent Assembly after it met, rejecting its competing claim to sovereignty ([“Decree on the Judicature,” in *First Decrees of Soviet Power*](https://www.marxists.org/history/ussr/government/1917/firstdecreesofsovietpower.pdf); [U.S. Office of the Historian, “Constitution of the Russian Socialist Federated Soviet Republic”](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1918Russiav01/d579); [U.S. Office of the Historian, *Foreign Relations of the United States, 1918, Russia*, “Papers” index](https://history.state.gov//historicaldocuments/frus1918Russiav01/papers)). In practice, party control increasingly overshadowed formal Soviet authority, while later Soviet legal codes also drew on earlier legal systems and European legal thought ([Ivan Sablin, “An antiparliamentary revolution”](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/oa-mono/10.4324/9781003431794-5/antiparliamentary-revolution-ivan-sablin); [Cambridge University Press, “Making Sense of 1917”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/slavic-review/article/making-sense-of-1917-towards-a-global-history-of-the-russian-revolution/EABBD25ED5AD7E6C871EB07A9F84B657)).

Iran abolished the monarchy but combined elected institutions with clerical supervision. Its 1979 Constitution provided for an elected president and parliament alongside a Supreme Leader and Guardian Council, which can assess legislation against Islamic criteria; this made popular voting part of a system in which religious oversight held constitutional authority ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Constitution of the Islamic Republic”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/constitution-of-the-islamic-republic/)). The settlement retained some provisions and forms associated with the earlier constitutional order while replacing monarchy with *velayat-e faqih*. The constitution was later revised rather than replaced: the 1989 changes reshaped executive and judicial arrangements while preserving the Islamic-republican framework ([Library of Congress, *Iran: A Country Study*](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/frd/frdcstdy/ir/irancountrystudy00curt_0/irancountrystudy00curt_0.pdf); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Constitution of the Islamic Republic”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/constitution-of-the-islamic-republic/)). Across the three, revolutionary legitimacy became durable only when embedded in successor institutions, but those institutions did not necessarily preserve the breadth of the coalitions that had brought them to power.

## Social gains coexisted with renewed hierarchies

France abolished legal estates and feudal obligations and redistributed Church land, but redistribution did not yield uniform equality. District-level research associates more extensive revolutionary land redistribution with higher agricultural productivity and investment in the mid-nineteenth century, while also finding greater land inequality; the productivity advantage later diminished ([“The Effects of Land Redistribution: Evidence from the French Revolution”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/713688)). Religious policy was equally unsettled: revolutionary governments reorganized and attacked Catholic institutions, while later arrangements alternated between conflict, toleration, and state supervision. Napoleon restored Catholicism as the religion of the majority under state oversight ([Suzanne Desan, “The French Revolution and Religion, 1795–1815”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-christianity/french-revolution-and-religion-17951815/23080915D6DF1353015D048BF043A357)). Women participated politically but did not gain political citizenship; reforms to marriage and divorce were followed by Napoleonic law that reinforced husbands’ authority ([“Women’s Roles, Rights and Representations in France, 1758–1848”](https://academic.oup.com/liverpool-scholarship-online/book/30930/chapter/262610347); [“The Achievement of Female Suffrage in Europe: On Women’s Citizenship”](https://academic.oup.com/icon/article/12/1/4/628588)).

Russia replaced inherited status with state-defined class and party hierarchies, while expanding some routes to education and employment. Early Soviet higher education admitted more workers and peasants, but institutional stratification and unequal selection persisted; women’s educational and employment opportunities expanded without removing unequal domestic labor ([David Lane, “The Impact of Revolution”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/003803857300700206); [“Anatomy of a Stalled Revolution”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0891243218776309)). The Soviet project also attacked the imperial religious order and promoted a “New Soviet Person,” yet religious practices and communities continued to shape experience. Its formal recognition of nationalities and territories altered how populations were classified, although the scope and consistency of nationality policy remain debated ([*Religion and the Russian Revolution*](https://iupress.org/9780253076175/religion-and-the-russian-revolution/); [“Was There a Soviet Nationality Policy?”](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/ceasxx/v71y2019i6p972-993.html)).

Iran’s postrevolutionary order reshuffled class position and public identity, but the changes were neither uniform nor permanent. Census-based research finds sharp changes in employment and class categories after the revolution, followed by movement in some respects toward the prerevolutionary structure by 2006; the authors caution that census categories cannot finely distinguish all class positions ([Sohrab Behdad and Farhad Nomani, “What a Revolution! Thirty Years of Social Class Reshuffling in Iran”](https://personal.denison.edu/~behdad/publications/WhataRevolution-10.pdf)). The Islamic state sought to replace Pahlavi-era symbols with a new account of national memory, without evidence that all citizens adopted a single identity ([“The Politics of Street Names: Reconstructing Iran’s Collective Identity”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/sena.12393)). Women’s early legal and political position worsened, even as later gains in education and health coexisted with weaker gains in employment and political participation. Their revolutionary participation and expanding public presence therefore existed in tension with state-enforced gender norms and constraints on independent organizing ([Valentine M. Moghadam, “Revolutions and Women’s Rights”](https://www.aku.edu/ismc/publications/Documents/AFAOcassionalPaperSeries_Issue1.pdf)). Across the cases, revolutionary openings could be narrowed or redirected by later law and state policy; neither participation nor social equality followed automatically from regime change.

## A conjunctural framework clarifies pathways, not dates

A strong comparative framework combines structural pressure with political agency. Skocpol’s account emphasizes international pressure on states, elite conflict, administrative breakdown, and autonomous social revolt—especially the interaction of state crisis and peasant action in France and Russia ([Theda Skocpol, “France, Russia, China: A Structural Analysis of Social Revolutions”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/B137B1993FA19B8044F46F1D55C652F8/S0010417500008197a.pdf/france-russia-china-a-structural-analysis-of-social-revolutions.pdf)). Foran’s framework adds political cultures of resistance, opposition organizations, coalition-building, economic downturn, and openings in the international environment ([John Foran, *Taking Power: On the Origins of Third World Revolutions*](https://www.iicat.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/Taking-Power-by-John-Foran-2005-Cambridge-University-Press.pdf)). Together, these accounts explain why pressure alone is insufficient: a state must become vulnerable, opposition must coordinate well enough to challenge it, and the organizations that control successor institutions must be able to make their claims binding. Iran particularly demonstrates the importance of leadership and ideological competition in determining the settlement; the outcomes for women, for example, reflected the postrevolutionary forces that built the state, not simply women’s participation in the overthrow ([Moghadam, “Revolutions and Women’s Rights”](https://www.aku.edu/ismc/publications/Documents/AFAOcassionalPaperSeries_Issue1.pdf)).

This framework supports conditional scenarios, not forecasts about a specific country or date. If authorities preserve administrative capacity and elite and security-force cohesion while implementing credible reforms and maintaining channels for negotiation, pressure may be defused or redirected into institutional change. If economic or fiscal stress coincides with elite division, persistent cross-group mobilization, and opposition networks able to coordinate a shared program, a revolutionary breakthrough becomes more plausible. If opposition groups can remove incumbent leaders but cannot agree on successor rules, regime change may leave established institutions powerful or allow a narrower, better-organized faction to dominate. And if security institutions remain cohesive while opposition networks are fragmented, repression may contain unrest or contribute to prolonged, fragmented conflict. These pathways follow from the distinction between state breakdown, opposition coordination, and control of successor institutions in comparative accounts, rather than from any single indicator ([Turchin, Gavrilets, and Goldstone, “Linking ‘Micro’ to ‘Macro’ Models of State Breakdown to Improve Methods for Political Forecasting”](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt1xt9k875/qt1xt9k875.pdf); [Foran, *Taking Power*](https://www.iicat.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/Taking-Power-by-John-Foran-2005-Cambridge-University-Press.pdf)).

A practical assessment would track fiscal and economic stress alongside elite cohesion, administrative capacity, security-force loyalty, protest persistence and spread, labor and other organizational participation, opposition coordination, repression, and external pressures. Institutional and participation measures such as V-Dem can help monitor political change, but do not by themselves measure fiscal strain, opposition unity, or security-force cohesion ([V-Dem Dataset, version 16](https://v-dem.net/data/the-v-dem-dataset/)). Protest size or hardship alone cannot distinguish a breakthrough from contained unrest. The historical cases offer mechanisms and cautions, not a universal threshold or template: they differ in social structure, institutions, communications, and the organization of coercive power, and the evidence does not establish a single causal weighting that applies across them.

## Conclusion

The most consequential turning point is not simply the outbreak of protest but the interaction between state capacity, opposition organization, and coercive loyalty. This explains both why deep grievances can persist without revolution and why an overthrow does not guarantee a more inclusive or stable order.

For present-day analysis, the cases are most useful as a framework for updating conditional scenarios. Watch whether institutional weakness and coalition-building develop together, and distinguish the fall of a government from the struggle to control the institutions that follow. Exact timing and outcomes remain uncertain because those transitions depend on contingent decisions and changing alignments, not on a fixed historical sequence.
