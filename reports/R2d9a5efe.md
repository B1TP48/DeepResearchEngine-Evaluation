# Elite Splits and State Breakdown Drove Three Revolutions

Across 1789 France, 1917 Russia and 1979 Iran, fiscal or wartime strain weakened the state, elites or clerical networks split from it, and mass mobilisation exploited the gap. In France, **the crown's finances were the immediate trigger**, and the 1788–89 subsistence crisis turned fiscal grievance into open revolt ([Giommoni, Tabellini & Loumeau, HBS WP 25-047](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/25-047_3885514c-63a6-4d68-aee2-3ba7977c827f.pdf)). In Russia, the war compounded a small, strike-prone industrial workforce and a largely peasant empire, and **nearly 1.5 million workers struck in the first half of 1914** ([Economy, Tsarist](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/economy-tsarist)). In Iran, **oil revenues quadrupled after 1973**, and the Shah's spending on weapons and grand projects produced inflation, bottlenecks and corruption ([Persian Heritage](https://persianheritage.org/articles/en/iranian-revolution-1979)). The outcomes diverged sharply: France moved from constitutional reform through Terror to a military-backed Consulate, Russia ended in a one-party state after civil war, and Iran produced a clerical constitution that survived the 2022 protests by repression. The comparative literature supports a structural-plus-contingency model, but the forecasting literature is weak. The best-known instability model reports high accuracy that independent reanalysis disputes, and no published model targets revolution specifically. Any future scenario should therefore be framed as conditional, with its indicators and base rates stated.

## Fiscal strain and land grievance preceded all three upheavals

The French case shows how fiscal crisis can become political. Historians generally treat state finances as the main short-term cause, and the fiscal crisis traces back to war finance, since **Necker financed the war by borrowing**, and Calonne used the same method to clear arrears ([Lefebvre, The French Revolution](http://www.elimeyerhoff.com/books/Deleuze/Lefebvre%20-%20The%20French%20Revolution.pdf)). Newer quantitative work refines this. The **salt tax accounted for 22% of royal revenues in 1780**, and areas with higher tax rates saw more revolts before 1789 ([VoxEU/CEPR](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/extractive-taxation-and-french-revolution)). That working paper is not yet peer-reviewed, and it also reports that the hot summer of 1788 and a harsh winter pushed prices higher ([HBS WP 25-047](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/25-047_3885514c-63a6-4d68-aee2-3ba7977c827f.pdf)). Historians still dispute whether debt was exceptional, since one encyclopaedia notes that French debt was not high compared with Britain's ([Wikipedia, French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution)).

The Russian preconditions were structural and agrarian. By the war's outbreak the empire had **roughly 170 million people, mostly rural, with fewer than 10% working in industry**, and only about a quarter of peasants held individual title to their land ([Economy, Tsarist](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/economy-tsarist)). Conscription then drained labour from peasant holdings and cut food output ([AlphaHistory](https://alphahistory.com/russianrevolution/world-war-i/)). Historians weight these factors differently. Figes places peasant land hunger, worker militancy and intelligentsia alienation at the centre, with the war as the detonator ([Soviet-union.com](https://soviet-union.com/russian-revolution/overview)). Pipes gives more weight to the state's collapse and to organised minorities, and argued the February collapse should not be attributed mainly to war fatigue ([Tracesofevil, quoting Pipes](https://www.tracesofevil.com/2014/03/russian-revolution-historiography.html)). That blog summary should be checked against Pipes's own text.

Iran's preconditions were created by top-down modernisation. The White Revolution of 1963 was a set of reforms that the clergy opposed, and poorly implemented land reform pushed rural people into urban slums ([Persian Heritage](https://persianheritage.org/articles/en/iranian-revolution-1979)). A diaspora study citing Kazemi (1980) attributes disillusionment to unequal distribution of oil wealth, rural-urban migration and weak job creation for a growing youth population ([Iranian Diaspora Cooperation & Development Council](https://iranians.global/guide/roots-of-the-1979-revolution-and-diaspora-activism/)). The precise economic figures are poorly sourced. One partisan Trotskyist-aligned article reports manufacturing wages rising 2.6% in 1977 against 27.3% inflation ([Red Flag](https://redflag.org.au/article/stolen-revolution-iran-1979/)), but that figure needs checking against a statistical source. Across the three cases, economic grievance is a consistent precondition, but its causal weight relative to politics remains contested.

## Ideology and leadership moved from reform toward radical or clerical control

In France, the ideological vocabulary came from the Enlightenment, but the sources differ on how much it drove events. **Sieyès's January 1789 pamphlet** argued that the Third Estate was France's only legitimate estate ([Wikipedia, What Is the Third Estate?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Is_the_Third_Estate%3F)), and a scholarly study finds that his sovereignty theory does not adopt Rousseau's *volonté générale* ([Studies in the History of Political Thought](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/54649/9789004273993.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)). One line of scholarship argues that natural-law reasoning radicalised the revolution over the decade, while another stresses historic rights and patriotism in 1788–89 ([Journal of the History of Ideas](https://academic.oup.com/jsh/article/52/3/545/5159508)). The leadership sequence ran from elite-led reform to Paris-based radicals. The **Girondins fell after the Commission of Twelve**, which led to the revolt of 2 June 1793 and the start of the Terror ([Wikipedia, Commission of Twelve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_of_Twelve)). The **Committee of Public Safety** received executive power in December 1793 ([EPFL concept page](https://graphsearch.epfl.ch/en/concept/158248)). After Thermidor, the revolution became a Directory governed until **9 November 1799** ([Wikipedia, French Directory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Directory)), and Brumaire replaced it with a Consulate in which **all actual power was held by the First Consul, Bonaparte** ([Wikipedia, Constitution of the Year VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Year_VIII)).

Russia's ideological contest centred on the Constituent Assembly and the soviets. The Bolsheviks first backed the Constituent Assembly alongside the other socialists, then came to oppose the parliamentary bodies in favour of the soviets ([Russian Constituent Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Constituent_Assembly)). The **Socialist Revolutionaries won about 45.5% of the vote and the Bolsheviks 24.9%** ([Encyclopedia of Ukraine](https://www.encyclopediaofukraine.com/display.asp?linkpath=pages\A\L\All6RussianConstituentAssembly.htm)). The seat totals conflict. One source gives the SRs 324 of 767 seats and the Bolsheviks 183 ([Russian Constituent Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Constituent_Assembly)), while Tony Cliff's account describes the SRs as winning a clear seat majority ([Cliff, Lenin 3](https://www.marxists.org/archive/cliff/works/1978/lenin3/ch03.html)). The Wikipedia figure is the more precise and the only one given in seat terms, and 324 of 767 is not a majority, so Cliff's wording is hard to reconcile with it. Power then passed to the soviets. The **Provisional Government, led by Prince Lvov, held formal authority**, while the Petrograd Soviet controlled practical levers such as troops and railways ([February Revolution, Military History Fandom](https://military-history.fandom.com/wiki/February_Revolution); [Dual Power, 1914–1918-Online](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/article/dual-power/)). **Sovnarkom was formed on 8 November 1917**, the **Cheka was created on 7 December 1917** ([Government of Vladimir Lenin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Vladimir_Lenin); [Boston University](https://sites.bu.edu/revolutionaryrussia/student-research/agatha-leach/)), and the Constituent Assembly, which met on 18 January 1918, was dispersed by troops after it rejected the demand to recognise Soviet authority ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Constituent-Assembly-Russian-government)).

Iran's ideology was more layered, and its institutional outcome more deliberate. **Khomeini set out clerical government in Najaf lectures in 1970**, published as *Islamic Government*, and velayat-e faqih became the founding principle of the Islamic Republic ([Wikipedia, Ideology of the Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology_of_the_Iranian_Revolution); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/velayat-e-faqih)). Before victory, the revolution's ideology was described as imprecise, with its theocratic character not yet public, and protest slogans combined "Independence, Freedom, and Islamic Republic" ([Wikipedia, Ideology of the Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology_of_the_Iranian_Revolution)). Leadership moved quickly. Khomeini **appointed Bazargan prime minister on 4 February 1979**, but Bazargan's government lacked security forces, and control of the streets passed to clerics in the Revolutionary Council and the IRP ([Interim Government of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interim_Government_of_Iran_(1979)); [Iran Chamber](https://www.iranchamber.com/history/islamic_revolution/revolution_and_iran_after1979_1.php)). Bazargan resigned on 6 November 1979 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mehdi-Bazargan)). The **Council of the Islamic Revolution was the undisputed government** until the first parliament convened in August 1980 ([Council of the Islamic Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_the_Islamic_Revolution)). The constitution then created the **Assembly of Experts and the Guardian Council**, which vets candidates and holds a veto over parliamentary laws ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/irans-ruling-structure-explained-277913)).

## Wars and foreign powers radicalised revolutions and drove repression

Foreign war was central to France's radicalisation. **Austria and Prussia's Declaration of Pillnitz on 27 August 1791** threatened revolutionary France with military action ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/War_of_the_First_Coalition/)), but many Girondins also **wanted a foreign war** because they believed it would rally the nation ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/early-wars-french-revolution/)). The Assembly declared war on Austria on 20 April 1792 ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/early-wars-french-revolution/)). The Brunswick Manifesto intimidated Paris and helped spur radicalisation ([Lumen Learning](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-worldhistory/chapter/22-4-3-foreign-intervention/)). Sources conflict on its date. Lumen and EBSCO point to 25 July 1792, while Wikipedia notes that news reached Paris on 1 August ([Insurrection of 10 August 1792](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurrection_of_10_August_1792)). Furet argued the 1792 war was intrinsic to the revolution rather than an external accident ([Wikipedia, François Furet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Furet)), a claim that divides historians.

The Terror was a response to war, rebellion and treason accusations ([Wikipedia, Reign of Terror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reign_of_Terror)), but its scale is measured differently across sources. Britannica reports that **over 200,000 citizens were detained under the Law of Suspects**, that **about 10,000 died in prison**, and that **about 17,000 death sentences** were handed down, 72% for armed rebellion in the federalist southeast and the Vendée ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/France/The-Reign-of-Terror)). An aggregator gives about 16,594 death sentences ([Grokipedia](https://grokipedia.com/page/Committee_of_Public_Safety)), which is close but less reliable. Wikipedia cites a figure of 500,000 accused suspects ([Law of Suspects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Suspects)). These are different categories and should not be combined. Historians also disagree on the Terror's character. Jacobson argues that the Jacobins were part of a "revolutionary resistance" that enacted temporary, ruthless laws ([LRB](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v39/n06/gavin-jacobson/there-is-no-more-vendee)), while the traditional reading treats the Terror as a political choice by the leadership ([Brewminate](https://brewminate.com/streets-red-with-blood-the-french-revolution-and-the-reign-of-terror/)).

Russia's external shocks were more decisive. **The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk was signed on 3 March 1918**, and Russia lost territories holding more than a quarter of its population and more than a third of its grain harvest ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union/Brest-Litovsk)). Britannica argues that the treaty nonetheless saved the Bolshevik regime by bringing eight months of German diplomatic and financial support ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union/Brest-Litovsk)). Germany's supplementary treaty of 27 August 1918 extended its economic rights, and the Bolshevik legislature annulled the treaty on 13 November 1918 ([Lumen Learning](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-hccc-worldhistory2/chapter/the-treaty-of-brest-litovsk/)). Allied intervention began in spring 1918 ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/russian-civil-war-1918-1921)). An International Socialism article argues that intervention prolonged the war by sustaining the Whites with supplies, and that White mutinies contributed to their failure ([International Socialism](http://isj.org.uk/the-circle-of-death-of-the-counter-revolution-allied-intervention-in-bolshevik-russia/)). Intervention also helped the Bolsheviks present themselves as defenders against foreign invaders ([HistoryCrunch](https://historycrunch.com/red-army-vs-white-army-in-the-russian-revolution/)). The domestic counter-revolution was weak in its appeal to peasants. The Whites often failed to win over local populations who feared a return to the Tsarist order, and the Greens refused to join either side ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Russian_Civil_War/)). The **Red Terror followed the attempt on Lenin's life on 30 August 1918** ([New World Encyclopedia](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Red_Terror)), and a decree of 5 September 1918 ordered the Cheka to isolate class enemies in camps ([John Clare](https://www.johndclare.net/Russ7_RedTerror.htm)). Sources conflict on the Terror's end, with one dating it to 6 November 1918 ([prlib.ru](https://www.prlib.ru/en/section/1173285)) and another giving August 1918 to February 1922 ([Wikipedia, Red Terror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Terror)).

Iran's international dimension was shaped by US policy and the hostage crisis. The Carter administration was **divided between Vance's preference for engagement and Brzezinski's preference for confrontation** ([Wikipedia, Foreign policy of the Carter administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_policy_of_the_Carter_administration)). The **Shah arrived in New York on 22 October 1979**, and thirteen days later protesters stormed the embassy ([Gilder Lehrman Institute](https://www.gilderlehrman.org/history-resources/spotlight-primary-source/breaking-diplomatic-ties-iran-during-hostage-crisis-1980)). Reports of the number held differ, with Britannica Kids giving 66 seized and 52 held for more than a year ([Britannica Kids](https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/Iran-hostage-crisis/624580)). The crisis gave the new state a foreign enemy around which to consolidate. Iraq then **invaded in September 1980**, expecting a quick victory given Iran's post-revolutionary chaos ([History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/iran-iraq-war)). Internal repression ran alongside the war. Kurdish rebellion began in mid-March 1979 ([Wikipedia, 1979 Kurdish rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_Kurdish_rebellion_in_Iran)), and the **MEK's June 1981 street battles with the Revolutionary Guard killed hundreds** ([Wikipedia, Iran–Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War)). War deaths are highly contested. Kurzman reports estimates above 600,000 from one dataset and 500,000 Iraqi and 750,000 Iranian deaths from another, while a systematic review gives **188,015 to 217,489 Iranians killed** ([Kurzman](https://kurzman.unc.edu/death-tolls-of-the-iran-iraq-war/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23981159/)). Figures should be presented as ranges.

## Theories explain the sequence well but predict poorly

The main theories agree on a sequence more than on causes. Skocpol's state-centred model treats revolution as state breakdown combined with class-based revolt from below, and she rejects voluntarist accounts that stress leadership ([LSE review](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/101503/1/International_Affairs_Skocpol_review.pdf)). Goldstone's demographic-structural model makes fiscal crisis and elite division the core of state breakdown and explicitly names 1789 France as a case ([Sociostudies](https://www.sociostudies.org/journal/articles/3353532/); [AbeBooks](https://www.abebooks.com/9780520067585/Revolution-Rebellion-Early-Modern-World-0520067584/plp)). Brinton's stage model describes moderates, then extremists, a Reign of Terror and a return to moderation across England, France and Russia ([Wikipedia, The Anatomy of Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution)). Davies' J-curve predicts revolt after a sharp reversal of expectations and cites 1917 as a fit ([Heliyon 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9615042/)). Goodwin's *No Other Way Out* argues that authoritarian regimes unwittingly channel resistance into radical directions ([Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/9780521629485)).

These theories fit France and Russia better than Iran, though the sources reviewed give no Iran-specific predictions from them. Skocpol's model accounts for France and Russia as state breakdown plus peasant revolt ([Wikipedia, States and Social Revolutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_and_Social_Revolutions)). Goodwin's regime-type argument offers a plausible reading of Iran, where a repressive monarchy channelled resistance toward a clerical and militant alternative, though that application is this report's inference rather than a claim made in the sources. The critiques are substantive. Geddes argues that Skocpol selected cases on the dependent variable, which weakens her test ([Insights and Pitfalls](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/240457176_Insights_and_Pitfalls_Selection_Bias_in_Qualitative_Research)), although a defence holds that the design survives if state crisis is only necessary rather than sufficient ([Freyburg, Warwick](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/cross_fac/q-step/currentstudents/qs103/schedule/2_cases.pdf)). Davies' J-curve has weak empirical support: one early CIA-held study found his relative-deprivation measure of little value ([CIA reading room](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp86b00985r000100070018-6)). Brinton's model fits poorly with its own cases, since the American and English revolutions lacked a Reign of Terror ([Lucky Bookshelf](https://www.luckybookshelf.com/the-anatomy-of-revolution-by-crane-brinton/)).

The central disagreement is whether leadership and ideology are causes or effects. Skocpol treats them as effects of structural openings, while the fourth-generation work argues that leadership, ideology and identification should be treated as partly independent causes, especially in communist and developing-country cases ([Varsity Tutors](https://www.varsitytutors.com/practice/subjects/college-political-science/lessons/revolutions-and-regime-change); [SSRN, Goldstone 2001](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1531902)). The three cases support both readings. Lenin's leadership and the Bolshevik pivot from the Constituent Assembly to the soviets look contingent, yet the agrarian and war conditions that made the soviets powerful look structural. A consistent framework should therefore treat fiscal and military strain as enabling conditions, elite or clerical fracture as the proximate trigger, and leadership and ideology as variables that determine which of several outcomes follows.

## Forecasting future scenarios with indicators and caveats

Published forecasting models are useful for broad instability but weak for revolution. The Goldstone et al. (2010) model reported **81.7% of instability onsets correctly classified across instability types** at a two-year lead ([Goldstone et al., AJPS 2010](https://www.systemicpeace.org/vlibrary/PITFForecastingInstabilityAJPS2010.pdf)). That figure comes from a case-control design, and a secondary summary claims **85.7% of onset countries in the top predicted quintile** out of sample for 1995–2004 ([ResearchGate, "The Future is a Moving Target"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331233334_The_Future_is_a_Moving_Target_Predicting_Political_Instability)). The same reanalysis argues the model's out-of-sample predictions are inaccurate. Gary King's critique of the State Failure Task Force found no out-of-sample tests had been conducted by the task force's own reports ([King, World Politics 2001](https://gking.harvard.edu/files/civil.pdf)). A reanalysis of Fearon and Laitin's civil-war model found it **fails to predict any of 107 civil-war onsets** in one setting ([Ward, Greenhill & Bakke](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/0022343309356491)). Blair and Sambanis conclude that models built on these theories produce surprisingly inaccurate forecasts ([Blair & Sambanis, JCR 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0022002720918923)). A 2021 machine-learning study reports **AUPRC values of 0.108 and 0.115** for PITF-based models ([Explainable models, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8321219/)). These low values mean that even with high overall accuracy, most flagged cases are false alarms.

The most consistent indicator is regime type. A secondary summary of the Goldstone model says **partial democracies with factionalism face over 30 times greater relative odds of instability than full autocracies** ([Academia.edu summary](https://www.academia.edu/2815121/A_global_model_for_forecasting_political_instability)), but an independent re-estimation found that the participation-competitiveness variable outperformed factionalism ([arXiv 1409.7105](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.7105)). Economic and state-capacity variables mostly appear as controls, and the sources reviewed do not show that economic shocks alone predict onset out of sample. Elite defection, security-force loyalty, fiscal stress and external pressure lack validated out-of-sample tests, which is a serious gap given that these are the mechanisms the historical cases emphasise.

Current data sources are uneven. **V-Dem's version 16, published in March 2026, covers 182 countries through 2025** ([V-Dem Methodology v16](https://www.v-dem.net/documents/71/methodology_v16.pdf)). Polity's numerical data run only to 2018, and **US funding for Polity ended on 29 February 2020** ([Systemic Peace](https://www.systemicpeace.org/)). ACLED's CAST forecasting tool **will be retired on 30 November 2026** ([ACLED](https://acleddata.com/conflict-data)), which is nine days after this report's date, so any model using CAST inputs will need replacing. The World Bank revised its fragility classifications from July 2026, separating a Public FCV List from an Institutional Fragility List ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/en/brief/2026/07/01/classification-of-fragile-and-conflict-affected-situations)). The Fragile States Index has a 2026 edition, but its release date was confirmed only through a third-party post ([Think To DO Institute](https://thinktodoinstitute.com/fsi-2026/)).

Scenario methods have their own known limits. Tetlock's work finds that expert predictions over multi-year horizons are often overconfident and rarely better than chance ([CIA, Limits of Prediction](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Limits-of-Prediction.pdf); [Foreign Policy](https://foreignpolicy.com/2012/09/07/trending-upward/)). Lustick and Tetlock argue that contingency planning needs conditional forecasts, possibly from simulation ([Lustick & Tetlock](https://www.polisci.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/LUSTICK%20ffo2.64_Simulation%20Manifesto_Lustick_Tetlock_0.pdf)). No source reviewed applies scenario methods to revolution onset or evaluates them against outcomes, so scenarios should be presented as structured judgements rather than validated forecasts.

Given these limits, three scenario types follow from the historical cases. The first is a **fiscal-elite fracture in a factionalised partial democracy**, the France pattern, where the signal to watch is splits within the ruling coalition alongside fiscal stress. Factionalism has the strongest published support, while fiscal stress does not. The second is a **war-driven breakdown**, the Russia pattern, where conscription, food shortage and labour loss trigger mass protest. External shocks have no out-of-sample test, but the 1915–17 record shows how war strain interacted with urban and rural grievance. The third is **repressive consolidation with a legitimacy gap**, the Iran pattern after 2022. The 2022 crackdown on protests after Mahsa Amini's death was condemned by UN experts for killings and arbitrary arrests ([OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2022/10/iran-crackdown-peaceful-protests-death-jina-mahsa-amini-needs-independent)), and two years on observers argued that order had been restored but not legitimacy ([Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/two-years-after-mahsa-amini-protests-iran-restores-order-but-not-legitimacy/)). Amnesty's summary, as reported by Wikipedia, says the 2025–2026 protests were suppressed in similar ways on a larger scale ([Wikipedia, Mahsa Amini protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahsa_Amini_protests)), but no primary source on those events was retrieved. The breakdown risk in this scenario depends on security-force loyalty, a variable that no source reviewed tests.

## Conclusion

The three revolutions show a durable sequence of fiscal or wartime strain, elite or clerical fracture, and mobilisation, but the outcomes depended on contingent choices about leadership, ideology and foreign pressure. France's revolution radicalised under war and then ended in military rule. Russia's Bolsheviks used a war-weakened state and a German treaty to consolidate power, and then governed through the Cheka and War Communism until the NEP forced a retreat. Iran's revolution gave clerical networks control within months and built unelected institutions into the constitution. The common lesson is that the state's capacity to absorb shocks, and the cohesion of its elites and security forces, determined whether a crisis became a revolution.

For forecasting, the strongest practical guidance is caution. The best-known instability models are weak for revolution, and their accuracy claims conflict across sources. A useful monitoring frame would track regime type and factionalism, which have the most support, alongside war or fiscal shocks and elite splits, which have strong historical grounding but no validated out-of-sample test. Any scenario should state its base rate, its false-positive risk and the data source used, with V-Dem as the current democracy measure and the CAST replacement as an immediate practical task.
