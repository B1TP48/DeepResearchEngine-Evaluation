# Separate Formal Guarantees from Human Alignment

No unconditional theorem establishes that a self-improving AI will remain aligned with human values indefinitely through arbitrary recursive self-modification; nor do the cited results prove that such a guarantee is impossible for every possible design. A mathematical guarantee is possible for a **specified formal property** if the system starts in a state satisfying it and every permitted transition—including self-modifications—preserves it. Induction then establishes the property at every step of an unbounded modeled execution ([Certified Self-Modifying Code](https://flint.cs.yale.edu/certikos/publications/smctr.pdf)). But this proves preservation of the property encoded in the model, not that the property captures human values or that the model covers a real system’s full behavior ([Gödel Machines](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0309048); [Iason Gabriel, “Artificial Intelligence, Values, and Alignment”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11023-020-09539-2)). General results in computability theory also rule out a total, always-correct verifier for certain behavioral properties of arbitrary programs, without ruling out proofs for particular systems or restricted classes ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Recursive Functions”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/recursive-functions/)). The defensible answer is therefore **conditional possibility for formal proxies, but no established indefinite guarantee of alignment with human values themselves**.

## An invariant can be preserved indefinitely in a formal model

“Alignment” and “recursive self-modification” do not have one shared formal meaning. Alignment may mean following instructions, intentions, preferences, interests, or rights, which are not interchangeable targets ([Iason Gabriel, “Artificial Intelligence, Values, and Alignment”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11023-020-09539-2)). Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning, for example, models an agent learning a human’s initially unknown reward function in a cooperative game; it formalizes alignment relative to that reward, rather than proving that the reward fully represents human values ([Hadfield-Menell et al., “Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.03137). Self-improvement might mean changing code, parameters, or policy, and “indefinitely” must specify whether a property must hold on every step, every possible trajectory, or with a stated probability. A formal model of recursive self-improvement notes that “friendliness” is not defined by its framework, while broader accounts also describe systems whose requirements may evolve or remain underspecified ([Matt Mahoney, “A Model for Recursively Self Improving Programs”](https://www.mattmahoney.net/rsi.pdf); [Szer and Char, “Growing Recursive Self-Improvers”](https://web.media.mit.edu/~kris/ftp/AGI16_growing_recursive-self-improvers.pdf)).

The basic preservation argument is direct. Let \(x\) encode the system’s code, runtime state, and relevant environment; let \(x\to x'\) mean any permitted execution or modification step; and let \(A(x)\) be the formal property to preserve. If the initial state satisfies \(A(x_0)\), and every permitted transition from an \(A\)-state leads to another \(A\)-state, induction establishes \(A\) at every finite step. That covers every step of an infinite execution **in the modeled system**. If alignment instead concerns actions or whole trajectories, the specification must express that; a state invariant may not capture it. In a stochastic system, “always,” “with probability one,” and “with probability at least a stated threshold” are distinct claims.

There are constructive results for this narrower task. *Certified Self-Modifying Code* defines safety over every natural-number step count and proves preservation for self-modifying code in its formal machine model. Its GCAP2 system establishes progress and preservation for well-formed worlds, and its parametric specifications can cover families of code blocks without enumerating each one, including infinite families ([Certified Self-Modifying Code](https://flint.cs.yale.edu/certikos/publications/smctr.pdf)). Proof-carrying code offers a related mechanism: a proposed program supplies a machine-checkable proof that it meets a specified policy, and a host checks the proof before execution ([Necula, “Proof-Carrying Code”](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/teaching/6.893/readings/necula-popl97.pdf)). These results show that code change need not defeat an invariant proof. They do not show that an open-ended AI’s changing models, tools, hardware, and environment are all captured by the formal machine.

## Preservation depends on the specification and verifier staying in scope

A recursive guarantee requires more than proving one successor safe. The initial system must satisfy the property; every relevant transition must be modeled; the proof system and checker must be sound; and the mechanism admitting changes must prevent unverified modifications, including changes to the checker or specification. If the target may evolve, the update rule itself needs specification. Preserving a fixed target indefinitely could preserve an earlier objective while no longer tracking later human values.

Gödel machines illustrate both the potential and the conditional nature of proof-gated self-modification. They model code changes admitted after a proof that a change is useful relative to a formal utility function and axioms describing the machine and environment. Their global-optimality result depends on consistency of the underlying formal system and compares executing a proved change with continuing proof search; it does not establish that the encoded utility is correct or that the system will find every useful change ([Schmidhuber, “Gödel Machines,” Theorem 4.1](https://sferics.idsia.ch/pub/juergen/gm4.pdf)). A potentially useful change whose value is undecidable may be ignored under the model ([Schmidhuber, “Gödel Machines”](https://sferics.idsia.ch/pub/juergen/gm4.pdf)).

Other agent models make their assumptions similarly explicit. Everitt and colleagues’ goal-preservation result relies on evaluating future outcomes using the current utility and anticipating the consequences of self-modification; the authors treat the initial utility as what designers want, rather than proving that it captures human values. Their result also does not guarantee good behavior on unreachable histories ([“Self-Modification of Policy and Utility Function in Rational Agents”](https://www.hutter1.net/publ/selfmod.pdf)). In proof-based successor reasoning, a parent’s safety argument depends on conditions such as preserving the safety predicate, outcome model, and safe fallback policy. The discussed “descending trust” approach can license finite successor chains, not an infinite chain ([“Vingean Reflection: Reliable Reasoning for Self-Improving Agents”](https://intelligence.org/files/VingeanReflection.pdf)). Thus a proof about a successor is only as reliable as its assumptions about the successor’s implementation, model, and proof mechanism.

## A proof of the proxy does not prove it represents human values

Formal verification can establish conformity to a stated reward or safety policy; it cannot, by itself, settle whether that policy is the right account of human values. Cooperative inverse reinforcement learning formalizes uncertainty about a human reward, and value-alignment verification studies whether behavior matches explicit or implicit values under specified conditions. Neither result establishes that its formalized target is a complete or normatively correct account of human values ([Hadfield-Menell et al., “Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.03137); [Brown et al., “Value Alignment Verification”](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/brown21a.html)). Gabriel’s analysis treats the choice of whose values count and how disagreements should be handled as distinct from the technical task of encoding a target ([Iason Gabriel, “Artificial Intelligence, Values, and Alignment”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11023-020-09539-2)).

The uncertainty is not merely theoretical: a review of 94 value-alignment papers reports that most do not define “values” and often use preferences as a substitute ([“Toward a Theory of Value in AI Alignment”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10327)). A review focused on long-term objective retention describes provable retention of ethical human values as a research challenge, particularly where dynamics and recursive self-improvement are difficult to formalize ([Fasoro, “Engineering AI for Provable Retention of Objectives over Time”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/aaai.12167)). A fixed formal target can be preserved perfectly and still be a mistaken, incomplete, or outdated proxy. A dynamic target might track changing values, but then the authority and rules for updating it become part of what must be guaranteed.

## Undecidability limits general verification, not every possible guarantee

Rice’s theorem rules out a general decision procedure for every nontrivial semantic property of arbitrary programs in its scope. It does not rule out verifying a particular program, checking restricted architectures, or proving properties that are syntactic rather than behavioral ([Rice’s theorem, 1953](https://users-cs.au.dk/amoeller/spa/1-TIP.pdf); [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Recursive Functions”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/recursive-functions/)). Since arbitrary recursive modifications can be arbitrary program changes, such results limit universal verification **when** the systems and properties satisfy the theorem’s assumptions; Rice’s theorem alone does not show that every parent-to-successor preservation question is undecidable. A 2026 preprint likewise reports undecidability results for unrestricted transformations of Turing-complete systems, while distinguishing restricted transformation classes that can be certified ([“Algorithmic Unverifiability of Safety for Fixed and Recursively Self-Improving Systems”](https://borowiecki.dev/pdf/2606.28639)).

Restrictions can change what can be proved. A 2025 paper describes cases where guaranteed halting makes some checks decidable, and where an output filter enforces a computable judge if the filter itself runs; neither approach proves that the judge captures human values ([“Machines that halt resolve the undecidability of artificial intelligence alignment”](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-99060-2.pdf)). Conversely, specific models supply counterexamples to overconfident preservation claims. Everitt and colleagues describe a chess agent exploiting an interface bug to replace its utility with a constant, illustrating the risk when modification consequences are not anticipated ([“Self-Modification of Policy and Utility Function in Rational Agents”](https://www.tomeveritt.se/papers/AGI16-sm.pdf)). In a bounded-rationality model, self-modification can produce worst-case exponential deterioration in value over time; the result depends on a simplified error model and does not imply that every self-modifying agent degrades ([“Performance of Bounded-Rational Agents With the Ability to Self-Modify”](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2808/Paper_26.pdf)). These findings expose vulnerabilities under particular assumptions, not a theorem that every safeguard fails.

## Conclusion

The strongest definite conclusion is conditional: a system can be mathematically shown to preserve a formal invariant indefinitely if the initial condition, transition model, specification, verifier, and enforcement mechanism satisfy the proof’s assumptions. Such a result is meaningful but narrower than a guarantee of continued alignment with human values.

No cited result establishes that an adequate, stable formal representation of human values can be chosen and maintained through realistic recursive self-modification; no cited result proves that this is impossible for every restricted design either. The central distinction is between proving that a system preserves a specified target and proving that the target is the right one.
