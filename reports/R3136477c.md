# Demanding Evidence Before Trusting Legal AI

Neither headline claim holds in the strong form in which it is usually made. The evidence does not show that predictive policing improves police resource allocation: the only positive randomised result, a Los Angeles trial, was run with close developer involvement and reported a **7.4% reduction in crime volume per unit of patrol time** (2015) ([Mohler et al., JASA 2015](https://ojp.gov/library/publications/randomized-controlled-field-trials-predictive-policing)), while the independent Shreveport trial found no significant difference in property crime ([NIJ summary](https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/evaluation-shreveport-predictive-policing-experiment)). Audits of the Geolitica (formerly PredPol) system are worse: in Plainfield, New Jersey, predictions succeeded **less than 1% of the time** ([The Markup, 2023](https://themarkup.org/prediction-bias/2023/10/02/predictive-policing-software-terrible-at-predicting-crimes)). Bias is documented in the geography of predictions and in simulations, but a Los Angeles trial found algorithm-directed arrests no more discriminatory than existing practice, so the net effect of these tools is unsettled. In contract analysis, efficiency gains are consistently reported in direction but not yet in independently measured size. The best independent benchmark (Vals, 2025) shows AI ahead on extraction and question-answering but behind lawyers on redlining, and the headline 2018 LawGeex result is vendor-run. Responsibility for contract errors falls, in US and UK practice, on the signing lawyer, while the binding EU rules for police risk tools take effect only from **2 December 2027** ([aiacto.eu](https://www.aiacto.eu/en/blog/digital-omnibus-ai-act-report-2027)).

## Predictive policing has not shown better patrol allocation

Three terms need fixing before the evidence can be read. Place-based predictive policing means software that forecasts where crime will occur, such as the ETAS model tested in Los Angeles and the PredPol system later sold as Geolitica ([Mohler et al., JASA 2015](https://ojp.gov/library/publications/randomized-controlled-field-trials-predictive-policing)). Resource allocation is measured as crime reduced per unit of patrol time in randomised trials, and forecast quality as the share of predicted locations matched by a recorded crime ([The Markup, method note](https://themarkup.org/show-your-work/2023/10/02/how-we-assessed-the-accuracy-of-predictive-policing-software)). Recidivism risk tools such as COMPAS are judged by error rates, specifically how often people who did not reoffend were wrongly flagged as high risk ([arXiv 2007.02890](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.02890)).

The best-known evidence is a pair of US randomised trials that disagree. The Los Angeles trial, run from 2011 to 2013, reported that ETAS-guided patrols produced an average **7.4% reduction in crime volume per unit of patrol time**, while analyst-guided patrols showed no significant effect ([Mohler et al., JASA 2015](https://ojp.gov/library/publications/randomized-controlled-field-trials-predictive-policing)). The independent Shreveport trial found **no significant difference in property crime** between predictive and hot-spot strategies, and the RAND team concluded the intervention had no effect ([NIJ summary](https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/evaluation-shreveport-predictive-policing-experiment); [RAND RR-531, Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Evaluation_of_the_Shreveport_Predictive.html?id=FH4TBAAAQBAJ)). The report follows the independent null result as the better guide to resource gains, because the Los Angeles trial's authors had developer ties (an aggregator states one was an LAPD officer and another a PredPol co-founder, which I could not verify) and the Shreveport evaluation was designed to test the claim directly. The evidence does not decide whether a narrow version holds: the Shreveport report says its tests had low statistical power and calls for larger evaluations ([NIJ final report](https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/evaluation-shreveport-predictive-policing-experiment-final-report)).

The claim that predictive policing improves allocation also meets field audit data. The Markup found Geolitica's predictions in Plainfield succeeded **under 1%** of the time, with officers visiting **129 of 23,760** predicted locations ([The Markup, 2023](https://themarkup.org/prediction-bias/2023/10/02/predictive-policing-software-terrible-at-predicting-crimes)). Across 11 jurisdictions, The Markup found arrest rates in predicted areas were unchanged whether or not PredPol had predicted a crime there ([The Markup, 2021](https://themarkup.org/newsletter/hello-world/the-disparate-impact-of-predictive-policing-software)). The other side rests on a 2026 simulation of Baltimore that ranked PredPol the most accurate algorithm in **87.5%** of scenarios ([arXiv 2602.02566](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.02566)). The report follows the field audits, because a simulation cannot show whether officers' time was better spent in deployed cities. The Plainfield figures also carry a caveat: a separate Markup analysis puts burglary accuracy at 10%, and the two Markup pages give slightly different prediction counts (23,760 and 23,631).

The strongest honest version of the allocation claim is narrow. A Pittsburgh randomised trial of targeted hot-spot patrols found small but significant crime reductions, and only foot patrols significantly reduced violent crime ([arXiv 2011.06019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.06019)). That trial also warns that adding hot spots without considering geographic dispersion means the same areas are repeatedly patrolled. Costs are thin in the evidence. Oakland's mayor sought over **$150,000** to buy PredPol ([Mic](https://www.mic.com/articles/156286/crime-prediction-tool-pred-pol-only-amplifies-racially-biased-policing-study-shows)), and a Shreveport summary says the predictive programme "likely did not cost any more" than hot-spot policing, a speculative claim without a cost audit ([Smart Policing](https://www.smart-policing.com/resources/evaluation-shreveport-predictive-policing-experiment)). No source establishes a cost per crime prevented.

## Bias is documented, but the counterfactual is contested

Predictive tools concentrate attention in ways that track neighbourhood composition. The Markup and Gizmodo analysed more than **5.9 million PredPol predictions** and found that many White neighbourhoods had zero predictions while nearby communities of colour received thousands ([The Markup, 2021](https://themarkup.org/newsletter/hello-world/the-disparate-impact-of-predictive-policing-software)). In a 2016 simulation using Oakland's drug records, a PredPol-style model would have sent police to Black residents at about **twice the rate** of white residents, although survey estimates put drug use at similar levels ([Lum & Isaac, Significance](https://rss.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1740-9713.2016.00960.x); [Lum & Isaac, AAAS abstract](https://aaas.confex.com/aaas/2017/webprogram/Paper18706.html)). The mechanism is that recorded crime data over-represent some groups, so predictions built on it send further policing back to the same places. The Markup also found that only **2 of 38** departments supplied patrol data for the predicted areas, which limits any test of who was actually policed ([The Markup, 2023](https://themarkup.org/prediction-bias/2023/10/02/predictive-policing-software-terrible-at-predicting-crimes)).

Against this, the Los Angeles trial found arrests from algorithm-directed patrols "neither more nor less discriminatory" than those from existing patrol practice ([Benbouzid, Big Data & Society](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2053951719861703)). The Oakland and Baltimore simulations, by contrast, report bias concentrating on Black residents, and the Baltimore model found predictive policing faster to amplify bias while "fairer and more accurate on average" ([arXiv 2602.02566](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.02566)). The report does not resolve this. The Los Angeles comparison is the right counterfactual, but it shows only that the algorithm did not add bias beyond an already unequal baseline. The simulations and the Markup's prediction geography show how feedback can entrench that baseline. The evidence does not decide whether these tools add bias on net.

Recidivism tools raise a parallel dispute. ProPublica's analysis of Broward County data found that COMPAS falsely flagged Black defendants as future criminals at **almost twice the rate** of white defendants ([arXiv 2007.02890](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.02890)). Reported rates were a **40.4%** false-positive rate for Black defendants versus **25.4%** for white defendants ([MIT SERC, 2021](https://mit-serc.pubpub.org/pub/risk-prediction-in-cj)). The vendor's position, that disparities come from differing baseline rates of recidivism, is set out in [arXiv 1703.04957](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.04957), and a rejoinder in *Federal Probation* argued that ProPublica's statistics were faulty ([uscourts.gov, 2016](https://www.uscourts.gov/about-federal-courts/probation-and-pretrial-services/federal-probation-journal/2016/09/false-positives-false-negatives-and-false-analyses-a-rejoinder-machine-bias-theres-software-used)). The dispute is mathematical as well as empirical: where prevalence differs across groups, a tool cannot satisfy both calibration and equal error rates at once ([arXiv 2003.13808](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.13808)). A 2026 replication reproduced the pattern that false positives were higher for Black defendants and false negatives higher for White defendants ([Springer, 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43681-026-01116-0)). The report therefore treats the disparity in error rates as established and the question of fairness as a choice between metrics.

Transparency is the main legal lever. In *State v. Loomis* (Wisconsin, 2016), the court held that a sentencing judge may consider a COMPAS score if it observes stated limits and cautions, so the tool was not barred ([State v. Loomis, vLex](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/state-v-loomis-no-888404547)). Critics argued that without disclosure of how COMPAS weighs variables, defendants cannot challenge its fairness ([Princeton Legal Journal](https://legaljournal.princeton.edu/predicting-future-criminals-the-due-process-implications-of-recidivism-predictions-in-state-v-loomis/)). In policing, the US courts have not held forecasts unlawful as such. The Fourth Circuit en banc ruled 9–6 in *United States v. Curry* that exigent circumstances did not justify a suspicionless search of a man near a shooting, although the judges split sharply over predictive policing itself, with Judge Thacker calling it "little more than racial profiling writ large" ([ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/4th-circuit-spars-over-policing-dissenter-said-to-write-with-a-smooth-pen-and-a-tin-ear)). A news aggregator reports a federal ruling that an arrest cannot rest on an uncorroborated AI conclusion, but I could not verify the court, docket or appeal status, so I do not rely on it ([lavx.hu summary](https://news.lavx.hu/article/court-rules-fourth-amendment-bars-ai-from-establishing-probable-cause)).

Trust is the least measured outcome. No retrieved study compares resident trust before and after predictive deployment. The one experiment, a 2026 Dutch survey, found that information about external monitoring of police algorithms raised trust, while information about legislation alone did not ([Nieuwenhuizen, Public Administration](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/padm.70034?af=R)). A student thesis reports that **61%** of respondents in a French survey (Piotrowicz, 2019) said their trust in police would not be harmed by predictive software, but the original survey was not retrieved, so this is weak evidence ([Leiden thesis](https://studenttheses.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:3239837/view)). Procedural-justice research predicts that trust erodes when residents face frequent or unfair stops, which is the pathway the bias findings imply, but no retrieved study tests it for predictive tools.

Vendors and critics also disagree on the source of bias. PredPol argued that its choice of input data makes predictions unbiased ([Governing](https://www.governing.com/security/crime-prediction-software-perpetuates-biases)). Critics point to the feedback loop: a 2018 paper by PredPol's own creators acknowledged that place-based algorithms focus on areas already heavily policed ([Criminal Legal News](https://www.criminallegalnews.org/news/2022/nov/15/predictive-policing-doesnt-reduce-crime-does-increase-targeting-vulnerable-communities/)). A Chicago study attributes socioeconomic enforcement gaps to resource constraints and discretionary prioritisation of wealthier areas ([arXiv 2405.07715](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.07715)). Elgin's deputy chief called the tool "bias-by-proxy" ([Criminal Legal News](https://www.criminallegalnews.org/news/2022/nov/15/predictive-policing-doesnt-reduce-crime-does-increase-targeting-vulnerable-communities/)). The sources do not resolve whether bias enters through data, model or practice, and the Chicago evidence suggests practice matters most.

## Contract AI accuracy depends on the task

The most independent accuracy evidence is the Vals Legal AI Report (February 2025), which tested four legal AI tools against a lawyer control group on seven tasks ([Vals](https://www.vals.ai/industry-reports/vlair-2-27-25)). On redlining, the closest task to flagging risky clauses, the lawyer baseline scored **79.7%**, the best tool (Harvey Assistant) **65%**, and Vincent AI **53.6%** ([Vals, Harvey evaluation](https://www.vals.ai/application-reports/harvey)). On data extraction, two tools scored **75.1%** and **73.2%** against a lawyer baseline of **71.1%** ([MRI Software, citing Vals](https://www.mrisoftware.com/blog/benefits-contract-review-automation-legal-efficiency/)). Vals' own report says current tools should only be used for certain redlining tasks, and the vendors chose which tasks they entered ([ContractSafe, citing Vals](https://www.contractsafe.com/blog/ai-contract-review-software)). A critique argues the percentage scoring distorts results, for example by scoring a page-long AI answer against a one-paragraph lawyer answer ([Tokuma Labs](https://tokumalabs.com/insights/reviewing-the-review/)).

Vendor-run comparisons report AI ahead. The 2018 LawGeex test reported **94%** accuracy for the AI against **85%** for 20 lawyers, on five NDAs, with the AI finishing in **26 seconds** against about **92 minutes** ([Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/israeli-ai-software-whips-expert-lawyers-in-contract-analysis/)). A critique reports that the answer key was built from the best answers of all 21 participants, including the AI itself, and that the study was not peer-reviewed or replicated ([Substack critique](https://nemock.substack.com/p/the-study-that-proved-ai-beats-lawyers)). The vendor's own report said the technology was not meant to be used standalone ([beforeyou-sign](https://beforeyou-sign.com/blog/ai-contract-review-vs-lawyer)). A 2024 Onit preprint reported that advanced models match or exceed human accuracy on identifying legal issues in procurement contracts, but Law360's summary notes that legal process outsourcers led in locating more issues ([arXiv 2401.16212](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.16212); [Law360](https://www.law360.com/pulse/articles/1783910/ai-quicker-at-contract-review-than-attys-but-less-accurate)). The report follows the Vals result as the most credible independent comparison, while noting that it is small and about a year and a half old.

Reliability warnings come from outside contract review. A preregistered Stanford study found that legal research tools advertised as "hallucination-free" hallucinated **17–33%** of the time ([Stanford RegLab](https://reglab.stanford.edu/publications/hallucination-free-assessing-the-reliability-of-leading-ai-legal-research-tools/)). Drafting results also vary: a Legal Benchmarks study reported human lawyers at **56.7%** and the best general model at **73.3%** on output reliability, and that only **6%** of surveyed lawyers require outputs to be 100% accurate before using AI ([Legal Benchmarks](https://www.legalbenchmarks.ai/research/phase-2-research)). These are drafting tasks, not clause-finding, and the study notes that most AI drafting still needs expert review.

Privacy duties apply before any accuracy question. ABA Formal Opinion 512 (29 July 2024) requires lawyers to assess the risk that information entered into a generative AI tool could be disclosed or accessed by others, and it applies even to staff who should not see the information, such as lawyers behind an ethical wall ([GC AI](https://gc.ai/blog/aba-formal-opinion-512); [The Legal Prompts](https://thelegalprompts.com/blog/aba-formal-opinion-512-generative-ai)). Lawyers must check whether a vendor retains data or claims rights to it ([Read Beyond the Bar](https://readbeyondthebar.com/guides/aba-formal-opinion-512-explained)). In the EU, GDPR applies to personal data processed by AI systems, and sending personal data to an external provider requires a processor agreement under Article 28 ([Usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/gdpr-ai/); [Docuflair](https://www.docuflair.com/en/pages/resources/blog/gdpr-ai-personal-data.html)). A US court in *United States v. Heppner* (S.D.N.Y., February 2026) held that documents created in a consumer AI tool were not privileged ([Chapman and Cutler](https://www.chapman.com/publication-federal-court-rules-that-ai-generated-documents-are-not-protected-by-privilege)). That case concerned a client's self-initiated use, not lawyer-run review, but it shows that the tool's terms can defeat confidentiality. The US has no licensing regime for legal AI products, which leaves the assessment duty with the firm ([Spellbook](https://spellbook.com/learn/legally-compliant-ai-use)).

## Responsibility for contract errors sits with the signer

Courts have sanctioned lawyers for AI-fabricated citations in filings, and the consistent pattern is that the signing lawyer answers for verification. In *Mata v. Avianca* the court sanctioned counsel but said reliance on AI is not inherently improper, the failure was in verification ([RuleX](https://rulex.org/united-states/guides/lawyers-sanctioned-ai-fake-citations)). Later cases follow. The Alabama Supreme Court sanctioned an attorney in April 2026 for inaccurate AI-generated citations ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-lawyers-keep-citing-fake-cases-invented-by-ai/)); an Arizona federal judge sanctioned attorney Sheree Wright after finding at least **18** fabricated citations ([AttorneyReview](https://attorneyreview.com/blog/ai-courtroom-lawyers-sanctioned-fake-citations)); and the Nebraska Supreme Court temporarily suspended an attorney after **57 of 63** citations were found defective ([AI for Lawyers Academy](https://www.aiforlawyersacademy.com/ai-hallucination-cases/)). Trackers disagree on volume: one counts US cases rising from **239 to 434** between February and June 2026 ([Motion Ready](https://motion-ready.com/ai-citation-sanctions)), while another cites more than **1,200** cases globally by early 2026 ([AttorneyReview](https://attorneyreview.com/blog/ai-courtroom-lawyers-sanctioned-fake-citations)). The sanctions concern court filings. I found no sanction for an error in an AI-reviewed or AI-drafted contract, so the evidence on contract errors is absent rather than negative.

Professional duties reach the firm as well. ABA Opinion 512 applies existing Model Rules, and managerial lawyers must set firm policies and supervise under Rules 5.1 and 5.3 ([regulations.ai](https://regulations.ai/regulations/RAI-US-NA-ABAOP512-2024)). It requires lawyers to understand the tool's capabilities and limits, and reliance on uncritical output can itself be a competence failure ([DIZON.LAW](https://www.dizon.law/insights/aba-formal-opinion-512-what-it-requires)). The UK position is similar but tighter. In *Ayinde v London Borough of Haringey* (2025), the Divisional Court held that fabricated citations must not reach the court, and it made wasted-costs orders of **£2,000** each and referred the lawyers to regulators ([Noxia](https://www.noxia.co.uk/field-notes/eighteen-of-forty-five-citations-did-not-exist); [Hausfeld](https://www.hausfeld.com/what-we-think/perspectives-blogs/fake-it-til-you-get-referred-to-the-sra)). Sources give different citations for this decision, with one dating it 30 April 2025 and another 3 April 2025 ([Technology Law](https://www.technologylaw.ai/p/ayinde-v-london-borough-of-haringey-fake-cases); [Record of Law](https://recordoflaw.in/when-chatgpt-goes-to-court-r-ayinde-v-london-borough-of-haringey-al-haroun-v-qatar-national-bank-qpsc/)). The Law Society says a professional may be negligent for failing to validate outputs, while noting there is no SRA guidance on when solicitors should use AI ([Law Society](https://www.lawsociety.org.uk/topics/ai-and-lawtech/using-artificial-intelligence-in-legal-practice)). A 17 August 2026 SRA warning notice reportedly says solicitors remain accountable for AI-generated work, but I could not retrieve its text ([OpsIntel](https://www.opsintel.io/blog/sra-warning-notice-17-august-2026-solicitors-remain-liable-for-ai-generated-advice-confidentiality-breaches-and-court-conduct/)).

Vendor contracts push risk back to customers. Standard AI vendor agreements commonly cap total liability, including indemnity, at **12 months of fees** ([Honigman](https://www.honigman.com/the-matrix/ai-insurance-gap-what-it-means-for-technology-contracts)), and a fallback "super-cap" of about **two to five times** annual fees is reported where a vendor refuses an uncapped indemnity ([Consilium](https://consilium.law/sparkpoint/ai-vendor-indemnification-ip/)). Vendor indemnities are typically limited to third-party IP claims and may exclude the customer's failure to review outputs ([tish.law](https://tish.law/blog/ai-vendor-contract-clauses-training-data-confidentiality-output-ownership-human-review-and-indemnity/)). One legal-AI vendor makes the customer liable for errors it relies on without independent review, and caps the vendor's liability at fees paid in the preceding 12 months ([LegalAI terms](https://legalai.com/terms)). An aggregator claims some contract-review vendors share risk through accuracy guarantees, but it cites no contract and conflicts with these terms ([LegalPDF](https://legalpdf.io/knowledge/ai_legal_liability_and_malpractice_in_2026_who_pays_when_generative_ai_gets_it_wrong.php)). The report follows the standard-term pattern, since it is the one shown in several vendor documents. The implication is that a client's recovery against a vendor for a missed clause is likely to be small, and that the lawyer's verification record is the main defence.

The EU product-liability regime may change this. Directive (EU) 2024/2853 must be transposed by **9 December 2026**, and it treats software, including AI, as a product ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-product-liability-directive-responding-to-software-ai-and-complex-supply-chains/); [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/risk-and-compliance/product-risks-today-how-the-new-product-liability-directive-turns-ai-act-complia-102mpu2)). Commentators say strict liability is less likely for software AI because it rarely causes physical harm ([Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/physical-ai-and-strict-liability-what-impact-eu-product-liability-directive)). The Commission withdrew the proposed AI Liability Directive ([Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/what-the-new-product-liability-directive-means-for-manufacturers-of-ai-based-products-and-services)). Whether the directive reaches pure economic loss, the usual loss in contract errors, was not verified against the text.

## Efficiency gains are real in direction but not yet in firm economics

Controlled studies report large speed gains. In a randomised trial, law students using GPT-4 became much faster on legal analysis, while quality improved only slightly and inconsistently ([Choi et al., University of Minnesota](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/lawyering-in-the-age-of-artificial-intelligence/)). A 2026 randomised trial of **127** upper-level students reported productivity gains of about **38% to 115%** for one tool and **34% to 140%** for another ([Schwarcz et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/2755323X261427048)). A field experiment with **133** patent lawyers found junior lawyers with AI finished a drafting task **18 minutes** faster, but when AI was removed, junior lawyers showed no average skill gain, with scores splitting into more strong and more weak results ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/does-better-work-always-mean-better-workers/)). A study of **206** law students found AI highlighting of complaints cut task time by **30%** without a measured quality drop ([arXiv 2607.01256](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.01256)). These are students or narrow tasks. A 2024 legal aid study found **90%** of staff reported higher productivity, but this is self-reported ([arXiv 2602.24130](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.24130)). I found no independent measurement of time or cost saved on employed lawyers' contract review over a sustained period. A WorldCC benchmark of about **$6,900** per basic contract is cited only secondhand and was not verified ([Judicio](https://judicio.ai/blogs/contract-review-time-benchmarks)).

Fee structures have not yet moved. A 2026 Best Law Firms survey found **58%** of firms said AI had not changed their billing or reduced billable hours ([Best Law Firms](https://www.bestlawfirms.com/articles/law-firms-embrace-afas-but-clients-want-more-flexibility/7098)), while another summary of it reports **19%** saw billable hours decline ([Sandstone](https://sandstone.com/blog/the-impact-of-ai-on-legal-billing-how-the-billable-hour-changes)). Alternative fee arrangements slipped to **70%** of firms from 72%, and most remain built on the billable hour ([Best Law Firms](https://www.bestlawfirms.com/articles/law-firm-alternative-fee-arrangements-survey/8611)). About **90%** of legal dollars still flow through hourly arrangements, per a January 2026 report ([LegalTech Magazine](https://www.legaltechmag.com/blog/legal-ai-billable-hour)). In-house clients expect savings: Axiom's 2026 survey of **528** in-house leaders found **92%** expect or negotiate AI-related rate cuts, while few obtain them ([Sandstone](https://sandstone.com/blog/the-impact-of-ai-on-legal-billing-how-the-billable-hour-changes)). The economic logic is clear: under hourly billing, time saved is revenue lost, and under fixed fees the firm keeps the gain. Lawyers billing by the hour must bill actual time even when AI shortens the work ([Legal AI Governance](https://legalaigovernance.com/resources/aba-opinion-512/)).

Job effects are contested. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects little or no change in paralegal employment from 2024 to 2034, with about **376,200** jobs in 2024 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/legal/paralegals-and-legal-assistants.htm?__hstc=182781753.07430159d50a3c91e72c280a7921bf0d.1543449600111.1543449600112.1543449600113.1&__hssc=182781753.1.1543449600114&__hsfp=998628806)). Sources differ on whether BLS names AI as the cause, and I could not verify that wording in the primary text. Lawyer growth figures use different windows (5% for 2025–2035 in one source, 4% for 2024–2034 in another), so they should not be combined ([LawJobs](https://lawjobs.com/career-resources/find-the-job-1/legal-tech-jobs-573); [AIIFI](https://www.aiifi.ai/ai-at-work/will-ai-replace-lawyers)). The junior-lawyer evidence is the sharpest disagreement. A firm-level study finds junior employment at AI-adopting firms declined relative to senior employment, driven mainly by slower hiring, while a contrasting analysis of postings finds junior and senior postings fell in parallel and a Danish study finds AI is not the main driver ([arXiv 2605.23159](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.23159)). Most surveyed employers report no hiring change: a 2026 Relativity/IDC survey of **700** professionals found **57.9%** saw no impact on hiring, **24.2%** increased hiring and **9.2%** decreased it ([Legal Practice Intelligence](https://www.legalpracticeintelligence.com/blogs/technology-intelligence/relativity-study-ai-legal-hiring)). Australian firms have cut graduate intakes but deny AI is the reason ([Law Society Journal](https://lsj.com.au/articles/ai-to-slash-graduate-law-jobs-over-next-decade/)), so self-explanations need checking. Task-exposure estimates suggest a fifth to nearly half of legal tasks could be automated ([AllWork](https://allwork.space/2026/04/ai-is-rewriting-legal-careers-and-changing-where-lawyers-work/)), but these are not measured displacement.

Access is uneven. Almost half of corporate legal departments had some enterprise generative AI tool, and **61%** of departments in a 2026 Deloitte survey were in deployment phases ([Modern Counsel](https://modern-counsel.com/2026/record-law-school-demand-tests-the-us-legal-talent-pipeline/)). Large firms and in-house teams are the documented adopters, and the Vals human baseline was drawn from large firms ([MSBA](https://www.msba.org/site/site/content/News-and-Publications/News/General-News/AI_vs._Attorneys_Insights_from_the_Vals_Legal_AI_Report.aspx)). Work moving to alternative providers is forecast, not observed: more than half of Wolters Kluwer's respondents expect contract drafting to be reallocated ([Wolters Kluwer](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en-gb/expert-insights/alsp-automation-billable-hours)). I found no source measuring contract work lost by solo practitioners or small firms, nor the outcomes for small businesses using self-serve tools.

## Binding rules diverge, and few practices have been tested

The EU AI Act is the only binding framework among those reviewed for police tools. Annex III lists law enforcement and the administration of justice as high-risk ([Bundesnetzagentur](https://www.bundesnetzagentur.de/EN/Areas/Digitalisation/AI/09_HighRisk/start.html)), and Annex III systems are presumed high-risk under Article 6(2) ([RegulatoryAI](https://www.regulatoryai.eu/annex-iii/)). The point covering risk of offending or re-offending is limited where the assessment is not based solely on profiling ([Annex III text](https://ai-eu-act.eu/annexes/annexes-annex-iii/)). The Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) moved Annex III obligations from 2 August 2026 to **2 December 2027** ([aiacto.eu](https://www.aiacto.eu/en/blog/digital-omnibus-ai-act-report-2027)). Article 14 requires that high-risk systems be designed for human oversight: overseers must understand the system's limits, stay alert to automation bias, override outputs and halt the system ([ai-act.io, Article 14](https://en.ai-act.io/article/high-risk-ai-systems/requirements-for-high-risk-ai-systems/human-oversight)). Article 12 requires automatic lifetime event logging, but the detailed log fields apply only to remote biometric identification ([euaiact.com, Article 12](https://www.euaiact.com/article/12)). Article 86 gives affected persons a right to a clear explanation of an AI system's role in a decision with significant effects, and the duty sits with the deployer ([euaiact.com, Article 86](https://www.euaiact.com/article/86); [agentliability.eu](https://agentliability.eu/articles/eu-ai-act-article-86-right-to-explanation-deployer-guide)). Sources disagree on Article 86's start date, and none clearly resolves whether the Omnibus moved it. I did not retrieve the Article 5 text, so the status of crime-risk profiling under prohibited practices is unassessed.

The three non-binding frameworks set vocabulary. The IEEE Ethically Aligned Design (2019) calls for transparency so that the basis of any decision can be discovered, and for identifying decisions that should never be delegated to such systems ([IEEE EAD overview](https://sagroups.ieee.org/global-initiative/wp-content/uploads/sites/542/2023/01/ead1e-overview.pdf)). The OECD AI Principles (2019) call on actors to be accountable for proper functioning, and the 2024 update kept them while noting transparency "could benefit from some clarifications" ([OECD Recommendation](https://wecglobal.org/uploads/2019/07/2019_OECD_Recommendations-AI.pdf); [OECD C/MIN(2024)17](https://one.oecd.org/document/C/MIN(2024)17/en/pdf)). The NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 (January 2023, NIST AI 100-1) is voluntary, and its MANAGE 2.4 asks for mechanisms to disengage a system behaving outside intended use ([Safeguard.sh](https://safeguard.sh/resources/blog/nist-ai-rmf-four-functions-explained)). For legal practice, ABA Opinion 512 is persuasive rather than enforceable, and Florida's Bar Opinion 24-1 goes further by recommending informed consent before third-party AI use that discloses confidential information ([JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/aba-weighs-in-on-generative-ai-use-in-3097638)).

The practices with the most testing are logging, human oversight, and disclosure of monitoring. Testing is thinnest for fairness audits: no bias audit, impact assessment or algorithm-register result for a police or contract system was retrieved.

## Conclusion

The two cases share an evidentiary pattern. The most-cited benefit figures come from developers and vendors, while independent evaluations are fewer and more cautious. Predictive policing has a demonstrated harm, not a demonstrated benefit: feedback loops and biased geography are documented, and the Shreveport trial and Plainfield audit undercut the claim of better allocation. Contract AI has a demonstrated benefit in direction, speed on bounded tasks, and a demonstrated limit in judgement-heavy work such as redlining. Both cases show that a tool's stated accuracy is not a substitute for testing against the counterfactual that matters, whether that is an analyst's hot-spot map or an experienced lawyer's redline.

For responsible use, the lessons converge. Accountability has to be assigned before deployment, and the sources point to the deployer and the signing lawyer, not the vendor. Transparency means more than publishing a model: the Dutch experiment and Loomis both suggest that people want to know who monitors the system and how its output can be contested. Fairness requires measuring error rates and feedback effects, not only training data, and the COMPAS dispute shows that the choice of metric is itself a value judgement. Policymakers should require independent, adequately powered evaluations before scaling either tool, and should treat vendor benchmarks as hypotheses. The EU's December 2027 date gives police risk tools a window to build the oversight, logging and explanation duties that US practice has so far left to litigation, while lawyers using contract AI should document their verification now, since that record is what courts and regulators will examine.
