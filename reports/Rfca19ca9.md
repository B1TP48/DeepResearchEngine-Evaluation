# Responsible Legal AI Requires Enforceable Process

Responsible AI in law fails in opposite directions in the two cases. Predictive policing’s core risk is coercive state error: algorithms trained on police records predict where patrols have been, not necessarily where crime is, and they distribute surveillance and arrest exposure unevenly across Black, Latino and low-income communities. Contract AI’s core risk is private speed outrunning verification: it can cut first-pass review time substantially, but commercial tools have documented hallucination rates of **17–33%**, and liability for a missed clause stays with the lawyer rather than the vendor. The regulatory split is equally sharp: the EU AI Act bans individual criminal-offence risk prediction based solely on profiling, treats residual law-enforcement risk scoring as high-risk, and generally leaves private contract review outside its Annex III high-risk list because point 8(a) is framed around judicial authorities and ADR. High-risk obligations have now been deferred to **2 December 2027**, so for most of the current period the binding constraints on legal AI are professional conduct, data protection and procurement rather than AI Act conformity assessment. The comparative lesson is that fairness, accountability and transparency are not single standards: in policing they must constrain state coercion; in contract work they must be written into vendor contracts and verification protocols.

## The two cases sit at opposite ends of the regulatory risk map

The major ethical frameworks converge on the same labels — human oversight, transparency, non-discrimination and accountability — but diverge on whether they create remedies. The OECD AI Principles, adopted in **May 2019** and updated in **May 2024**, set out five values-based principles and five policy recommendations, with **47 adherents**; the OECD Recommendation describes itself as “the first intergovernmental standard on AI,” and the EU, Council of Europe, United States and United Nations use its definition of an AI system ([OECD AI Principles overview](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles?trk=public_post-text)). UNESCO’s Recommendation was adopted in **November 2021** by **193 Member States** as “the first global standard on AI ethics,” combining values and principles with policy recommendations grounded in human rights, transparency, fairness and human oversight ([UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics)). IEEE’s *Ethically Aligned Design* frames human oversight as a design constraint — “A/IS should always be subordinate to human judgment and control” — and IEEE 7000-2021 supplies a systems-engineering methodology for ethical concerns, but neither creates an individual right of explanation ([IEEE EAD overview](https://r2.ieee.org); [IEEE SA](https://standards-dev21.ieee.org)).

The Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law is the only binding international AI treaty, adopted by the Committee of Ministers on **17 May 2024** and opened for signature on **5 September 2024**; its principles include transparency and oversight, accountability, equality and non-discrimination, and privacy. It had **21 signatures** but had not entered into force as of **October 2026**, and the EU ratified it in **May 2026** ([Council of Europe](https://www.coe.int); [ActuIA](https://www.actuia.com)). The EU AI Act is therefore the operative binding regime. Article 5(1)(d) prohibits risk assessments predicting a person’s criminal offence based solely on profiling or personality traits, while Annex III point 6 makes residual law-enforcement risk scoring, evidence-reliability evaluation and investigative profiling high-risk; point 8(a) covers AI assisting judicial authorities or ADR in researching and applying the law ([AI Act Service Desk, Article 5](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-5); [Annex III](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/)). Article 6(3) allows an Annex III system to escape high-risk status where it poses no significant risk of harm, and the Commission’s service desk stresses that “not all AI systems used in a particular area (such as law enforcement) are automatically considered high-risk” ([Article 6](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/6/); [AI Act Service Desk, High-Risk AI Systems](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/taxonomy/term/71)).

| Instrument | Legal status | What it adds to legal AI governance |
|---|---|---|
| OECD AI Principles | Non-binding, **47 adherents**, adopted **May 2019**, updated **May 2024** ([OECD](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles?trk=public_post-text)) | Common definition and principle vocabulary; no remedy |
| UNESCO Recommendation | Non-binding, **193 states**, **November 2021** ([UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics)) | Human oversight, fairness, transparency, literacy |
| IEEE EAD / IEEE 7000-2021 | Voluntary design standards ([IEEE EAD](https://r2.ieee.org)) | Human-control design constraint; no individual contestation right |
| Council of Europe Convention | Binding treaty but not in force as of **October 2026** ([Council of Europe](https://www.coe.int)) | Rights-based transparency, oversight and accountability |
| EU AI Act | Binding; Art. 5 bans from **2 February 2025**; high-risk from **2 December 2027** ([AI Act Service Desk](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-5); [Danish DPA](https://www.ds.dk/en/news/2026/digital-omnibus-for-ai-approved)) | Prohibitions, conformity assessment, Art. 26 deployer oversight, Art. 86 explanation |

The same model can therefore be limited-risk in a law firm and high-risk when embedded in court or ADR workflows: classification follows deployment context, not technology. Deployers of high-risk systems must assign human oversight to named persons with competence, training and authority, keep logs for at least **six months**, and inform individuals subject to AI-assisted decisions ([AI Act Service Desk, Article 26](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-26)). Article 86 gives affected persons a right to “clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system” in Annex III decisions with legal effects, but only to the extent the right is not otherwise provided under Union law and subject to national-law exceptions ([Article 86](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/86/)). Outside the EU, the map fragments: the US revoked the Biden-era AI order through EO 14179 and later created an “AI Litigation Task Force” to challenge state AI laws, while California requires validation and public disclosure for pretrial risk-assessment tools under Penal Code § 1320.35; the UK has no AI Act and relies on sectoral regulators, which a parliamentary committee called “patchy and confused”; China’s generative-AI measures impose filing and security-assessment duties ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov); [White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov); [California Legislative Information](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov); [Licentium](https://www.licentium.io); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com)).

## Predictive policing’s fairness failures are structural and its economic case is marginal

The strongest empirical finding in predictive policing is not that the algorithms are accurate but that they encode prior policing. ProPublica’s analysis of more than **7,000** Broward County arrests found that only **20%** of people predicted to commit violent crimes did so and **61%** of those deemed likely to re-offend were arrested for any crime within two years; Black defendants were **77%** more likely to be flagged high risk for violent crime and **45%** more likely for any crime after controlling for criminal history, recidivism, age and gender ([ProPublica, “Machine Bias”](https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing)). COMPAS used **137 questions**, did not include race as an input, and Northpointe did not disclose its calculations; Northpointe replied that ProPublica ignored different base rates and that high-risk defendants had roughly similar recidivism across races — predictive parity rather than error-rate balance ([ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing); [Columbia Human Rights Law Review](https://hrlr.law.columbia.edu/)). Both statistics can be true because calibration and equal false-positive rates are mathematically incompatible when group base rates differ ([Legal Bond Index preprint](https://zenodo.org/)). The choice of fairness metric is therefore a constitutional and policy decision, not a technical fix.

The accuracy evidence undercuts the commercial case for opacity. Dressel and Farid found COMPAS was no more accurate than laypeople’s predictions and that a two-feature linear classifier matched COMPAS’s **137-feature** accuracy, with reported accuracies of about **65%** for COMPAS and **62%** for laypeople ([Science Advances record](https://scholar.google.hr/); [Science](https://www.science.org/)). A **2026** peer-reviewed replication reproduced higher false positives for Black defendants and higher false negatives for white defendants, and found that hyper-parameter optimisation and correlation removal only “modestly improve accuracy,” concluding that “embedded bias in the data can persist” ([AI and Ethics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43681-026-01116-0)). Person-based lists show the largest disparities: Chicago’s Strategic Subject List assigned scores to nearly **400,000** people from **2012–2019**, yet the Inspector General found only **16.3%** of listed individuals were confirmed gang members against commanders’ belief of about **95%** ([npj Urban Sustainability](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42949-026-00394-1)). London’s Gangs Matrix listed about **3,800** people, **78%** of them Black while Black Londoners were **13%** of the population; **64%** were lowest risk yet remained under surveillance, and Amnesty reported **15%** were children, some as young as **12** ([npj Urban Sustainability](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42949-026-00394-1); [UK Parliament written evidence](https://committees.parliament.uk/)).

Feedback loops are the central mechanism. Lum and Isaac argue that because systems are trained on police records they predict where arrests will occur, “predicting future policing, not future crime,” and Ensign et al. model “runaway feedback loops, where police are repeatedly sent back to the same neighborhoods regardless of the true crime rate” ([Significance](https://rss.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/); [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/)). Brayne’s fieldwork found that people in over-policed neighbourhoods are “more likely to be stopped, thus increasing their point value, justifying their increased surveillance, and making it more likely that they will be stopped again” ([Strauss Center](https://www.strausscenter.org/)). Rotterdam’s Sensing Project and New Orleans’ covert Palantir partnership illustrate the same dynamic: flagged vehicles faced enhanced monitoring that generated the data supporting the original risk assessment, and defence attorneys reported never receiving Palantir analytical products in discovery ([npj Urban Sustainability](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42949-026-00394-1)). The mechanism is best understood as a measurement-validity problem before it is a fairness problem, although Kinsman and Wong show the loop applies to proactive policing generally, not only to algorithms ([AMS](https://www.ams.org/)).

|---|---|---|

| Los Angeles, Mohler et al. | Randomised ETAS patrol boxes vs analyst boxes | **7.4%** average crime reduction as a function of patrol time ([CEBCP](https://cebcp.org/evidence-based-policing/the-matrix/micro-places/micro-places-mohler-et-al-2015/)) |
| Philadelphia | Three-arm, **90-day** experiment | **31%** property-crime decline in marked-car treatment; diffusion summaries disagree at **40%** vs **42%** ([Campbell](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com); [OJP](https://www.ojp.gov)) |
| Uruguay | Two RCTs, imported software vs local analysts | No significant crime difference; trained task force produced **20% more patrol time** and **30%** robbery reduction only in high-crime shifts ([Wisconsin](https://search.library.wisc.edu/article/cdi_proquest_journals_2877034408)) |

The economic case is narrow. Licence fees are small relative to police payrolls — US municipal PredPol contracts ran from about **$9,000** to **$80,000** a year, Kent paid **£100,000** a year, and Plainfield paid **$20,500** initially and **$15,500** to extend — but the only trial-based cost evidence suggests cost-neutrality at best ([RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR531.html); [KentOnline](https://www.kentonline.co.uk/kent/news/police-quietly-dropped-predictive-policing-project-months-ago-194189/); [The Markup](https://themarkup.org/prediction-bias/2023/10/02/predictive-policing-software-terrible-at-predicting-crimes); [City of Merced](https://cityofmerced.legistar.com)). Shreveport’s in-house analytics were “likely” no more costly than the status quo, while Uruguay’s authors concluded that “local crime analysts are more cost-effective” than imported software ([RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR531.html); [Wisconsin](https://search.library.wisc.edu/article/cdi_proquest_journals_2877034408)). No published, peer-reviewed cost-benefit analysis monetising crime reductions net of training, analyst time and maintenance was located.

The social costs are concentrated on people who cannot easily contest inclusion. Pasco County’s “prolific offender” list led deputies to contact one **15-year-old** and his family **21 times in four months**, and the December **2024** settlement required the Sheriff’s Office to end the programme permanently and pay **$105,000** to four plaintiffs after acknowledging interference with First, Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights ([Reason](https://reason.com/2024/12/04/pasco-county-sheriff-will-end-predictive-policing-program-to-settle-lawsuit-over-harassment/)). The trials generally found diffusion rather than displacement of benefits: Philadelphia found less crime in the **8 hours** after treatment shifts, Uruguay found diffusion to adjacent areas, and hot-spots reviews report “minimal displacement effects and some diffusion of benefits” ([Campbell](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com); [Wisconsin](https://search.library.wisc.edu/article/cdi_proquest_journals_2877034408)). No study directly measures whether algorithmic patrol reduces crime reporting, and the German Constitutional Court’s **16 February 2023** judgment voided Hesse’s §25a(1) and Hamburg’s §49(1) because they lacked a sufficient “concretised danger” threshold, with Hesse’s Palantir-supported hessenDATA used “a thousandfold” annually ([Bundesverfassungsgericht](https://bundesverfassungsgericht.de/SharedDocs/Pressemitteilungen/DE/2023/bvg23-018.html?nn=68080)).

Transparency has been operationalised as notice plus caution, not as a contestable account of the inference. In *State v. Loomis*, the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that COMPAS at sentencing did not violate due process even though Northpointe refused to disclose its methodology; the score contributed to a **six-year** sentence and denial of parole eligibility, and the court imposed a five-part warning that the score had limited utility ([Harvard JOLT](https://jolt.law.harvard.edu/digest/algorithmic-due-process-mistaken-accountability-and-attribution-in-state-v-loomis-1)). The defendant knew the inputs but “had no idea how it was using said information”; the litigation fixated on source-code disclosure, which JOLT calls “a fundamental misunderstanding” ([Harvard JOLT](https://jolt.law.harvard.edu/digest/algorithmic-due-process-mistaken-accountability-and-attribution-in-state-v-loomis-1)). A parallel trade-secret case in education, *Houston Federation of Teachers v. Houston ISD* (2017), ruled for teachers challenging a proprietary algorithmic evaluation system, but no source shows a US or UK court ordering disclosure of a predictive-policing model’s source code ([Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/)).

Public trust is conditional and only partly measurable. Pew Research found **58%** of Americans believe computer programs will always reflect their designers’ biases while **40%** think bias-free design is possible, and about **68%** found a personal-finance score algorithm unacceptable ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/); [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/)). A UNICRI/INTERPOL survey of **670** respondents across six continents found “cautious optimism” alongside “significant ethical concerns,” with transparency, human oversight and legal frameworks “deemed essential” ([UNICRI](https://unicri.org/)). A meta-analysis of **56** studies over **28 years** found procedural justice and legitimacy predict willingness to cooperate with police, with correlations around **0.20** — modest, not a collapse thesis ([Journal of Criminal Justice](https://www.sciencedirect.com/)). A Dutch survey experiment with **877** citizens found that disclosing external oversight significantly increased trust while disclosing legislative detail did not ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com)). The ICO linked data-protection failures directly to community relations: “Ignoring people’s fundamental data rights erodes trust and confidence, which risks alienating the communities the Met serves” ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-the-public/ico-40/metropolitan-police-gangs-matrix/)).

## Contract AI’s speed gains outrun accuracy, privacy and accountability

Confidentiality is the most heavily regulated contract-AI risk. ABA Formal Opinion 512 of **29 July 2024** applies Model Rules 1.1, 1.6, 1.4, 5.1 and 5.3 to generative AI, identifies contract analytics for M&A due diligence and drafting as core use cases, and holds that self-learning tools “by their very nature” raise the risk that one client’s information may be disclosed even inside a single firm’s instance; a client’s informed consent is required before inputting representation information, and “merely adding general, boiler-plate” consent language is inadequate ([ABA](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/health_law/news/2024/08/aba-ethics-opinion-use-of-ai/); [ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf)). The SRA’s “Misuse of AI” warning notice of **17 August 2026** states that paid and free tools “may not provide the contractual, and technical safeguards needed to maintain client confidentiality,” requires no training on client data except where explicitly authorised, and requires no retention beyond necessity ([SRA](https://jobs.sra.org.uk/solicitors/guidance/misuse-ai/)). The Upper Tribunal in *UK v Secretary of State for the Home Department* [2026] UKUT 81 (IAC) at [21] observed that putting client letters into an open-source tool “is to place this information on the internet in the public domain,” and the SRA warns that “legal professional privilege may be permanently waived and unable to be recovered” ([SRA](https://jobs.sra.org.uk/solicitors/guidance/misuse-ai/)). GDPR Article 28 adds processor instructions and sub-processor authorisation duties, making the full sub-processor chain and inference location, not a headline “no training” promise, the real due-diligence question ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679)). The CCBE guide of **8 December 2025** raises confidentiality, independence and transparency, and flags “hallucinations, bias and sycophancy” ([CCBE](https://www.ccbe.eu/document/ccbe-guide-on-the-use-of-the-use-of-generative-ai-for-lawyers/)).

Accuracy is worse than marketing implies. The Stanford/Yale study describes itself as “the first preregistered empirical evaluation of AI-driven legal research tools” and found Lexis+ AI, Westlaw AI-Assisted Research and Ask Practical Law AI hallucinated “between 17% and 33% of the time,” despite vendor claims such as Lexis+ AI delivering “100% hallucination-free linked legal citations,” CoCounsel not making up facts, and Thomson Reuters avoiding hallucinations; Westlaw was accurate **42%** of the time while hallucinating “nearly twice as often” as the other tools ([Hallucination-Free?](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2405.20362)). Stanford HAI separately reports Lexis+ AI and Ask Practical Law AI producing incorrect information more than **17%** of the time, Westlaw more than **34%**, and general chatbots **58–82%** on legal queries ([Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/ai-trial-legal-models-hallucinate-1-out-6-or-more-benchmarking-queries)). Contract benchmarks measure a narrower skill: CUAD was built with “dozens of legal experts” and “over 13,000 annotations,” and Transformers showed only “nascent performance” with “substantial room for improvement” ([CUAD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)). CLAUSE generated “over 7500 real-world perturbed contracts” across **10 anomaly categories** and found models “often miss subtle errors and struggle even more to justify them legally”; it motivates the work with *Perini Corp. v. Greate Bay Hotel & Casino*, where omission of a consequential-damages waiver produced **$14.5 million** in liability, over **20 times** the contract’s fee ([Better Call CLAUSE](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.00340v2)). ContractEval concludes that “most LLMs perform at a level comparable to junior legal assistants,” with proprietary models outperforming open-source ([ContractEval](https://aclanthology.org/2025.nllp-1.19/)). The widely cited **94% vs 85%** LawGeex result came from a **2018** vendor-funded study of five NDAs, with the best AI scoring **65** against lawyers’ **79.7** on redlining ([Adams on Contract Drafting](https://www.adamsdrafting.com/automated-review-of-contracts-some-thoughts-on-lawgeexs-ai-versus-humans-study/); [AttorneyHelp](https://www.attorneyhelp.org/)).

Accountability is allocated almost entirely to the lawyer. The SRA states that “the use of AI does not diminish or transfer your professional responsibilities” and that “appropriate human oversight, informed professional judgement and a proportionate, risk-based approach are essential” ([SRA](https://jobs.sra.org.uk/solicitors/guidance/misuse-ai/)). ABA Opinion 512 requires “a reasonable understanding of the capabilities and limitations” of the tool and that “any AI output must be reviewed and examined to ensure accuracy” ([ABA](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/health_law/news/2024/08/aba-ethics-opinion-use-of-ai/)). On a missed restrictive covenant or change-of-control clause, “liability rests not with the software vendor but with the solicitor who relied on it,” and engagement-letter disclaimers are unlikely to survive the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 reasonableness test unless the client expressly accepted the risk ([Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/ai-and-due-diligence-the-sras-next-regulatory-blind-spot)). The duty can extend to foreseeable third parties such as lenders and investors, widening the liability surface ([Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/ai-and-due-diligence-the-sras-next-regulatory-blind-spot)). No reported judgment specifically allocates liability for an AI tool missing a contract clause; the analysis is doctrinal, not yet litigated.

Bias in contract AI runs along a commercial axis. Kolt’s study of GPT-3 reading website terms of service found the model “performed poorly on contractual provisions that favor the rights and interests of consumers, suggesting that it may contain an anti-consumer bias,” and was “highly sensitive to the wording of questions, but surprisingly indifferent to variations in contractual language” ([Kolt, Berkeley Technology Law Journal](https://robots.law.miami.edu/2021/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Kolt-Predicting-Consumer-Contracts-August-13-2021.pdf)). CCBE flags “sycophancy” — agreeing with the user’s framing — as a distinct drafting hazard ([CCBE](https://www.ccbe.eu/document/ccbe-guide-on-the-use-of-the-use-of-generative-ai-for-lawyers/)). Access-to-justice analysis notes that tools “largely cater to large firms, creating a two-tier system that leaves small businesses underserved,” and that a tool must be free or extremely low-cost and usable by people with limited digital literacy ([Law, Innovation and Technology](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13600869.2025.2602106)). Transparency is mainly contractual: Article 13 requires high-risk systems to be “sufficiently transparent to enable deployers to interpret a system’s output” and to supply instructions covering accuracy, robustness and risks ([AI Act Service Desk, Article 13](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-13)). But Annex III point 8(a) is framed around judicial authorities and ADR, not private law-firm contract review, so most contract tools have no statutory right to model-level explanation; the operative standard is professional conduct — the lawyer must be able to understand, supervise and justify the output ([AI Act Service Desk](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/fr/node/468)).

## Contract AI concentrates benefits while eroding entry-level legal work

The credible efficiency pattern is “speed first, quality second.” The only randomised controlled trial located — law students, realistic legal tasks, blind grading — found GPT-4 access “only slightly and inconsistently improved the quality” of legal analysis but “induced large and consistent increases in speed,” with the lowest-skilled participants gaining most ([Minnesota Law Review](https://scholarship.law.umn.edu/minnlrev/vol109/iss1/3/)). A UK in-house team recorded a **60%** cut in contract review time during a live pilot and a **25–30%** productivity improvement after integration, with lawyers still reviewing every suggested change ([Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/associate-news/in-house-team-cuts-contract-review-time-by-60-after-ai-pilot-in-microsoft-word/print)). Survey evidence is perception rather than measurement: **91%** of in-house counsel cite efficiency as the most tangible GenAI benefit and **82%** see cost-saving potential in contract drafting ([ACC/Everlaw](https://www.acc.com/about/newsroom/news/acc-genai-report-corporate-law-departments-ai-use-everlaw)).

Adoption is bimodal. Among mid-sized firms, AI adoption rose from **19%** to **93%** in one year, with over half using it “widely or universally,” while **72%** of smaller firms use AI in some capacity but only **10%** extensively; only **8%** of solo practitioners and **4%** of small firms have adopted AI widely or universally ([Clio mid-sized](https://www.clio.com/au/about/press/clios-2025-legal-trends-for-mid-sized-law-firm-report/); [Clio solo/small](https://www.clio.com/about/press/legal-trends-solo-small-law-firms-2025/)). UK regulator data show law firms using generative AI rose from **14%** in **2024** to **26%** in **2025**, while among sole practitioners and small firms AI automation use is **14%**, with **82%** agreeing technology will help the sector provide better value but only **4%** seeing no barriers ([SRA Lawtech Insight](https://publications.sra.org.uk/lawtech-insight-june-2025/in-the-news)). In-house active GenAI use more than doubled to **52%** from **23%** among **657** professionals across **30** countries; **64%** expect to rely less on outside counsel, and respondents see opportunities to insource drafting (**78%**), contract management (**71%**) and research (**62%**) ([ACC/Everlaw](https://www.acc.com/about/newsroom/news/acc-genai-report-corporate-law-departments-ai-use-everlaw); [Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251014382116/en/64-of-In-House-Counsel-Expect-GenAI-to-Reduce-Reliance-on-Outside-Counsel-50-Expect-Lower-Outside-Counsel-Costs-ACCEverlaw-Survey-Finds)). Spend is rising even where savings are not visible: average law-firm technology spending grew **9.7%**, talent costs **8.2%**, and average profit per firm **13.0%** in the Thomson Reuters 2026 survey ([Thomson Reuters Institute](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/01/2026-State-of-the-US-Legal-Market.pdf)). Harvey reports **3,500+** customers and **200,000+** lawyers in **85+** countries at “several hundred dollars per user per month” ([Harvey FAQ](https://help.harvey.ai/articles/harvey-faq)). Market-size estimates conflict by an order of magnitude: legal AI software at about **$1.45bn** in **2024** at **17.3%** CAGR versus “AI in legaltech” at **$6.82bn** in **2025** rising to **$34.77bn** by **2032** at **26.2%** CAGR ([MarketPublishers listing 1](https://pdf.marketpublishers.com/); [listing 2](https://pdf.marketpublishers.com/)).

Official labour projections show stability, not collapse. BLS projects paralegals and legal assistants (**404,900** jobs) to show “little or no change” from **2025** to **2035** (**−1,100**), with about **40,800** openings a year and a median wage of **$62,890**; lawyers (**863,700** jobs) are projected to grow **5%**, with about **28,700** openings a year and a median wage of **$159,670** ([BLS paralegals](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/legal/paralegals-and-legal-assistants.htm); [BLS lawyers](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/legal/lawyers.htm)). But the entry-level pyramid has already turned down: firms with more than **500** lawyers hired **6,588** new graduates, down **538** (**7.5%**) from the **2024** peak, the first decline since **2011**; a Citi survey of **57** large firms found two-thirds expect fewer first-year associates in **2028** than **2025** and nearly **60%** expect fewer second-years ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/daily-tax-report-state/big-law-first-year-hiring-set-to-plunge-as-history-ai-collide)). NALP expects firms to “likely hire fewer” juniors because AI erodes the revenue that cross-subsidises training, warning firms may expect first-years to “perform more like today’s fourth-year associates, much, much sooner” ([NALP Bulletin](https://nalpbulletin.mydigitalpublication.com/december-2025/page-9)). Baker McKenzie is cutting up to a tenth of its global business services workforce, reportedly about **700** roles, “driven in part by its increased use of AI” ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/us-law-week/wake-up-call-hundreds-laid-off-at-baker-mckenzie-as-ai-grows)). BigHand finds **64%** of firms report a decrease in billable hours while **99%** have increased attorney targets and plan to raise them again ([Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/associate-news/latest-bighand-research-reveals-financial-fragility-and-profit-risk-among-major-law-firms)).

The billable-hour model is absorbing AI rather than dissolving. Thomson Reuters reports worked rates rose **7.3%** while clients “spent less per hour on the average legal service in 2025 than they did in 2024,” and firms remain “locked in hourly billing arrangements that may no longer reflect value delivered” ([Thomson Reuters Institute](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/01/2026-State-of-the-US-Legal-Market.pdf)). Nearly **60%** of in-house counsel report “no noticeable savings yet” from outside counsel’s GenAI use, **58%** say firms “haven’t adjusted pricing,” and **61%** are likely to push for pricing changes; **43%** expect more value-based billing ([ACC/Everlaw](https://www.acc.com/about/newsroom/news/acc-genai-report-corporate-law-departments-ai-use-everlaw)). Nearly **90%** of firms confirm increased write-offs, **88%** expect further increases in **2026**, **90%** report increased discounts or write-downs, and **29%** cite discounts of **11–20%** ([Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/associate-news/latest-bighand-research-reveals-financial-fragility-and-profit-risk-among-major-law-firms)). **26%** of corporate legal departments expect to cut spending on law firms in **2026**, and **85%** now have a dedicated resource or committee managing AI ([Artificial Lawyer](https://www.artificiallawyer.com/2025/12/08/some-clients-see-law-firm-spend-drop-just-as-ai-embeds/)). UK firms’ median fee income rose **11.2%** in **2025**, with **85%** reporting fee growth and fee earners up **2.3%** ([Law Society](https://www.lawonsociety.org.uk/contact-or-visit-us/press-office/press-releases/legal-sector-sees-strong-growth-annual-survey-finds)).

Access to justice is improving through provider-side efficiency, not consumer-side price cuts. Legal aid has adopted AI unusually fast: **74%** of legal-aid organisations surveyed (**112** professionals, **May 2025**) already use AI, double the wider profession’s rate, while legal-services organisations turn away about half of eligible applicants ([LSC](https://www.lsc.gov/press-release/talk-justice-lsc-podcast-why-legal-aid-embracing-ai-faster-other-legal-professionals)). Globally, **86%** of **56** legal-aid organisations reported using at least one AI tool, mostly for back-office tasks such as translation, drafting and proofreading; cost was the most cited barrier and nearly one-third flagged bias risk ([HiiL](https://hiil.org/news/hiil-launches-new-report-ai-in-access-to-justice/)). The SRA has authorised a fully AI-delivered regulated service for SME debt recovery after checking quality control, confidentiality, conflicts and hallucination risk; in a poll of **4,000+** UK/US respondents, **30%** would allow an AI system to represent them and **47%** said lower cost would encourage AI use ([SRA](https://publications.sra.org.uk/lawtech-insight-june-2025/in-the-news)). But no measured evidence shows AI contract review has reduced the price paid by individuals or SMEs at scale, and small firms face cost, data-compliance and “wild west” option barriers ([SRA](https://publications.sra.org.uk/lawtech-insight-june-2025/in-the-news)).

The net economic case is narrower than gross time savings. Validation overhead is structural: in the documented UK pilot, lawyers remained responsible for reviewing every AI-suggested change ([Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/associate-news/in-house-team-cuts-contract-review-time-by-60-after-ai-pilot-in-microsoft-word/print)). Only **12%** and **16%** of in-house teams track technology ROI or outcomes relative to cost ([ACC](https://www.acc.com/about/newsroom/news/acc-genai-report-corporate-law-departments-ai-use-everlaw)). A general enterprise benchmark found **95%** of corporate generative-AI pilots showed no measurable P&L impact across **300** public deployments, **150** executive interviews and **350** employee surveys — not legal-specific, but the only large-N independent assessment located ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/08/18/mit-report-95-generative-ai-pilots-at-companies-failing/)). AI seats at several hundred dollars per user per month mean payback depends entirely on utilisation, and the realistic operating model is AI plus reviewer, which caps savings at the reviewer’s marginal cost ([Harvey FAQ](https://help.harvey.ai/articles/harvey-faq)).

## Responsible legal AI requires enforceable process, not principles alone

The synthesis across the two cases is that fairness requires an explicit metric, accountability must be non-delegable, and transparency must be contestable. In policing, calibration and equal false-positive rates cannot both be satisfied when base rates differ, so a jurisdiction must decide, constitutionally and politically, whether it is equalising error rates, predictive parity or something else; “debiasing” cannot satisfy incompatible criteria ([ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing); [Legal Bond Index preprint](https://zenodo.org/)). In contract AI, fairness is primarily alignment: a model trained on commercial drafting may encode anti-consumer assumptions or sycophantic reasoning, so prompt framing and client side become sources of disparate treatment ([Kolt](https://robots.law.miami.edu/2021/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Kolt-Predicting-Consumer-Contracts-August-13-2021.pdf); [CCBE](https://www.ccbe.eu/document/ccbe-guide-on-the-use-of-the-use-of-generative-ai-for-lawyers/)). The same principle — fairness is a design and governance choice, not a model property — applies in both contexts, but the affected party differs: a defendant facing a score versus a consumer or small business relying on an AI-reviewed contract.

Accountability is already allocated, but unevenly. For contract work, the SRA and ABA are explicit that AI use “does not diminish or transfer” professional responsibility, and English commentary places liability on the solicitor who relied on the output, with third-party reliance widening exposure ([SRA](https://jobs.sra.org.uk/solicitors/guidance/misuse-ai/); [Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/ai-and-due-diligence-the-sras-next-regulatory-blind-spot)). For policing, accountability is split between the state deployer and the vendor, and the practical barrier is contractual secrecy: New Orleans’ Palantir programme operated without council knowledge or public oversight, and defence attorneys never received analytical products in discovery ([npj Urban Sustainability](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42949-026-00394-1)). The EU model tries to formalise the split: providers face conformity assessment, CE marking and registration, while deployers must assign human oversight and keep logs ([AI Act Service Desk](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/taxonomy/term/71); [Article 26](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-26)). But Article 86’s explanation right is residual and subject to national-law exceptions, so its value in criminal justice depends on domestic procedure ([Article 86](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/86/)).

The safeguards menu is stable across jurisdictions: pre-deployment algorithmic impact assessments, independent audits with access to model internals, notice and contestability rights, mandated human judgement, and community or privacy-commissioner oversight. The Ontario Human Rights Commission position requires impact assessments before procurement and amendment or abandonment if discriminatory outcomes appear; a UBC submission recommends reliability, necessity and proportionality prerequisites, moratoria on some uses, and mandatory algorithmic impact assessments ([Law Commission of Ontario](https://www.lco-cdo.org/); [UBC Allard](https://commons.allard.ubc.ca/)). A legal analysis recommends “technical and legal audit trails for all AI tools deployed in criminal investigations” so defendants can contest results, plus regular bias audits ([NLSIU repository](https://repository.nls.ac.in/)). New York City’s Automated Decision Systems Task Force recommended public disclosure, third-party audits and impact assessments ([IJLMH](https://ijlmh.com/)). For contract AI, the equivalent is procurement-plus-process: no-training and no-retention terms, sub-processor disclosure, private or in-house deployment, informed client consent, and documented human verification with supervision ([ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf); [SRA](https://jobs.sra.org.uk/solicitors/guidance/misuse-ai/)).

Evidence that these safeguards work is thin and often negative. Audit systems are “overwhelmingly bespoke,” with only **7%** using a standardised methodology, and many independent auditors rely on compensation from the organisations they evaluate, creating “perverse incentives to avoid unfavorable findings” ([Montreal AI Ethics Institute](https://montrealethics.ai); [Carnegie Mellon University](https://ml.cmu.edu)). Only **8 of 29** members of the Global Partnership on AI have at least one public-sector algorithm register, and Ontario’s registry reportedly contained only **three** examples across the entire public service ([OBVIA](https://www.obvia.ca); [LCO/CDO](https://lco-cdo.org)). *Loomis*’s safeguards were paper warnings, and subsequent Wisconsin decisions upheld COMPAS where the trial court “commented on the report only briefly” ([Harvard JOLT](https://jolt.law.harvard.edu/digest/algorithmic-due-process-mistaken-accountability-and-attribution-in-state-v-loomis-1); [Florida Courts](https://www.flcourts.gov/)). Human-in-the-loop is double-edged: experimental evidence indicates judges seeing a COMPAS result before making their own prediction did not improve overall accuracy but did create unfairness favouring white defendants on fairness metrics ([Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/)). The ICO’s **2018** enforcement against the Gangs Matrix was followed by years of continued operation before discontinuation, and Chicago’s SSL ran from **2012** to **2019** despite Inspector General and RAND findings, consuming **$3.8 million** in federal grants ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-the-public/ico-40/metropolitan-police-gangs-matrix/); [npj Urban Sustainability](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42949-026-00394-1)).

The limits of the framework layer are structural. “Fugazi regulation” describes “vaguely defined, non-enforceable moral principles with no specific implementation mechanisms,” and corporate ethics offices can be “merely symbolic,” meeting infrequently and issuing recommendations that teams ignore ([AI & Society](https://rd.springer.com); [Canadian Journal of Philosophy](https://resolve.cambridge.org)). The EU deferred high-risk obligations because harmonised standards were unavailable, with the standardisation request revised to a **February 2027** deadline, and the Council of Europe Convention remains not in force ([CEN-CENELEC](https://www.cencenelc.eu); [ActuIA](https://www.actuia.com)). Transparency-only strategies are distributionally regressive: a defendant facing a recidivism score, or a consumer facing an erroneous AI contract review, must first know the system was used and then have the capacity to demand an explanation — burdens that fall hardest on unrepresented and smaller parties. That is an inference from the structure of Articles 26 and 86 and Article 5(1)(d)’s carve-out, not a measured finding.

## Conclusion

The comparative lesson for legal AI is that the two contexts need different institutional remedies built from the same principles. Predictive policing should be restricted to place-based forecasting that doses existing evidence-based patrol tactics, benchmarked against analyst-led hot spots, with published accuracy audits, independent oversight and pre-committed evaluation; person-based risk lists fail the cost-benefit test on the evidence reviewed, because they show no measured victimisation benefit, measurable increases in arrest exposure and demonstrated legal liability. Contract AI should be governed through procurement and verification: private or in-house instances, no training on client data, sub-processor transparency, informed client consent, and documented human review against source text. In both cases the binding constraint is not the availability of auditing techniques but access, funding separation, standardisation and a remedy for the person affected.

Responsible legal AI is therefore less about better models than about allocating discretion and error. The lawyer remains the human-in-the-loop for contract analysis, and the state remains accountable for coercive policing; neither can outsource that responsibility to a vendor’s warranty or a model card. The frameworks supply the vocabulary — oversight, transparency, fairness, accountability — but the enforceable work happens in procurement terms, audit access, logging, explanation rights and professional discipline. Where those mechanisms are absent, ethics language functions as a substitute for governance rather than a check on it.
