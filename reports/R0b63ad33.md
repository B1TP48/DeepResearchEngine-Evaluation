# Breaking Regimes: Strain, Coalitions, Coercive Collapse

All three revolutions were conjunctural state breakdowns, not single-cause upheavals. In each, fiscal or supply strain exposed a regime whose tax and legitimacy bargain had failed; elite defection and autonomous networks converted grievance into multiple sovereignty; and the decisive proximate shift was the withdrawal or paralysis of coercive loyalty. Structural theory explains why the old regimes were vulnerable, but organizational theory explains why France became a propertied-bureaucratic state, Russia a one-party socialist state, and Iran a clerical-republican theocracy ([PhilPapers review essay](https://philpapers.org/archive/BRZAVR.pdf); [Brinton](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution); [Tilly](https://reserves.vmi.edu/hiermanbd/Archived/states_and_social_revolutions.pdf)). The contrast is sharpest in the carrier of opposition: a factional Jacobin and sans-culotte network, a disciplined Bolshevik party, and a nationwide Shi'a clerical institution. This synthesis yields a seven-variable framework and a ranked risk assessment for late 2026; on that framework Iran is the highest-risk case, but regime survival remains more likely than revolutionary overthrow in the near term.

## Fiscal strain and legitimating ideologies set the stage, but no single cause explains outbreak

France entered 1789 with a structurally insolvent monarchy: the American war left a debt of about 3.4 billion livres and an annual deficit near 80 million, while debt service consumed on the order of half of revenue ([Hampson](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/mono/10.4324/9781315887883-6/victory-aristocracy-norman-hampson?context=ubx)). Necker’s May 1789 statement put the deficit at 56.15 million livres on 531.4 million of spending, though rival accounting gives only 27.2 million, about 7 per cent, and the fiscal baseline is contested ([Internet Archive](https://archive.org); [Foundation for Economic Education](https://fee.org)). The tax system exempted nobles and officials from the *vingtième* and relied on the *gabelle* for nearly a tenth of royal revenue ([Britannica](https://kids.britannica.com)). The 1788 harvest cut grain yields by nearly a quarter, the four-pound loaf rose from 8 to 14 sous by January 1789, and bread consumed roughly 55 per cent of a worker’s budget ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Why-did-bread-prices-rise-sharply-in-Paris-before-the-storming-of-the-Bastille); [AMS](https://journals.ametsoc.org)). Elite refusal to be taxed forced the Estates-General; peasant mobilisations in July 1789 then pushed deputies to abolish feudalism ([CHNM](https://revolution.chnm.org/items?output=atom&page=5&sort_field=Dublin+Core,Title&tag=Text)). The classic “rising bourgeoisie” thesis is contested: Cobban and Tackett found declining office-holders and lawyers, and little coherent pre-revolutionary oppositional ideology ([Wikipedia historiography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography_of_the_French_Revolution); [H-Net](https://www.h-net.org)), while Lefebvre’s Marxist account tied the revolution to the feudal-capitalist transition ([Brill](https://brill.com)).

The French ideological sequence widened sovereignty and narrowed pluralism. Sieyès’s claim that the Third Estate “is everything” supplied the national claim; the 1789 Declaration universalised rights; the 1793 constitution established universal male suffrage but was never applied; and the Civil Constitution of the Clergy made the Church a state department, with about half the parish clergy taking the oath ([Open University](https://www.open.edu); [Conseil constitutionnel](https://www.conseil-constitutionnel.fr); [CHNM](https://revolution.chnm.org); [Oxford Reference](https://www.oxfordreference.com/display/10.1093/oi/authority.20110803095614278)). Dechristianisation from 1793 to 1794 was locally driven rather than a single committee programme, and Robespierre halted it by the cult of the Supreme Being ([Musée protestant](https://museeprotestant.org/en/notice/dechristianisation-during-the-reign-of-terror-1793-1794/)). Napoleon’s Code Civil of 1804 codified civil equality and property but restored patriarchal authority and rolled back divorce ([Google Books](https://books.google.com.sg)).

Russia’s crisis was developmental and military. Emancipation in 1861 left peasants with redemption debts and communal tenure; late state-sponsored industrialisation created a small, hyper-concentrated proletariat—40 per cent of workers in plants of 1,000 or more by 1914, against 18 per cent in the United States—while St Petersburg doubled to 1.9 million between 1890 and 1910 ([Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution)). WWI killed or captured nearly five million men by late 1916, quadrupled prices and broke rail and food procurement; bread rationing protests on 23 February 1917 became a general strike, and on 27 February the Petrograd garrison defected ([Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution); [February Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_Revolution)). The autocracy had already lost its elite: Duma liberals demanded responsible government in 1915 and senior officers discussed forcing abdication in 1916 ([Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution)). Lenin’s April Theses rejected stagist support for the Provisional Government, demanded all power to the soviets, and turned the party into the only major force promising peace, land and bread ([April Theses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/April_Theses)). Mensheviks and SRs offered rival socialist paths; the Left SRs joined the government then broke over Brest-Litovsk ([Russian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Civil_War)). After October, proletarian dictatorship was operationalised through war communism, forced requisition and the Cheka, then partially reversed by NEP, which Lenin called “one step backward to take two steps forward” ([War communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_communism); [NEP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Economic_Policy)).

Iran’s crisis was a rentier-modernisation crisis, not a subsistence one. The 1963 White Revolution redistributed land to about 2.5 million families but broke the *vaqf* endowments that financed the ulama and pushed failed farmers into Tehran and other cities ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/White-Revolution)). Oil income jumped from $2.3 billion in 1970 to $20.7 billion in 1974, inflation reached 27.29 per cent in 1977, and SAVAK fielded about 5,300 full-time agents by 1977 ([Cambridge summary](https://www.cambridge.org); [IndexMundi](https://www.indexmundi.com); [SAVAK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAVAK)). The Shah was widely seen as corrupt and beholden to Washington; the revolution occurred in relative prosperity, lacking war defeat, financial collapse, peasant rebellion or a disloyal military ([Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)). Kurzman calls it an “unthinkable” revolution whose timing is poorly captured by standard structural variables ([Harvard University Press](https://www.hup.harvard.edu)). Khomeini’s 1970 Najaf lectures supplied *velayat-e faqih* as a theory of clerical guardianship; Shariati’s “red Shi’ism” gave students a revolutionary vocabulary; the Mojahedin blended Shi’ism with Marxism; and “Neither East nor West” framed anti-imperialism ([Islamic Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Government); [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org); [Mojahedin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People%27s_Mojahedin_Organization_of_Iran); [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com)). Karbala martyrdom symbolism cast the Shah as Yazid and made compromise with the regime sacrilegious ([Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)).

## Organizational carriers and coercive loyalty decide whether protest becomes revolution

In France, sovereignty moved from the Estates-General to the National Assembly on 17 June 1789, then to the Committee of Public Safety created on 6 April 1793, then to the Directory and Napoleon ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.edu.au); [Branch Collective](https://branchcollective.org); [Vie-publique.fr](https://www.vie-publique.fr)). The radical base was the Parisian sections and sans-culottes, artisans and shopkeepers who gave the Jacobin elite street capacity ([De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com)). War was the radicalising channel: the Brunswick Manifesto of July 1792 preceded the 10 August storming of the Tuileries, and the *levée en masse* of 23 August 1793 called up all single men aged 18–25—about 750,000—before the Jourdan law of 1798 institutionalised conscription ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.taylorfrancis.com); [Elizabeth Cross](https://academic.oup.com/fh/article-abstract/25/2/188/605450); [Internet Archive](https://archive.org); [Assemblée nationale](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr)). Counter-revolution was substantial but fragmented: about 150,000 émigrés left, the Vendée war killed between 120,000 and 600,000 by competing estimates, and the Terror’s 16,594 judicial executions followed the geography of counter-revolution and invasion ([Archives nationales](https://www.siv.archives-nationales.culture.gouv.fr); [La Libre Belgique](https://www.lalibre.be); [Colin Jones](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/mono/10.4324/9781315836416-34/impact-terror-colin-jones?context=ubx)). Greer’s political, classless and geographically reactive interpretation remains authoritative ([Journal of Modern History](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu)).

Russia’s February revolution broke out without central leadership, but dual power gave the Bolsheviks an alternative legitimacy in the soviets ([February Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_Revolution)). After the Kornilov affair in September 1917, Bolshevik influence in the soviets surged; on 10 October the Petrograd Soviet backed an uprising, and the Military-Revolutionary Committee deployed about 10,000 sailors and 20,000–30,000 Red Guards against under 2,000 loyal troops ([Russian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Civil_War); [October Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_Revolution)). The party had 250,000–300,000 members, but central coordination in the capital, not mass membership, was decisive ([Seventeen Moments](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/communist-party-building/)). Counter-revolution was divided among White generals, SR-Menshevik “democratic counter-revolution,” nationalist movements, peasant insurgents and thirteen foreign states; the Whites’ “united and indivisible Russia” programme denied land and self-determination and narrowed their base ([Russian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Civil_War)). Bolshevik victory rested on the industrial core, railways, a 5.5 million-strong Red Army, Cheka terror and hostage-taking; the Red Terror killed an estimated 50,000–600,000, Tambov cost about 15,000 lives and 100,000 arrests, and Kronstadt was crushed with about 1,000 killed and 1,200–2,168 executed ([Red Terror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Terror); [Tambov Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tambov_Rebellion); [Kronstadt rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kronstadt_rebellion)).

Iran’s movement was decentralised in the mosques, bazaars and seminaries but directed by a secret clerical nucleus. Khomeini formed the Revolutionary Council on 10 January 1979, before his 1 February return; it mixed seven clerics, seven secular figures and two security representatives and issued hundreds of decrees while remaining hidden until 1980 ([Council of the Islamic Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_the_Islamic_Revolution)). The Shah left on 16 January, Bakhtiar’s government collapsed when the Supreme Military Council withdrew support on 11–12 February, and the monarchy fell ([Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)). The hostage crisis from 4 November 1979 marginalised moderate and secular allies and internationalised the revolution; Operation Eagle Claw failed on 24–25 April 1980 ([Iran hostage crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran_hostage_crisis); [Operation Eagle Claw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Eagle_Claw)). The regime then crushed former allies: the Mojahedin’s June 1981 demonstration was suppressed, more than 7,900 people were executed in 1981–85, and 4,500–5,000 prisoners were extrajudicially executed in 1988 ([Human rights in Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Iran); [Casualties of the Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casualties_of_the_Iranian_Revolution); [Amnesty International](https://www.amnesty.org.uk)). The IRGC and Basij, founded in 1979–80, gave the clergy a coercive apparatus independent of the regular army ([Khamenei.ir](https://english.khamenei.ir); [De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com)).

The pattern supports Tilly’s multiple sovereignty and Brinton’s organizational advantage: radicals win when they are better organized, better staffed and face moderates who bear the old regime’s unpopularity ([Tilly](https://reserves.vmi.edu/hiermanbd/Archived/states_and_social_revolutions.pdf); [Brinton](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution)). Skocpol’s structural claim explains the openings—state crisis under international military-fiscal pressure plus peasant insurrection—but Iran is the anomaly: its army collapsed without a foreign war defeat, and no peasant rebellion preceded 1979 ([PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/archive/BRZAVR.pdf); [Background and causes of the Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/api/rest_v1/page/html/Background_and_causes_of_the_Iranian_Revolution)).

## Institutional outcomes diverged even though all three built stronger states

All three revolutions increased state capacity and centralisation. France replaced feudal privilege with a departmental, metric, conscript state; Russia replaced tsarist bureaucracy with a party-state that nationalised industry; Iran replaced the monarchy with a clerical-republican constitution and parallel coercive bodies. The divergent outcomes are not a matter of “more” or “less” revolution but of which coalition captured the new state.

## A configurational framework ranks risk but cannot time revolutions

The framework synthesises Brinton’s life-cycle, Skocpol’s state-class structuralism, Tilly’s multiple sovereignty, Goldstone’s fiscal-demographic strain, Kuran’s preference falsification and Foran’s world-systemic opening ([Brinton](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution); [Skocpol](https://philpapers.org/archive/BRZAVR.pdf); [Tilly](https://reserves.vmi.edu/hiermanbd/Archived/states_and_social_revolutions.pdf); [Goldstone](http://books.google.co.id/books/edition/_/M-T9dR7nWDUC?hl=en&sa=X&ved=2ahUKEwisn8iurISKAxXwxDgGHSjzBdAQ8fIDegQIDhAI); [Kuran](https://casssunstein.substack.com/p/on-syria-and-unpredictable-revolutions); [Foran](https://www.cambridge.org/li/universitypress/subjects/sociology/political-sociology/taking-power-origins-third-world-revolutions)). It treats revolution as a sequence: stable authoritarian equilibrium; fiscal and elite strain; frame articulation and mass mobilisation; multiple sovereignty; coercive collapse; radicalisation and coalition fragmentation; Thermidor or consolidation; new equilibrium. The variables are fiscal-administrative strain, elite fragmentation, autonomous mass networks and international opening; mediating legitimation frames, organizational capacity and preference cascades; the proximate coercive-loyalty shift; and regime outcome type. No condition is sufficient. All four structural conditions and a coercive-loyalty shift appear in France, Russia and Iran, but each also appears without revolution; the coercive shift is the closest to a necessary proximate condition ([Tilly](https://reserves.vmi.edu/hiermanbd/Archived/states_and_social_revolutions.pdf); [Brinton](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution)).

Outcomes diverged through three mechanisms: state-class structure sets the menu of possible new orders; organizational form selects which contender captures the state; war and counter-revolution determine how much coercion the winner can deploy ([Skocpol](https://philpapers.org/archive/BRZAVR.pdf); [Brinton](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution); [Tilly](https://reserves.vmi.edu/hiermanbd/Archived/states_and_social_revolutions.pdf)). France’s propertied-bureaucratic fusion, Russia’s over-determined one-party outcome and Iran’s “ideology–organisation fit” follow that logic. Thermidor theory predicts de-radicalisation in France and Russia’s NEP, but predicts poorly for Iran, where clerical radicalisation continued after the Shah fell ([Brinton](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution)).

The design has known limits: selection on the dependent variable, Galton’s problem from diffusion, conceptual stretching and retrospective bias ([eScholarship](https://escholarship.org); [Clarke](https://www.annualreviews.org/docserver/fulltext/polisci/28/1/annurev-polisci-040623-114124.pdf?expires=1754592282&id=id&accname=guest&checksum=5F9144A46C769F9391E8EF7C9C437606); [Kurzman](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/524788?mi=b2ydrw&af=R&ContribRaw=Ermakoff%2C+Ivan&SeriesKey=ajs&target=default)). The three cases are not independent—France’s wars, Russia’s WWI and Iran’s transnational clerical networks violate independence—and all are successful revolutions, so base rates cannot be estimated. The framework should rank risk and specify falsifiers, not produce point predictions. The global backdrop is an autocratization plateau of about 45 countries, with 74 per cent of world population in autocracies ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com); [IW EPS](https://www.iweps.be)).

The predictive content is in the ordering, not the trigger. A bus crash, canopy collapse or blackout can start the sequence, but regime change requires a security-force hedge or elite split; every 2025–26 success case shows that, while every failure case shows sustained lethal firing ([New York Post](https://nypost.com/2026/11/19/world-news/protesters-call-for-regime-change-in-iran-following-deadly-crash-that-killed-13-oil-and-gas-workers/); [Foreign Affairs](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/iran/third-islamic-republic-middle-east-suzanne-maloney)). Forecast accuracy is period-dependent: Goldstone and colleagues predicted 85 per cent of instability onsets in 1995–2004, but later performance deteriorated and the drivers are not constant ([Bowlsby et al.](https://https-www-cambridge-org-443.webvpn.ynu.edu.cn/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/0028744BE1AFF83F879E7759D798D88A/S0007123418000443a.pdf/future_is_a_moving_target_predicting_political_instability.pdf)). Only 17 of 169 nonviolent campaigns reached the 3.5 per cent peak-participation threshold, and Bahrain failed above 6 per cent, so participation alone is insufficient ([Harvard Kennedy School](https://www.hks.harvard.edu)). Predictions should be recorded as probabilities and scored with a Brier-type rule at fixed horizons rather than as narrative forecasts ([Good Judgment](https://goodjudgment.com)).

## Conclusion

The three revolutions share a causal grammar but not a script. Fiscal strain, elite fracture, autonomous networks and a coercive-loyalty shift are the recurrent preconditions; organizational form and war determine the regime that emerges. The strongest forecast is not that Iran will revolutionise, but that it will produce repeated near-revolutions while its security apparatus holds. The framework’s value is to specify what would falsify that judgement: IRGC defection, a sustained general strike, or clerical rupture. For comparative theory, the cases support a configurational, process-based approach rather than a single necessary-and-sufficient model; for prediction, they counsel ranked risk with wide bands and explicit disconfirmers.
