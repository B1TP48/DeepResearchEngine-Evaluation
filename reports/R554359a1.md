# Govern Legal AI Before It Governs

Responsible AI in the legal field requires more than accurate predictions or faster document review: it requires limits on use, scrutiny of the data and consequences, and accountable human judgment. **Predictive policing** means systems that forecast crime at places or estimate risk for individuals; these uses differ because one directs attention toward locations while the other can affect a named person’s liberty or supervision ([LAPD Office of the Inspector General, *Review of Selected Data-Driven Policing Strategies*](https://www.lapdpolicecom.lacity.org/031219/BPC_19-0072.pdf); [Harvard Law Review, *State v. Loomis*](https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-130/state-v-loomis/)). **AI-assisted contract analysis** means using tools to identify or extract clauses, flag risks, summarize agreements, or help draft legal text; conventional systems often classify passages against defined categories, while generative systems can produce new text ([CUAD: An Expert-Annotated NLP Dataset for Legal Contract Review](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268); [ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf)). The relevant measures also differ: policing claims should be tested against public-safety outcomes and effects on affected communities, while contract tools should be tested on the accuracy and completeness of specific tasks and on total work after human review. Evidence supports possible gains in targeted patrol and routine legal work, but it is context-specific and does not establish that either class of system is reliably fair or beneficial in general. Predictive policing carries unusually direct risks to civil liberties and public trust because it can change who is watched and where; contract AI more directly raises confidentiality, professional-responsibility, work-quality, and labor-distribution concerns. Across both settings, responsible use means demonstrating task-specific value, making errors and impacts reviewable, and ensuring that accountable people—not automated outputs—remain responsible for consequential decisions.

## Predictive policing can redirect both patrol and scrutiny

Place-based and person-based systems perform different functions and should not be treated as a single technology. LAPD’s PredPol used roughly a decade of records on crime type, location, date, and time to generate 12-hour maps directing patrols to small areas; the department’s Inspector General said the tool did not use information about specific people ([LAPD Office of the Inspector General, *Review of Selected Data-Driven Policing Strategies*](https://www.lapdpolicecom.lacity.org/031219/BPC_19-0072.pdf)). By contrast, LAPD’s Operation LASER paired hotspot policing with a “Chronic Offender Program,” in which inconsistent local selection and documentation practices affected which people were identified for follow-up ([LAPD Office of the Inspector General, *Review of Selected Data-Driven Policing Strategies*](https://www.lapdpolicecom.lacity.org/031219/BPC_19-0072.pdf)). The NYPD’s Patternizr provides a further distinction: analysts use it to identify possible patterns among crimes for review; it is not described as an automated finding that a person committed an offense ([New York City, *2021 Algorithmic Tools Reporting*](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/oti/downloads/pdf/reports/2021-algorithmic-tools-reporting.pdf)). COMPAS, by contrast, estimates an individual’s risk of reoffending and can inform decisions about supervision or sentencing. In *State v. Loomis*, the Wisconsin Supreme Court allowed its use as decision support subject to cautions, including that it not be the sole basis for incarceration or sentence severity; the court also identified concerns about proprietary calculations, group-based predictions, absent Wisconsin cross-validation, and potential racial disparities ([Harvard Law Review, *State v. Loomis*](https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-130/state-v-loomis/)).

The central fairness problem is that police records measure enforcement as well as crime. In an Oakland simulation, Lum and Isaac found that applying PredPol-style forecasting to drug-arrest data would direct disproportionate attention toward Black and low-income areas, even though survey-based estimates suggested drug use was more widely distributed. This was a simulation, not an audit of a live system, but it demonstrates how enforcement-shaped records can turn prior scrutiny into a forecast of future scrutiny ([Lum and Isaac, “To Predict and Serve?”](https://rss.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1740-9713.2016.00960.x)). A modeled feedback mechanism deepens the concern: more patrol can produce more police-discovered incidents, which become inputs to later forecasts and may send police back to the same places; resident-reported incidents may reduce, but do not necessarily eliminate, that risk ([Ensign et al., “Runaway Feedback Loops in Predictive Policing”](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v81/ensign18a)). Person-risk tools present a different but related fairness problem: their errors can affect specific people’s liberty, while statistical measures of fairness may conflict. ProPublica reported different false-positive and false-negative patterns for Black and White defendants in its COMPAS analysis; subsequent fairness research explains that calibration and equal error rates generally cannot both be achieved when group base rates differ and prediction is imperfect ([ProPublica, “How We Analyzed the COMPAS Recidivism Algorithm”](https://www.propublica.org/article/how-we-analyzed-the-compas-recidivism-algorithm); [Chouldechova, “Fair Prediction with Disparate Impact”](https://doi.org/10.1089/BIG.2016.0047)). The ethical question is therefore not just whether a score is “accurate,” but which errors matter, who bears them, and whether the chosen fairness standard fits the decision.

The evidence is mixed rather than uniformly adverse or favorable. A randomized LAPD field-trial analysis found no statistically significant difference in the racial or ethnic composition of arrests between algorithmic-patrol and control conditions, but its conclusion concerns measured arrests in the studied locations—not all forms of contact, surveillance, force, or downstream impact ([Brantingham, Valasik, and Mohler, “Does Predictive Policing Lead to Biased Arrests?”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2330443X.2018.1438940)). A systematic review of sentencing risk tools found predictive performance that was mixed and generally poor to moderate; in independent studies with samples above 500, reported AUCs ranged from 0.57 to 0.75, and most studies did not report calibration or false-positive and false-negative rates ([Monahan and Skeem, “The Predictive Performance of Criminal Risk Assessment Tools Used at Sentencing”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36530210/)). The Los Angeles and Kent randomized trials found that the ETAS model predicted 1.4–2.2 times as much crime as dedicated analysts’ forecasts in those study settings, and reported a 7.4% reduction in crime volume per 1,000 minutes of patrol for ETAS-directed patrol relative to analyst-directed patrol ([Mohler et al., “Randomized Controlled Field Trials of Predictive Policing”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01621459.2015.1077710)). Yet a Shreveport experiment found no statistically significant property-crime reduction relative to control districts, and a systematic review reports mixed effectiveness evidence, much of it based on historical-data prediction rather than measured outcomes after deployment ([Evaluation of the Shreveport Predictive Policing Experiment, NIJ](https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/evaluation-shreveport-predictive-policing-experiment-final-report); [“The Effectiveness of Big Data-Driven Predictive Policing: Systematic Review”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/24751979.2024.2371781)). The Los Angeles result shows that resource allocation can improve in particular conditions; it does not establish that algorithmic forecasting generally outperforms human analysis or makes policing fairer.

Opacity and weak evaluation also undermine accountability and trust. The LAPD Inspector General found discrepancies between data systems, difficulty attributing activity to programs, and inconsistent records that impaired assessment of effectiveness and resource deployment ([LAPD Office of the Inspector General, *Review of Selected Data-Driven Policing Strategies*](https://www.lapdpolicecom.lacity.org/031219/BPC_19-0072.pdf)). The Chicago Inspector General found unreliable scores, insufficient training, access-control gaps, and failures to evaluate several model versions before the department decommissioned its predictive risk models in 2019 ([Chicago Police Department’s Predictive Risk Models, Chicago Office of Inspector General](https://igchicago.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/OIG-Advisory-Concerning-CPDs-Predictive-Risk-Models-.pdf)). Secrecy can also erode legitimacy: New Orleans’ Palantir program operated without public disclosure or consultation, and the city ended it after public reporting; the arrangement involved criminal and non-criminal data ([Yale Law School, *Algorithmic Accountability Report*](https://law.yale.edu/sites/default/files/area/center/mfia/document/algorithmic_accountability_report.pdf)). In a Dutch survey experiment with 877 participants, information about external monitoring increased reported trust in predictive policing, while information about legislation alone did not; this suggests oversight may matter to trust, but it measures stated responses in one context, not real-world changes in police-community relations ([Nieuwenhuizen et al., “Does Institutional Transparency Affect Citizen Trust in Predictive Policing?”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/padm.70034); [LAPD Office of the Inspector General, *Review of Selected Data-Driven Policing Strategies*](https://www.lapdpolicecom.lacity.org/031219/BPC_19-0072.pdf)).

The economic case also needs careful accounting. The National Institute of Justice reported a model-based projection of about $17.3 million in annual societal savings for a specified Los Angeles patrol dosage, based on estimated social costs of crime; it was not an observed net saving after software, implementation, oversight, or personnel costs ([National Institute of Justice, *Predictive Policing Model in Los Angeles, Calif.*](https://crimesolutions.ojp.gov/ratedprograms/predictive-policing-model-los-angeles-calif)). The LAPD Inspector General identified $899,550.54 in LASER expenditures over the reported period and estimated the broader Palantir application suite at about $1.8 million annually, while noting that the suite supported functions beyond LASER ([LAPD Office of the Inspector General, *Review of Selected Data-Driven Policing Strategies*](https://www.lapdpolicecom.lacity.org/031219/BPC_19-0072.pdf)). Chicago received $3.8 million in federal grants for risk-model development, illustrating how public funding can reduce the immediate cost of adoption without proving lasting value ([Chicago Police Department’s Predictive Risk Models, Chicago Office of Inspector General](https://igchicago.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/OIG-Advisory-Concerning-CPDs-Predictive-Risk-Models-.pdf)). Vendor-subsidized systems may also create a foothold through data access, model-training opportunities, or future procurement advantages, as the New Orleans example illustrates ([Public Procurement of Artificial Intelligence Systems: New Risks and Future Proofing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9526785/)). Hotspot policing itself has evidence of crime reduction, but that does not establish that an algorithm selects hotspots better than analysts or that algorithmic policing produces net savings ([Braga et al., *Hot Spots Policing and Crime Reduction: An Update of an Ongoing Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis*](https://www.ojp.gov/library/publications/hot-spots-policing-and-crime-reduction-update-ongoing-systematic-review-and)).

## Contract AI can accelerate routine work but not transfer responsibility

Contract-analysis AI ranges from bounded extraction systems to generative tools. CUAD frames review as finding text relevant to 41 categories, including dates, governing law, restrictive covenants, and revenue risks, so that a human reviewer can examine relevant passages ([CUAD: An Expert-Annotated NLP Dataset for Legal Contract Review](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)). The ABA identifies analytics for due diligence in mergers and large transactions as an established use and says generative AI can assist with review, drafting, and due diligence, while warning that such systems produce statistically probable text rather than guaranteed legal conclusions ([ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf)). These are distinct tasks: finding a clause is not the same as interpreting its legal effect, and producing draft language is not the same as ensuring it reflects a client’s interests.

Benchmark evidence makes that distinction consequential. CUAD’s 2021 experiments found substantial variation across clause categories: some approached 100% AUPR, while others were around 20%; the authors characterized performance as nascent and noted that SEC-filed contracts may be more complex and heavily negotiated than the general contract population ([CUAD: An Expert-Annotated NLP Dataset for Legal Contract Review](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)). A 2025 ContractEval benchmark tested 4 proprietary and 15 open-source LLMs on clause-level risk identification; it found proprietary models stronger on correctness and output effectiveness, while identifying a tendency among open-source models to say no related clause existed when one did, and a tradeoff in which reasoning modes improved effectiveness but reduced correctness ([ContractEval](https://aclanthology.org/2025.nllp-1.19.pdf)). A 2026 ContractScrub preprint evaluates 3,014 tasks drawn from 44 contracts and reports that only one tested frontier model reached 0.75 macro-average recall, underscoring that general capability does not guarantee performance on specialized contract-scrubbing tasks ([ContractScrub: A Benchmark for Final Review of Legal Contracts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20204)). Another 2026 study found 80.2% correctness for GPT alone on 2,091 NDA-hypothesis pairs and cautioned against treating consumer LLMs as autonomous legal advisers; this is a bounded interpretation task, not a measure of full contract negotiation or practice ([“Terms and Conditions Apply: Evaluating Consumer LLM Reliability in NDA Interpretation”](https://aclanthology.org/2026.nllp-1.20/)). The ABA likewise warns that generative tools may produce inaccurate statements, omissions, or misleading content and requires lawyers to review outputs before relying on them ([ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf)).

Accuracy claims should be weighed by study design. A 2018 vendor-sponsored LawGeex comparison reported 94% average accuracy for its system and 85% for 20 lawyers on issue spotting in five NDAs; its narrow task, small sample, and vendor sponsorship mean it cannot establish that current tools generally outperform lawyers ([Comparing the Performance of Artificial Intelligence to Human Lawyers in the Review of Standard Business Contracts](https://images.law.com/contrib/content/uploads/documents/397/5408/lawgeex.pdf)). A controlled experiment with 206 law students found that AI-generated highlighting reduced completion time by 30% without measured quality loss on the tested task, while AI-generated summaries alone did not change performance metrics; it was not a contract-review study ([Building a Better Lawyer: Experimental Evidence That Artificial Intelligence Can Increase Legal Work Efficiency](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jels.12396)). A separate study of legal-research products found hallucination rates of 17% to 33%, but those figures concern legal research, not contract analysis; they reinforce a broader need for verification without supplying a contract-specific error rate ([Magesh et al., “Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jels.12413)). No cited evidence establishes how often human reviewers catch contract AI errors in ordinary practice, or how much review time offsets the initial time saved.

Privacy and professional responsibility are central because contracts can contain sensitive client, personal, and commercial information. ABA Formal Opinion 512 says lawyers must assess disclosure risks before entering client information into AI tools and examine vendor practices concerning security, retention, ownership, breach notification, and whether information persists after the service ends ([ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf)). The Information Commissioner’s Office advises risk-based data protection, data minimization, security measures, impact assessments where processing is likely to be high-risk, and vendor due diligence; outsourcing the system does not remove the deploying organization’s data-protection responsibilities ([How to Use AI and Personal Data Appropriately and Lawfully](https://ico.org.uk/media/for-organisations/documents/4022261/how-to-use-ai-and-personal-data.pdf)). In practice, safeguards should vary with the matter: a public form contract and a confidential merger agreement should not be treated as equivalent data exposures. Lawyers must also understand tool limits, verify output, supervise staff and vendors, communicate with clients where circumstances require, and charge reasonable fees; the ABA says they may not bill hourly clients for time not actually spent and generally may not bill clients for learning a tool they regularly use ([ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf)). The precise liability allocation for a particular error remains dependent on jurisdiction, professional rules, engagement terms, and vendor contracts; the available guidance does not create a universal rule that a vendor or lawyer alone bears every loss.

Efficiency may expand service capacity, but gains do not necessarily become lower prices or more access. In a legal-aid field study involving 91 professionals, 90% of participants perceived increased productivity and 75% intended to continue using AI after a supported pilot; the study describes promising use for lower-risk tasks such as summaries, first drafts, preliminary research, and translation, but does not establish lasting cost reductions or improved case outcomes ([Generative AI and Legal Aid: Results from a Field Study and 100 Use Cases](https://lawcat.berkeley.edu/record/1291747/files/Generative%20AI%20and%20Legal%20Aid_%20Results%20from%20a%20Field%20Study%20and%20100%20U.pdf)). Clio’s 2025 survey found that among firms using AI widely, 45% had adjusted pricing: 26% raised charges, 11% reduced prices, and 8% introduced AI-specific fees; one in five wide adopters said AI challenged billable-hour targets. These are reported firm practices, not proof of market-wide price effects ([2025 Legal Trends Report, Clio](https://www.clio.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2025-Legal-Trends-Report.pdf)). The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 1.6% growth in legal-services employment from 2023–33 and identifies paralegals and legal assistants as especially exposed to productivity effects from generative AI, while expecting continued lawyer review and work requiring human interaction; the projection is not a causal count of AI-driven job losses ([Incorporating AI Impacts in BLS Employment Projections](https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2025/article/incorporating-ai-impacts-in-bls-employment-projections.htm); [ABA, “Understanding the Impact of AI on the Legal Profession”](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2026-november/understanding-impact-ai-legal-profession/)). Thus, task displacement and work redesign are plausible, especially for routine document work and early-career training, but the scale of job loss, wage effects, and client savings remains uncertain.

## Responsible use requires proportional safeguards and real accountability

Several frameworks support common principles while imposing different obligations. IEEE’s *Ethically Aligned Design* emphasizes human rights, well-being, data agency, effectiveness, transparency, accountability, misuse awareness, and competence; NIST’s voluntary AI Risk Management Framework similarly organizes attention around validity, reliability, safety, security, privacy, fairness, transparency, and accountability ([IEEE, *Ethically Aligned Design*](https://standards.ieee.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/EAD1e-infographic.pdf); [NIST, AI Risk Management Framework FAQs](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework/ai-risk-management-framework-faqs)). These frameworks do not substitute for law. ABA professional duties of competence, confidentiality, communication, supervision, candor, and reasonable fees apply to lawyers’ AI use, though their binding effect depends on jurisdictional adoption; the CCBE likewise identifies confidentiality and competence as central duties for lawyers using generative AI ([ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/); [ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf); [CCBE, *Guide on the Use of Generative AI by Lawyers*](https://www.advokatura.lv/sites/default/files/2025-11/CCBE-guide-on-the-use-of-the-use-of-generative-AI-for-lawyers_2025.pdf)).

The EU AI Act illustrates a risk-based legal approach rather than a blanket rule for all legal AI. It prohibits individual criminal-offence risk assessment based solely on profiling or personality traits, subject to an exception for systems assisting a human assessment grounded in objective, verifiable facts directly linked to criminal activity; specified other law-enforcement assessments may be high-risk ([EUR-Lex, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, consolidated text](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689)). European Commission guidance distinguishes neighborhood crime mapping from assessments of whether a particular person will offend, making system purpose and design important to classification ([European Commission AI Act Service Desk, “Law Enforcement”](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/law-enforcement)). The Act also lists specified judicial-authority uses as high-risk, but ordinary private-law-firm contract review is not expressly listed as a stand-alone category in the cited Annex III provisions; other professional and privacy obligations still apply ([EUR-Lex, AI Act, Annex III](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689)). As of November 21, 2026, the consolidated text says the Regulation generally applies from August 2, 2026, while high-risk Annex III obligations are scheduled to apply from December 2, 2027 ([EUR-Lex, AI Act, Article 113](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/2026-07-27/eng)). For covered high-risk uses, the Act requires data governance, appropriate transparency, logging, effective human oversight, and—in relevant public-sector deployments—a fundamental-rights impact assessment ([EUR-Lex, AI Act, Article 10](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689); [EUR-Lex, AI Act, Article 27](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689); [EUR-Lex, AI Act, Articles 12–14](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689)).

The two contexts require different operational safeguards under that shared baseline. Policing audits should examine not only model error but also where patrols go, whose conduct is observed, how records are generated, and whether outcomes differ across communities. Logs should connect model versions and predictions to patrol or investigative actions and later outcomes; otherwise, agencies cannot establish whether an intervention helped or caused harm. For contract systems, evaluation should use the actual contract types, jurisdictions, languages, and review tasks for which the tool is intended, with particular attention to missed high-risk clauses and unsupported drafting. Review should be proportionate to consequence: locating a clause may need less scrutiny than advice affecting a major transaction. In both settings, a human reviewer must be able to understand limitations, disagree, and document the reasons for doing so; a nominal approval step is not meaningful oversight.

Jurisdiction and institutional power also matter. The United States lacks a comprehensive federal AI regulatory framework in the cited analysis and relies more on existing law, professional rules, and an evolving patchwork; the EU has a horizontal statute with prohibitions and phased obligations ([Congressional Research Service, *Regulating AI: U.S. and International Approaches*](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-06-04_R48555_96020d4144d9ad14646c4590eb43a264faf424ac.pdf); [Skadden, “A Guide to Coping With Divergent State AI Regulations”](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/09/the-informed-board/a-guide-to-coping-with-divergent-state-ai-regulations)). In the United States, *Loomis* illustrates one state court’s handling of proprietary risk assessment, not a uniform national rule ([*State v. Loomis*, Wisconsin Supreme Court opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/wisconsin/supreme-court/2016/2015ap000157-cr.html)). In both jurisdictions, transparency must be practical: affected people need ways to identify errors and seek review, while public agencies and law firms need records sufficient for independent evaluation. A system description alone cannot supply that accountability.

## Conclusion

The comparison shows why a single standard such as “human in the loop” or “high accuracy” is inadequate. Predictive policing uses state power to direct attention and potentially constrain liberty, so its legitimacy depends on demonstrated public-safety value, scrutiny of enforcement-shaped data, and safeguards against disproportionate surveillance. Contract AI can support timely and less costly legal work, but only if confidentiality is protected, outputs are checked against the client’s matter, and productivity gains do not obscure professional responsibility or the distribution of costs to workers and clients.

Institutions should treat deployment as a continuing, reviewable decision rather than a one-time procurement choice. They should define permitted purposes, establish task-specific performance thresholds, disclose material limits, preserve audit trails, provide routes for correction and challenge, and reassess systems when data or practice changes. Where evidence of benefit is thin, errors carry serious consequences, or meaningful oversight cannot be maintained, limiting or declining deployment is itself responsible AI governance.
