# Govern Legal AI with Context-Specific Safeguards

AI can help police allocate attention and lawyers review contracts, but neither efficiency nor predictive accuracy alone makes its use responsible. Predictive policing carries the greater direct risk to civil liberties: its outputs can influence state scrutiny of people and places, while historical policing data can reproduce disparities and feedback loops. Contract-analysis tools more directly put client confidentiality, legal accuracy, and professional accountability at stake; they can also compress routine work, although broad job displacement is not established by the available evidence. Across both contexts, responsible use requires evidence that a tool fits its specific purpose, safeguards for the people and information affected, meaningful human authority, and ways to detect and remedy errors. The safeguards must differ by context: policing demands public accountability and contestability, while legal practice demands rigorous data protection and lawyer verification. Existing laws and professional guidance support these principles, but they do not establish that any single safeguard reliably prevents harm.

## Predictive policing can focus resources but also deepen unequal scrutiny

Predictive-policing systems forecast crime locations or estimate individual risk, but their outputs reflect the data and choices built into their design and deployment. Police records—including calls for service, reported incidents, and arrests—can be incomplete, shaped by past enforcement, and less representative of crimes that are less often reported. As a result, a system may partly predict where police have historically looked, rather than independently measuring where crime occurs. DOJ identifies a further feedback risk: targeted patrols generate more recorded incidents or contacts in selected places or among selected people, and those records can then reinforce future targeting. Removing protected characteristics alone cannot resolve this problem when other variables act as proxies; DOJ, for example, identifies ZIP code as a possible proxy for race. ([DOJ, *Artificial Intelligence and Criminal Justice: Final Report*](https://www.justice.gov/olp/media/1381796/dl))

Evidence shows that focused policing can prevent crime, but it does not establish that every predictive model works, that algorithms are necessary to achieve those benefits, or that benefits and burdens are fairly distributed. A randomized evaluation of an LAPD hotspot model found that model-directed patrols reduced selected property-crime volume by an average of **7.4% per 1,000 minutes of patrol time** compared with analyst-directed patrols; the study covered burglary, car theft, and theft from vehicles, and did not report subgroup outcomes. ([National Institute of Justice, *Program Profile: Predictive Policing Model in Los Angeles, Calif.*](https://crimesolutions.ojp.gov/ratedprograms/predictive-policing-model-los-angeles-calif)) The same profile projected annual savings of about **$17.3 million** under a specified patrol scenario, but this was a model-based projection, not an audited measure of realized savings. ([National Institute of Justice, *Program Profile: Predictive Policing Model in Los Angeles, Calif.*](https://crimesolutions.ojp.gov/ratedprograms/predictive-policing-model-los-angeles-calif)) Separately, a systematic review found robust evidence that focused hotspot policing can reduce crime at targeted places, with no apparent displacement into immediately surrounding areas and some diffusion of benefits nearby; that evidence concerns hotspot policing generally, not AI forecasting specifically. ([Office of Justice Programs, *Hot Spots Policing and Crime Reduction: An Update of an Ongoing Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis*](https://www.ojp.gov/library/publications/hot-spots-policing-and-crime-reduction-update-ongoing-systematic-review-and))

The ethical stakes arise not only from forecast errors, but from what agencies do in response. Predictions may lead to extra patrols, surveillance, or police contact without a substantiated link to criminal activity; hotspot forecasts can concentrate burdens on residents even when no individual is scored. Responses could instead include problem-solving, diversion, or services, so the prediction itself does not determine the intervention. But where additional scrutiny follows, privacy, freedom of movement and association, and due-process concerns arise. The available evidence describes these risks and governing principles, but does not quantify predictive policing’s effects on those liberties across jurisdictions. DOJ emphasizes that criminal-justice AI remains subject to constitutional protections, applicable statutes, procedural and evidentiary rules, discovery obligations, and ethical commitments. ([DOJ, *Artificial Intelligence and Criminal Justice: Final Report*](https://www.justice.gov/olp/media/1381796/dl))

Recidivism-risk tools make disputes about fairness particularly visible. In the COMPAS debate, ProPublica reported different false-positive and false-negative patterns for Black and white defendants in its Broward County analysis, while a peer-reviewed study argued that COMPAS was no more accurate or fair than lay predictions. These findings concern a particular dataset and assessment context; disagreement over fairness measures matters because a tool can perform similarly under one measure yet differently under another. They therefore do not establish that all risk tools have the same bias, or settle which fairness standard should govern every decision. ([ProPublica, *How We Analyzed the COMPAS Recidivism Algorithm*](https://www.propublica.org/article/how-we-analyzed-the-compas-r); [Dressel and Farid, *The Accuracy, Fairness, and Limits of Predicting Recidivism*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5777393/); [DOJ, *Artificial Intelligence and Criminal Justice: Final Report*](https://www.justice.gov/olp/media/1381796/dl)) In *State v. Loomis*, the Wisconsin Supreme Court permitted limited consideration of COMPAS at sentencing, while cautioning that the score must not determine sentence severity or whether a person can be safely supervised in the community; the court also recognized concerns about proprietary methods and limited ability to assess scientific validity. ([*State v. Loomis*, Wisconsin Supreme Court](https://law.justia.com/cases/wisconsin/supreme-court/2016/2015ap000157-cr.html))

These practices can affect public trust as well as individual rights. DOJ warns that feedback loops can increase enforcement in communities already distrustful of law enforcement, further eroding trust. ([DOJ, *Artificial Intelligence and Criminal Justice: Final Report*](https://www.justice.gov/olp/media/1381796/dl)) In a survey experiment with **877 Dutch citizens**, disclosure of external oversight of police predictive-algorithm use increased trust modestly, whereas disclosure of legislation alone did not; the result is context-specific and does not establish long-term effects of real deployments. ([Nieuwenhuizen et al., *Does Institutional Transparency Affect Citizen Trust in Predictive Policing?*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/padm.70034)) A separate experiment with **4,200 U.S. adults** found greater favorability toward AI use by local law enforcement than by the FBI, and reported some subgroup differences, including lower support for predictive policing among Black respondents. ([Schiff et al., *Institutional Factors Driving Citizen Perceptions of AI in Government: Evidence from a Survey Experiment on Policing*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/puar.13754)) Together, these findings suggest that trust depends on who uses a system, who is affected, and whether oversight is visible—not simply on the existence of a policy or a claim of accuracy.

## Contract AI speeds first drafts, but errors and exposure remain material risks

AI-assisted contract analysis can help identify clauses, summarize terms, and generate drafts, but benchmark results show task-specific performance rather than dependable correctness across legal work. The CUAD dataset, built from expert annotations of more than **500 contracts**, **41 clause categories**, and over **13,000 annotations**, found substantial variation by category: one model’s area-under-precision-recall performance ranged from near **100%** on some categories to about **20%** on others. ([CUAD: *An Expert-Annotated NLP Dataset for Legal Contract Review*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)) A later benchmark comparing **19** proprietary and open-source models reported that proprietary models generally performed better, while open-source systems more often missed a relevant clause by saying none existed; quantization could also reduce performance. These are evaluations on a public dataset, not audits of routine commercial work. ([ContractEval: *Benchmarking LLMs for Clause-Level Legal Risk Identification in Commercial Contracts*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03080))

Drafting benchmarks likewise show both speed and substantive risk. An evaluation of **450 outputs** from **13 AI tools** across **30 drafting tasks** reported an overall human-lawyer reliability rate of **56.7%** and an AI average of **57.3%**; the best AI result was **73.3%**. The benchmark recorded inaccurate company details, omitted requested terms, and unnecessary concessions favoring the counterparty. ([Benchmarking Humans & AI in Contract Workflows](https://www.legalbenchmarks.ai/research/phase-2-research)) The same evaluation found that AI produced drafts in under **one minute per task** on average, compared with **12 minutes 43 seconds** for human lawyers, but measured draft generation rather than the full time needed for review, correction, negotiation, and finalization. Its limited task set and single-pass design also constrain generalization. ([Benchmarking Humans & AI in Contract Workflows](https://www.legalbenchmarks.ai/research/phase-2-research)) The results support AI as a potential accelerator of first drafts and repetitive review, not as a substitute for a lawyer who can assess context, commercial judgment, and whether the draft preserves the client’s position. ABA guidance similarly warns that generative AI can produce inaccurate or fabricated content and requires independent verification proportionate to the task. ([ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf))

Confidentiality is a distinct professional concern because contracts may contain sensitive personal, commercial, and strategic information. ABA Formal Opinion 512 advises lawyers to assess disclosure and unauthorized-access risks in light of the information’s sensitivity, the tool’s safeguards, and its terms. It highlights vendor diligence on retention and deletion, access practices, model reuse, breach notification, liability limits, and security. For self-learning tools that could disclose representation-related information, the opinion says informed client consent is required under its analysis; boilerplate engagement-letter language alone is insufficient. The opinion applies the ABA Model Rules and is not itself a statute or a binding rule in every jurisdiction. ([ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf)) A 2025 benchmark identifies confidentiality concerns as one reason legal teams may prefer local deployment, but also reports performance trade-offs, including missed clauses in some open-source models. Local deployment can reduce some third-party exposure; it does not, by itself, establish security or accuracy. ([ContractEval: *Benchmarking LLMs for Clause-Level Legal Risk Identification in Commercial Contracts*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03080))

Professional accountability remains with lawyers and firms. ABA Formal Opinion 512 applies duties of competence, confidentiality, client communication, supervision, candor, and reasonable fees to AI-assisted work: a lawyer must understand relevant benefits and risks, verify output, and retain professional judgment. The opinion calls for firm policies and staff training, and for client communication when required by client instructions, engagement terms, informed-consent obligations, material effects on a significant decision, or the basis of a fee. It also says hourly-billed lawyers must bill actual time rather than the time a tool might have taken to perform the work manually. ([ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf)) Vendor contracts may create separate obligations, but they do not transfer the lawyer’s professional responsibility for advice and work product.

The economic effects are plausible but not yet settled. CUAD’s authors identify the time and cost of contract review as barriers that can deter individuals and small businesses from obtaining it; that identifies a potential access benefit, not evidence that AI deployment has delivered one. ([CUAD: *An Expert-Annotated NLP Dataset for Legal Contract Review*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)) A **2026** Relativity survey reported that **82.8%** of respondents said AI had saved their organization money, but the result is self-reported and not a causal estimate specific to contract work. ([AI in Legal: 2026 Research Data](https://www.relativity.com/news-events/most-legal-professionals-say-ai-has-not-yet-changed-hiring-according-to-a-relativity-study/)) Meanwhile, the survey reported no impact on hiring for **57.9%** of respondents, increased hiring for **24.2%**, and decreased hiring for **9.2%**; these figures span legal work broadly and do not isolate contract-related roles. ([AI in Legal: 2026 Research Data](https://www.relativity.com/news-events/most-legal-professionals-say-ai-has-not-yet-changed-hiring-according-to-a-relativity-study/)) A separate **2025** professional-services survey found that fewer than **20%** of surveyed legal professionals saw GenAI as a major threat to their own jobs; those findings are perceptions, not observed employment effects. ([2025 Generative AI in Professional Services Report](https://legal.thomsonreuters.com/content/dam/ewp-m/documents/thomsonreuters/en/pdf/reports/2025-generative-ai-in-professional-services-report-tr5433489-rgb.pdf)) Thus, the evidence supports faster task completion and reported organizational savings, but not a conclusion that AI has caused broad legal-sector job loss. It does leave open the risk that reduced demand for routine junior work could weaken opportunities to develop contract-reading and drafting judgment.

## The cases share governance needs but demand different protections

Both applications can fail through poor data, inaccurate outputs, opacity, over-reliance, privacy risks, and unclear responsibility. The difference is who is exposed and how: predictive policing can direct state attention toward people and communities that cannot readily opt out, while contract AI primarily risks exposing client information and degrading the quality of legal service. The EU AI Act reflects the public-authority stakes by classifying specified law-enforcement uses as high-risk and prohibiting certain individual crime-risk assessments based solely on profiling or personality traits. Its law-enforcement guidance distinguishes location-based predictions from person-based ones, while recognizing that a location score used to profile an individual may be treated as person-based. ([EU AI Act, Annex III](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689); [European Commission law-enforcement guidance](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/law-enforcement); [Guidelines on prohibited AI practices](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-01/guide-prohibited_en.pdf)) Contract analysis does not automatically fall into a high-risk category merely because lawyers use it; under the Act, intended purpose and specified legal categories determine classification. ([AI Act, Article 6](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689))

The frameworks offer complementary but differently binding guidance. The EU AI Act imposes obligations on covered systems and actors; the Act’s consolidated text states a general application date of **August 2, 2026**, with Annex III high-risk requirements applying from **December 2, 2027**, while specified prohibitions apply earlier. ([Consolidated EU AI Act, Articles 111 and 113](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689)) For covered high-risk systems, the Act addresses data governance and bias testing, documentation, transparency to deployers, qualified human oversight, monitoring, and logging; Article 27 requires certain deployers to assess fundamental-rights impacts, affected groups, mitigation, and complaint arrangements. ([AI Act, Articles 10, 13, 14, and 26](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689); [AI Act, Article 27](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-27)) Article 86 provides an explanation right for qualifying affected people when specified high-risk AI outputs inform decisions with legal or similarly significant effects, subject to its scope and exceptions. ([AI Act, Article 86](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-86)) GDPR Article 22 separately addresses certain solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects; its application depends on the processing and legal context, not merely the presence of AI. ([GDPR, Article 22](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/ALL/?uri=celex%3A32016R0679))

IEEE *Ethically Aligned Design* articulates voluntary principles including human rights, well-being, data agency, transparency, accountability, and competence. NIST’s voluntary AI Risk Management Framework organizes lifecycle governance around **Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage**, emphasizing context, testing, documentation, monitoring, privacy, fairness, and affected communities. ([IEEE, *Ethically Aligned Design*](https://standards.ieee.org/wp-content/uploads/import/documents/other/ead1e.pdf); [NIST AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/)) ABA Formal Opinion 512 applies existing professional obligations to lawyers’ use of generative AI; its force depends on the jurisdiction’s rules and adoption, but its central point is that professional responsibility does not move to the tool. ([ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf)) Algorithm-audit research cautions that narrowly technical tests can miss the interests of affected stakeholders and broader social effects, and treats auditing methods and governance as an active research area. ([Brown, Davidovic, and Hasan, “The algorithm audit: Scoring the algorithms that score us”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2053951720983865))

In practice, governance should follow the risk of the use rather than rely on generic claims of accuracy or “human oversight.” For predictive policing, agencies should define a legitimate public-safety objective, examine data provenance and local validity, test errors across relevant groups and locations, monitor downstream police actions and feedback effects, and make the system’s purpose and limits accessible to the public. Independent evaluation, community participation, documentation, and a usable route to challenge consequential errors are especially important because a prediction can influence state treatment even if it does not itself authorize an intervention. DOJ recommends local validation, careful input selection, public notice, independent evaluation, and meaningful participation; it also cautions that complex models may not justify their loss of interpretability if they offer only marginal accuracy gains. ([DOJ, *Artificial Intelligence and Criminal Justice: Final Report*](https://www.justice.gov/olp/media/1381796/dl)) For contract AI, firms should assess vendor access, retention, reuse, security, and model-training terms; validate performance on representative contracts and clause types; and require competent lawyer review before relying on material outputs. The ABA guidance supports those controls, while contract benchmarks show why evaluation must check omissions, unsupported additions, factual accuracy, and whether a draft preserves the client’s position. ([ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf); [CUAD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268); [ContractEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03080); [Benchmarking Humans & AI in Contract Workflows](https://www.legalbenchmarks.ai/research/phase-2-research))

Human oversight is meaningful only when the reviewer has the competence, information, time, and authority to question an output, correct errors, and reject its recommendation. Transparency, too, must serve a purpose: public model documentation does not replace notice to affected people or a practical way to contest a consequential decision, and confidentiality constraints may limit what can responsibly be disclosed. NIST treats trade-offs among trustworthiness characteristics as matters to measure and manage, while audit scholarship warns against reducing accountability to technical metrics alone. ([NIST AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/); [Brown, Davidovic, and Hasan, “The algorithm audit”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2053951720983865)) In policing, efficiency can conflict with equal treatment and civil liberties; in contract work, richer data may improve context while increasing confidentiality exposure, and faster generation may still leave substantial verification work. These trade-offs should be documented, including who benefits, who bears residual risk, and what conditions should trigger review or suspension.

## Conclusion

Responsible AI in legal settings is not a property a vendor can promise or an accuracy score can establish. It depends on institutions making the system’s purpose, limits, evidence, and accountable decision-makers clear—and then monitoring whether the system’s real-world effects match its justification. The more directly a tool can shape state power over people, the stronger the case for public scrutiny, community participation, and meaningful challenge.

In legal practice, efficiency gains are valuable only if confidentiality, competent review, and professional accountability remain intact. In both settings, human involvement should mean more than a final sign-off: reviewers need the ability and authority to question outputs, and affected people need routes to correct consequential errors. Existing frameworks provide principles and duties for building those arrangements, but evidence that compliance alone ensures fair outcomes or public trust remains limited.
