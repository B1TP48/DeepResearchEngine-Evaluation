# Three Revolutions Show How States Break

The three revolutions followed a common sequence, though not a single cause. Fiscal or economic stress weakened the old state, mobilising networks supplied organisation, and the decisive break came when coercive institutions stopped defending the regime. In France the king accepted the National Assembly and the Bastille fell, in Russia the Petrograd garrison refused to fire in February 1917, and in Iran the armed forces declared neutrality on 11 February 1979 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/French-Revolution/Events-of-1789); [Outline of the Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outline_of_the_Russian_Revolution); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Iranian-Revolution)). The preconditions differed: French bread prices rose about 60 per cent between 1726 and 1789 ([Access to History](https://jcoss.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Access-to-History_France_in_Revolution_sample-chapter.pdf)), Russia's wartime inflation reached about 5.8 per cent a month in 1916 ([Harrison, Warwick working paper](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/staff/mharrison/public/rgwr_postprint.pdf)), and Iran's oil boom produced fast growth alongside inequality and urban grievance ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/1979-iran-and-america/)). Repression and long-run outcomes also diverged sharply, from France's Civil Code, which outlasted the revolution, to Iran's 1979 constitution, which remains in force. Current risk assessments flag Iran for revolutionary-process indicators, but no source gives a formal probability, and fragility indices do not measure revolution directly.

## Defining the terms and fixing the dates

A "revolution" in this comparison means a rapid transfer of state power accompanied by mass mobilisation. Skocpol's narrower term, the "social revolution," requires that the old state's administrative and military power break down at the same time as a peasant revolt, and her framework is built around that conjunction ([Wikipedia, States and Social Revolutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_and_Social_Revolutions)). "Fragility" in the cross-national datasets means state capacity and cohesion. The Fragile States Index uses four categories, Cohesion, Economic, Political and Social, to assess vulnerability to conflict or collapse ([World Population Review](https://worldpopulationreview.com/country-rankings/fragile-states-index)).

The dates are accurate for the decisive events but incomplete as process descriptions. The French Revolution is usually dated from the Estates-General of 1789 and runs to Brumaire in 1799 ([Wikipedia, French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution)). Russia's "1917" covers two revolutions: the February revolution fell on 8 March in Gregorian terms because Russia used the Julian calendar until February 1918, and the October revolution fell in November ([History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/russian-revolution); [Wikipedia, October Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_Revolution)). The Iranian date is only partly right: the Shah left on 16 January 1979 and the monarchy fell on 11 February 1979 ([Wikipedia, Khomeini's return](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruhollah_Khomeini's_return_to_Iran)), but Wikipedia dates the revolution from 7 January 1978, while Nonviolent Conflict dates open resistance to 1977 ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution); [Nonviolent Conflict](https://www.nonviolent-conflict.org/iranian-revolution-1977-1979/)). The sources do not settle the start date, and this matters for any claim about how quickly the Iranian state weakened.

## Socioeconomic preconditions: fiscal strain in France and Russia, oil disruption in Iran

The French case combined state debt with a regressive tax burden. Swansea's study guide reports that the Church paid a *don gratuit* instead of direct taxation, so the burden fell more heavily on peasants, and it puts seigneurial dues, tithe and taxes at between a third and a half of a peasant's income ([Swansea University](https://www.swansea.ac.uk/history/history-study-guides/the-long-and-short-reasons-for-why-revolution-broke-out-in-france-in-1789/)). Several sources call the financial crisis the main short-term cause, with bad harvests and bread prices as contributing factors ([Access to History](https://jcoss.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Access-to-History_France_in_Revolution_sample-chapter.pdf)). The sources disagree on relative weight, and no source measures it. The 60 per cent wheat rise is a long-run figure and cannot date a sudden crisis, and no year-by-year Paris bread-price series for 1780–1789 was found.

Russia's pre-war economy was growing fast but unevenly. Industrial output quadrupled between 1890 and 1913 but did not reach most workers and peasants ([History Tools](https://www.historytools.org/stories/the-key-causes-of-the-russian-revolution-a-historians-perspective)). The war then changed the picture. Harrison reports that average incomes in 1917 were still no more than about 20 per cent below pre-war levels, and that about 9.7 million people had fled or been displaced by 1917 ([Harrison](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/staff/mharrison/public/rgwr_postprint.pdf)). The sources conflict on food. History Tools says grain production was 40 per cent below pre-war levels by 1917, while AskFoodie argues the empire still produced enough food and that transport and hyperinflation caused the shortages ([History Tools](https://www.historytools.org/stories/the-key-causes-of-the-russian-revolution-a-historians-perspective); [AskFoodie](https://askfoodie.blog/why-food-shortages-russian-revolution)). AskFoodie is a blog, so the production figure remains disputed.

Iran's preconditions were different in kind. Oil prices quadrupled in 1973 ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/1979-iran-and-america/)), and Salehi-Isfahani argues that the poor's share of the population fell to near a quarter during the first boom while resources flowed faster to privileged urban neighbourhoods ([Brookings, Salehi-Isfahani](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/irans-economy-40-years-after-the-islamic-revolution/)). An ERF analysis estimates a rising Gini before the revolution ([Farzanegan and Markwardt](https://erf.org.eg/app/uploads/2018/09/1226.pdf)). Parstimes claims the bazaar did not benefit from the 1974–78 boom, but this is a secondary site and the claim is not corroborated elsewhere ([Parstimes](https://www.parstimes.com/history/economy.html)). The evidence supports a "boom-induced disruption" reading more firmly than immiseration. Aggregate and urban incomes rose, but the gains were distributed unevenly and the rentier state did not need to tax society.

Mobilised groups also differed. In France, bread protests and the 5 October 1789 march targeted prices ([History Skills](https://www.historyskills.com/classroom/modern-history/mod-french-revolution-reading/)), while the Third Estate's cahiers show a more fundamental political challenge than the nobles' ([Cambridge, "Peasants Protest"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/comparative-studies-in-society-and-history/article/abs/peasants-protest-the-claims-of-lord-church-and-state-in-the-cahiers-de-doleances-of-1789/D9992960073BD418B55344989005CEC0)). In Iran, the Qom seminary and the bazaar closed their doors in January 1978 ([Swarthmore](https://nvdatabase.swarthmore.edu/content/iranians-overthrow-shah-1977-79)), and the urban poor and working class joined in June 1978, when the movement had been upper-middle-class ([Swarthmore](https://nvdatabase.swarthmore.edu/content/iranians-overthrow-shah-1977-79)). Peasant land hunger drove the Russian countryside, where a Partisan account reports peasants deserting the army in 1917 to seize gentry land ([Partisan Magazine](https://partisanmagazine.org/2026/02/17/russian-peasants-and-the-agrarian-revolution/)).

## Leadership, ideology and the counter-revolution

The ideological repertoires were programmatic rather than shared. The French Declaration drew on Enlightenment natural-rights thought and the American example ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/2012/declaration-of-the-rights-of-man-and-of-the-citize/)), though an older Heidelberg study disputes that Rousseau's *Social Contract* was its direct source ([OLL](https://oll-resources.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/oll3/store/titles/1176/0162_Bk.pdf)). Lenin's April Theses called for a republic of soviets and refused support to the Provisional Government ([John D. Clare](https://www.johndclare.net/Russ4_AT.htm)). The origin of "All power to the soviets" is disputed: International Socialism argues Lenin discarded older Bolshevik thinking ([International Socialism](https://isj.org.uk/lenins-april-theses-and-the-russian-revolution/)), while John Riddell says the slogan was not adopted as a party slogan until early May ([Riddell](https://johnriddell.com/2017/03/23/all-power-to-the-soviets-part-1-biography-of-a-slogan/)). Khomeini's doctrine of guardianship of the jurist came from 19 lectures in Najaf in January and February 1970 ([Wikipedia, Islamic Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Government)). It was made public only after his supporters had consolidated power, and he made no mention of it through the rest of the 1970s ([Wikipedia, Ideology of the Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology_of_the_Iranian_Revolution)). The mobilising vocabulary was therefore broader than the doctrine later written into the constitution.

Leadership structures followed the same logic of bodies and seized institutions. In France, the Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly on 17 June 1789 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/French-Revolution/Events-of-1789)). In Russia, the Provisional Government and the Petrograd Soviet shared power in a "dual power" arrangement ([Wikipedia, The Dual Power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dual_Power)), and by autumn 1917 the Bolsheviks held up to 90 per cent of the Petrograd Soviet seats ([Wikipedia, Second All-Russian Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_All-Russian_Congress_of_Soviets_of_Workers%27_and_Soldiers%27_Deputies)). Figes argues that the Petrograd-centred account overstates central control, since the provinces were largely outside either body's power ([1914–1918-Online](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/article/dual-power/)). In Iran, Khomeini formed a Revolutionary Council in Paris on 12 January 1979 ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/blog/order-from-chaos/2019/01/24/the-iranian-revolution-a-timeline-of-events/amp/)) and appointed Bazargan prime minister on 4 February ([Wikipedia, Mehdi Bazargan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehdi_Bazargan)). Air force officers pledged loyalty to Khomeini on 5 February ([Wikipedia, Khomeini's return](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruhollah_Khomeini%27s_return_to_Iran)).

Counter-revolution was most organised in France and Russia. The Vendée uprising began in March 1793 ([Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/reign-of-terror.html)), and the sources give casualty figures that cannot be reconciled: about 200,000 deaths ([Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/reign-of-terror.html)), 450,000 deaths from 1793 to 1796 ([Historica](https://historica.fandom.com/wiki/Reign_of_Terror)), and 40,000–50,000 civilians killed ([Positive Liberty](https://www.positiveliberty.com/the-reign-of-terror-explained/)). In Russia, the Whites were backed by foreign forces, and EBSCO notes that the White leaders failed to frame their nationalism in terms that would win non-Russian peoples ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/russian-civil-war-1918-1921)). The strength of White armies is not given in the sources. In Iran, the decisive counter-revolutionary force was the army, and its neutrality rather than battlefield defeat appears to have ended the monarchy ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Iranian-Revolution)). This is an inference from the sources, not a stated finding.

Foreign involvement differed by case. Russia faced direct intervention: Britain, France and the United States landed troops at Murmansk and Archangelsk in March 1918 ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/russian-civil-war-1918-1921)), and thirteen foreign states intervened against the Red Army ([Wikipedia, Russian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Civil_War)). France faced invading coalition armies after April 1792 ([SAHO](https://sahistory.org.za/article/french-revolution)). Iran's foreign role was mainly diplomatic and historical: a CIA-sponsored coup had returned the Shah in 1953 ([Nonviolent Conflict](https://www.nonviolent-conflict.org/iranian-revolution-1977-1979/)), and Iranian students seized the US embassy in November 1979 ([Lowy Institute](https://www.lowyinstitute.org/the-interpreter/foreign-policy-jimmy-carter)). Brzezinski's view that the revolution was partly Soviet-orchestrated is recorded by Iranica, which notes it differed from official US policy ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/carter-administration-1977-81-policy-toward-persia/)).

Repression figures show how far each new government went, and they are the most disputed numbers in the notes. The table below sets out the main estimates.

The UN Special Rapporteur's preliminary view that the 1981 and 1988 killings had genocidal intent is not a court finding ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_executions_of_Iranian_political_prisoners)).

## Institutions and long-term outcomes diverged

France's lasting changes were civil and property law. The Assembly's decrees abolished feudal rights, and the Napoleonic Civil Code of 1804 is still in force, though heavily amended ([Wikipedia, Napoleonic Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Code)). The Code also restricted women's rights, which one source describes as a regression in favour of patriarchal control ([The Verdict](https://www.theverdictonline.com/post/the-napoleonic-code-france-s-quiet-revolution)). Land changed the most. Church and émigré land sales affected about 10 per cent of French farmland, and the OFCE working paper argues that the benefits went to urban owners, manufacturers and some peasants ([OFCE Working Paper 06/2022](https://www.ofce.sciences-po.fr/pdf/dtravail/OFCEWP2022-06.pdf)). The peasant share of land in the Nord department rose from 30 per cent in 1789 to 42 per cent in 1802, though this is a single-department series ([Napoleon Series](https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/abstract/economics/commerce/France/c_land.html)).

French economic outcomes are read as a pause rather than a boom. The OFCE paper finds that growth decelerated between 1789 and 1815 and that per-capita GDP reached 75 per cent of the British level around 1789 and matched it only around 1900 ([OFCE Working Paper 06/2022](https://www.ofce.sciences-po.fr/pdf/dtravail/OFCEWP2022-06.pdf)). Trade openness fell from 20 per cent of GDP in 1789 to 10 per cent by 1820 ([OFCE Working Paper 06/2022](https://www.ofce.sciences-po.fr/pdf/dtravail/OFCEWP2022-06.pdf)). The debt picture conflicts: the OFCE paper shows total debt falling from more than 3 billion francs in 1789 to 695 million francs in 1815, while a JMSS article gives public debt rising from 160 per cent of GNP in 1795 to 341 per cent in 1815 ([JMSS](https://jmss.org/article/download/58328/pdf_1/159033)). The two measure different things, and I give the OFCE picture more weight because it is a specialist economic-history study.

Russia's institutions moved by decree and then partly reversed. The 1917 Decree on Land abolished private landownership and barred sale, purchase, leasing or mortgage of land ([Marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/history/ussr/government/1917/firstdecreesofsovietpower.pdf)). The 1918 Family Code gave women the right to divorce without consent and to abortion without the father's consent ([Soviet History MSU](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/new-woman/new-woman-texts/code-of-laws-concerning-the-civil-registration-of-deaths-births-and-marriages/)), but it was succeeded by a more conservative 1926 code ([Wikipedia, Family in the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_in_the_Soviet_Union)). A student-level paper claims a 1944 decree reversed protections for single mothers ([Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/124602715/SOVIET_FAMILY_LAW_WOMEN_AND_CHILD_CARE_FROM_1917_TO_THE_1940s_)), but no primary text was retrieved to confirm it. On growth, estimates for Soviet industrialisation are high: average growth was 5.1 per cent (Maddison), 5.9 per cent (Harrison) and 6.3 per cent (Allen) over 1928–1940 ([Meliantsev](https://www.sociostudies.org/journal/articles/140501/)). Meliantsev argues this record was "far from the best" once investment and military spending are counted, and that the Soviet per-capita GDP relative to advanced countries fell from 28–30 per cent in 1913 to 16–18 per cent in 1990 ([Meliantsev](https://www.sociostudies.org/journal/articles/140501/)). Maddison's data show national income per head falling by more than three-fifths during 1913–1921 ([Maddison Project](https://www.rug.nl/ggdc/historicaldevelopment/maddison/publications/wp2.pdf)).

Russian inequality compressed sharply after 1917. The NBER series shows compression strongest during 1905–1925 and inequality rising again from 1925 to 1956 ([NBER, w23712](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w23712/w23712.pdf)). Pre-revolutionary land inequality was high, with a land Gini of about 0.60 for 1909, while the overall income Gini was about 0.36 ([NBER, w18383](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w18383.pdf)).

Iran's institutional outcome is the most durable of the three in its formal architecture. The 1979 constitution combines elected bodies with unelected ones, including the Supreme Leader and the Guardian Council, and was last amended on 28 July 1989 ([Wikipedia, Constitution of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Iran)). The 1989 amendment was approved by 97.6 per cent of voters ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1989_Iranian_constitutional_referendum)), and it lowered the Supreme Leader's religious qualification and allowed Khamenei to succeed Khomeini ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/constitution-of-the-islamic-republic/)). Family law changed fast: the Family Protection Act was abrogated immediately after 1979, though modified divorce provisions were adopted on 23 September 1979 ([Wikipedia, Family Protection Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran's_Family_Protection_Law)). The 2024 hijab law was approved by the Guardian Council, but a CNN report from August 2026 says its implementation was halted after the Supreme National Security Council intervened, and that Article 638 still applies ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/22/middleeast/iran-hijab-women-defy-hardliners-intl)). Female literacy rose sharply: USIP reports it had almost quadrupled since 1980 and was within ten points of male literacy in 2015 ([USIP](https://iranprimer.usip.org/blog/2016/may/25/iran-numbers-women)). The UNESCO adult female literacy projection for 2022 is 85 per cent, while a Tehran Times figure for 2020 gives 85.5 per cent for women ([Snopes](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/iran-female-literacy-rate/); [Tehran Times](https://www.tehrantimes.com/news/486261/Iran-witnessed-increase-in-number-of-literate-educated-women)).

Land policy after 1979 was partly reversed. A 1980 law legalised distribution of more than 800,000 hectares to farmers who had seized land ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, Farming](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/farming/)), but on 12 July 1984 the Guardian Council declared the land reform laws contrary to Islamic law and the process was halted ([IntechOpen](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/83934)). Iranica reports land-concentration Gini values of 0.55, 0.61 and 0.62 across three periods, which it reads as rising concentration after the reform ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, Farming](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/farming/)). Harris and Kalb and the Irannamag analysis read the Pahlavi-era outcomes differently, and the sources do not settle which account is right ([Harris & Kalb](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0b91f8zr); [Irannamag](https://www.irannamag.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/5.4E1-Irannamag-Winter2021-hk99-11-27-2.pdf)).

Iran's economic position is now the sharpest near-term indicator. World Bank data give GDP per capita of $3,924.4 in current US dollars for 2025 and GDP growth of −2.8 per cent ([World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org/country/ir)), though Wikipedia gives −0.3 per cent for 2025, so the figures conflict ([Wikipedia, Economy of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Iran)). Inflation exceeded 50 per cent in 2025 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/23/iran-economy-war-charts-rial-oil-strait-hormuz-blockade.html)). The IMF's April 2026 outlook projected a 6.1 per cent contraction and 68.9 per cent inflation ([FDD](https://www.fdd.org/analysis/2026/06/26/irans-economic-catastrophe/)), while a later revision to −5.4 per cent is reported second-hand ([UK FactCheck](https://www.ukfactcheck.com/fact-checker/293/iran-economy-5-charts-show-war-s-painful-impact-on-inflation-jobs-growth)). The primary IMF page was not retrieved. The World Bank income Gini was 35.9 in 2023 ([The Global Economy](https://www.theglobaleconomy.com/Iran/gini_inequality_index/)), which suggests moderate income inequality, but no pre-1979 series exists for comparison.

## Theory and future scenarios

Skocpol's structural framework explains France and Russia best. Her argument holds that revolutions emerge from political crises centred on old-regime state structures, combined with international forces and class relations ([Penn State Libraries](https://catalog.libraries.psu.edu/catalog/34839190); [Academia.edu, Skocpol Summary](https://www.academia.edu/15266288/Skocpol_Summary)). It fits the French fiscal breakdown and the Russian war-driven collapse of administration. It fits Iran less well, because Iran was not among her cases and the mobilisation was urban and religious rather than a peasant revolt. Her rentier-state concept adds a variant: oil revenue let the Shah provide welfare without taxing society, so the state's support depended on continued revenue ([Sozialpolitik.ch](https://www.sozialpolitik.ch/article/download/3695/3351/5586)). Kurzman argues that belief that revolution was possible was decisive, while Abrahamian's class account sees the economic crisis and international pressure as minor blows ([ResearchGate, Kurzman](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/37713332_The_Unthinkable_Revolution_in_Iran); [Sozialpolitik.ch](https://www.sozialpolitik.ch/article/download/3695/3351/5586)). Skocpol's own later account admits that Iran challenged her theory of social revolutions ([Kadivar](https://www.mohammadalikadivar.com/uploads/8/6/9/1/86910052/kadivar_2022_socdev.pdf)).

A usable synthesis for the three cases has three layers. The first is structural: fiscal or revenue crisis weakens the state's capacity to tax, pay or feed its population, as in France in 1787–89, Russia in 1916–17, and Iran's oil-revenue dependence before 1978. The second is mobilising: networks such as the Jacobin clubs, the soviets, or the Qom and bazaar networks give protest organisation and sustained coordination. The third is decisive: revolution succeeds when coercive institutions defect or stay neutral. The Bastille and the Petrograd garrison show this, and so does Iran's army in February 1979. The framework predicts that mass protest alone does not produce regime change while security forces remain loyal. This is consistent with all three cases in the sources, although no source tests it quantitatively.

Applied to the present, the framework points to different risks in Iran and Russia. In Iran, the November 2026 sources describe the regime deploying security forces to Tehran and Esfahan on 14 and 16 November, before protests there ([Critical Threats](https://www.criticalthreats.org/analysis/iran-update-november-17-2026)). They also report at least 1,000 protesters in Abdanan after a bus crash ([Critical Threats](https://www.criticalthreats.org/analysis/iran-update-november-17-2026)). The Iranian Axis review reports a US naval blockade since July 2026 and fuel shortages that triggered protests by mid-November, and a rift between political leadership and the IRGC ([Iranian Axis](https://israel-alma.org/the-iranian-axis-monthly-review-october-1-november-15-2026/)). These are the structural and mobilising signals of the framework. The decisive variable, security-force cohesion, is not reported in any source. The Wikipedia entry on the 2026 leadership crisis says Ali Khamenei was assassinated after strikes beginning 28 February 2026, but it names no successor and is crowd-edited ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Iranian_leadership_crisis)). The Iranian information base is also dominated by opposition and advocacy outlets, such as NCRI, which claims that repressive units have been placed on alert ([NCRI](https://www.ncr-iran.org/en/ncri-statements/statement-iran-protest/irans-regime-intensifies-crackdown-to-prevent-sparks-of-uprising/)).

In Russia, the framework points toward succession rather than mass revolution. The Duma elections of 18–20 September 2026 are described as a tool for managing elite competition and succession ([Senate Square](https://senatesquare.org/publications/russia-2026-elections)). The pre-vote incumbent figure for United Russia is 310 seats ([Wikipedia, 2026 Russian legislative election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Russian_legislative_election)), while the State Duma page lists 350 seats after the vote ([Wikipedia, State Duma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Duma)). The post-election figure is the more recent and is the one to use, though it is not independently verified in the notes. The SWP commentary argues that the regime is undermining itself while at war, but it is an analytical opinion rather than a measured risk score ([SWP Berlin](https://www.swp-berlin.org/en/publication/yet-another-sham-election-in-russia)).

Forecasting needs caution. Official Western assessments in 1977–78 underestimated the risk to the Shah. The State Department's January 1977 report expected stability to the mid-1980s ([National Security Archive](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/18190-national-security-archive-doc-01-state)), and a 1978 CIA assessment said Iran was not in a pre-revolutionary situation ([Spirling](https://arthurspirling.org/documents/iran.pdf)). The more pessimistic INR analysis and Theodore Moran's 1977 memo, which warned of rising social and economic tensions, were closer to the outcome ([Marine Corps University Press](https://www.usmcu.edu/Outreach/Marine-Corps-University-Press/Expeditions-with-MCUP-digital-journal/Policy-Perception-and-Misperception/); [Wikipedia, Theodore Moran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Moran)). The lesson is that economic-stress indicators outperformed confidence in the leadership. Current fragility rankings are weaker guides. The Fragile States Index measures capacity and cohesion ([World Population Review](https://worldpopulationreview.com/country-rankings/fragile-states-index)), so a high-capacity autocracy could look stable until it fails. The IMF's FY26 list, dated July 2025, does not include Iran ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/topics/fragile-conflict-affected-states/imf-fcs-list-fy26-11jul2025.pdf)). A 2026 newsletter names Libya, Yemen, Somalia, South Sudan and Haiti as at highest risk of collapse ([Substack](https://johnwalter25.substack.com/p/15-countries-highest-risk-that-collapse)), but it is an unnamed-author source about state failure rather than revolution.

## Conclusion

The three revolutions are best read as failures of state capacity that were converted into regime change by the behaviour of armed institutions. The preconditions differed in kind, from France's fiscal and food crisis to Russia's war-driven collapse and Iran's oil-boom disruption, but each depended on the loyalty of the coercive apparatus at the decisive moment. For forecasting, this suggests that the most useful indicators are fiscal and fuel stress, elite splits and security deployments, together with the cohesion of the security forces. Iran shows the first three in November 2026, but the sources do not show whether its army is divided, which is the variable that the historical cases suggest matters most.

The long-term outcomes also show that revolution does not settle its own legacy. France's civil-law changes outlasted its politics, Russia's land and family decrees were partly reversed within a generation, and Iran's constitutional architecture has lasted nearly fifty years with one amendment. Repression figures remain uncertain across all three cases, and the estimates differ by orders of magnitude. Any claim about revolutionary risk should therefore be stated as a probability with its sources, and it should be treated with the same caution the historical record demands.
