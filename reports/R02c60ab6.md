# How Revolutions Break States and Rebuild

A revolution is more than a protest or a change of government: here, it means extra-constitutional mass mobilization that displaces a regime and substantially remakes core political institutions; regime collapse, civil war, and electoral turnover do not by themselves meet that definition ([“Security force disloyalty in nonviolent and violent campaigns, 1970–2013”](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopres/xjaf020); [“Sparks and Prairie Fires: A Theory of Unanticipated Political Revolution”](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/preference-falsification/1989-kuran.pdf)). The familiar dates—France in 1789, Russia in 1917, and Iran in 1979—mark beginnings or decisive regime changes, not complete revolutionary processes: useful core periods are France, 1789–1799; Russia’s February and October upheavals in 1917; and Iran’s mass mobilization from 1978 to the monarchy’s fall in 1979, with some accounts extending its transition into 1980 ([Larousse, “Révolution française 1789–1799”](https://www.larousse.fr/encyclopedie/divers/R%C3%A9volution_fran%C3%A7aise/140733); [Oxford Bibliographies, “Russian Revolutions and Civil War, 1917–1921”](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62360/reference-article/554546917); [U.S. National Archives, “444 Days: Selected Records Concerning the Iran Hostage Crisis 1979–1981”](https://www.archives.gov/research/foreign-policy/iran-hostage-crisis); [Nicholas M. Nikazmerad, “A Chronological Survey of the Iranian Revolution”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/iranian-studies/article/abs/chronological-survey-of-the-iranian-revolution/F8C808E53F9F6DBE3D951DF1FB33A5CF)). The Russian labels “February” and “October” follow the Julian calendar then in use; the same upheavals fell in March and November on the Gregorian calendar ([Oxford Bibliographies, “Russian Revolutions and Civil War, 1917–1921”](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62360/reference-article/554546917)). Across the cases, the clearest common pattern is that grievances and state weakness opened opportunities, while organization, elite and security-force behavior, and the ability to claim authority shaped who prevailed. Their outcomes were not equivalent: each built new institutions, but through different mixes of war, coercion, exclusion, and social change. Evidence is stronger on these sequences and institutional changes than on directly comparable measures of inequality, casualties, or long-term welfare.

## State crises opened the way, but organization shaped who prevailed

The socioeconomic pressures differed, and none alone explains revolutionary timing. In France, fiscal crisis, unequal social burdens, political exclusion, and the poor 1788 harvest converged with disputes over representation; the crown’s financial predicament helped bring the Estates-General into session, turning a fiscal problem into a struggle over political authority ([*Britannica*, “French Revolution”](https://unvis.it/www.britannica.com/event/French-Revolution); [“The Estates-General, September 1788–July 1789,” *The Oxford History of the French Revolution*](https://erenow.org/modern/the-oxford-history-of-the-french-revolution/4.php); [“Origins of the French Revolution,” *History Compass*](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1478-0542.2007.00448.x)). Russia’s crisis was intensified by World War I: inflation, shortages, transport disruption, and war weariness eroded confidence in the tsarist state, while military logistics contributed to flour shortages in Petrograd in February 1917 ([British Academy, “A long look at the Russian Revolution”](https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/publishing/review/29/long-look-russian-revolution/); [“War and Revolution, 1914–1917,” *The Cambridge History of Russia*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-history-of-russia/war-and-revolution-19141917/B88B924B180BD9DF0330677F573277B8)). Iran’s oil-fueled modernization raised incomes while widening inequalities and disrupting established social positions; later inflation, tighter employment, reduced public spending, and political exclusion compounded the resulting dissatisfaction ([*The History of the Middle East*, “Chapter Fifteen: Arabia, Türkiye, and Iran, 1949–1979”](https://pressbooks.palomar.edu/historyofthemiddleeast/chapter/chapter-fifteen-arabia-turkiye-and-iran-1949-1979/); [Institute for National Strategic Studies, “Iran’s Islamic Revolution”](https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/128573/SF267.pdf)). The available evidence does not provide comparable quantitative measures of inequality, food prices, income, or state revenue across the three societies, so their economic pressures should be compared qualitatively, not ranked by a common metric.

Each revolution also began with a wider opposition than the eventual governing settlement. In France, Enlightenment ideas and claims to representation and popular sovereignty fed a politics that shifted from constitutional reconstruction toward republican and radical struggles; the Third Estate was not a unified class, and assemblies, clubs, and street mobilization competed to define the revolution ([*Britannica*, “French Revolution”](https://unvis.it/www.britannica.com/event/French-Revolution); [“Schematic Summary of the French Revolution”](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9780691274850-003/pdf); [“Never the Same Again? On Some Recent Interpretations of the French Revolution”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/BD4ED305B36CB90A45D20BB95192A8B1/S2398568217000140a.pdf/div-class-title-never-the-same-again-on-some-recent-interpretations-of-the-french-revolution-div.pdf)). In Russia, the February overthrow left a Provisional Government alongside influential workers’ and soldiers’ soviets; Lenin’s April Theses opposed continuing the war and urged a transfer of power toward workers and poorer peasants, helping distinguish the later Bolshevik seizure from the earlier collapse of tsarism ([Bard College, “From Empire to Republic”](https://projects.eh.bard.edu/1917/); [Lenin, “The Tasks of the Proletariat in the Present Revolution”](https://scalar.usc.edu/works/usm-open-source-history-text-the-world-at-war-world-history-1914-1945/media/Lenin%20April%20Theses.pdf)). Iran’s anti-Shah coalition included leftists, secular nationalists, liberal and moderate Islamic reformers, and religious networks. Khomeini’s prior opposition to the Shah and the reach of clerical and mosque networks helped him become the most influential leader, but his aims should not be treated as identical to those of every participant ([*The History of the Middle East*, “Chapter Fifteen”](https://pressbooks.palomar.edu/historyofthemiddleeast/chapter/chapter-fifteen-arabia-turkiye-and-iran-1949-1979/); [National Security Archive, “Iran’s 1979 Revolution Revisited”](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/iran/2019-02-11/irans-1979-revolution-revisited-failures-few-successes-us-intelligence-diplomatic-reporting); [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Khomeini i. Life”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/khomeini-i-life/)).

International forces sharpened these crises without determining their outcomes. French participation in the American War of Independence worsened the monarchy’s finances, while opposition from Austria and Prussia and war against Austria helped militarize the revolution ([*Britannica*, “French Revolution”](https://unvis.it/www.britannica.com/event/French-Revolution); [U.S. Department of State, “The United States and the French Revolution, 1789–1799”](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1784-1800/french-rev)). Russia’s upheaval occurred amid world war, and Germany enabled Lenin’s return to Russia; that intervention shows foreign strategic influence, not that Lenin was simply a German agent ([British Academy, “A long look at the Russian Revolution”](https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/publishing/review/29/long-look-russian-revolution/); [“The Stockholm Conference of 1917,” *International Review of Social History*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0020859000001528)). In Iran, the U.S.-involved 1953 coup helped restore the Shah and contributed to the history behind later anti-American sentiment. As the monarchy weakened, U.S. officials disagreed over whether to sustain the Shah, support Prime Minister Bakhtiar, or consider military options; the cited record says no consensus emerged and that a later-discussed U.S.-backed coup did not take place ([Institute for National Strategic Studies, “Iran’s Islamic Revolution”](https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/128573/SF267.pdf); [National Security Archive, “U.S.-Iran Relations: 40 Years of Antagonism, Distrust, and Frustration”](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/iran/2021-09-20/us-iran-relations-40-years-antagonism-distrust-and-frustration); [National Security Archive, “Iran’s 1979 Revolution Revisited”](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/iran/2019-02-11/irans-1979-revolution-revisited-failures-few-successes-us-intelligence-diplomatic-reporting)).

## Counter-revolution shaped institutions as well as violence

Opposition to revolution took different forms, from armed resistance to regional challenges and rivalry among revolutionary factions. France faced royalist and religious resistance, continuing foreign war, and domestic conflict; the radical phase included state-authorized terror, exceptional justice, and mass executions in 1793–1794. Violence continued after the fall of the Jacobins, as successive governments tried to stabilize a contested republic ([Cambridge University Press, “Violence and the French Revolution”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-the-age-of-atlantic-revolutions/violence-and-the-french-revolution/19552C72CB66761B541B7FF0C0773C9D)). Russia’s Bolshevik government faced the White armies and other opponents, alongside Allied intervention; the civil war extended from 1918 to 1921, and both Red and White forces used terror ([Oxford Bibliographies, “Russian Revolutions and Civil War, 1917–1921”](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62360/reference-article/554546917); [“Allied Intervention in Russia’s Civil War at 100”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13518046.2019.1690189); [“Russia’s Red Revolutionary and White Terror, 1917–1921”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09668136.2013.842362)). In Iran, revolutionary courts tried former officials and later opponents, while revolutionary committees and other coercive bodies remained part of the new order. The sources describe rapid consolidation and exclusion of rival power contenders, but do not offer an equally developed explanation of violence across all three cases ([Library of Congress, *Iran: A Country Study*](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/frd/frdcstdy/ir/irancountrystudy00curt_0/irancountrystudy00curt_0.pdf); [Ervand Abrahamian, *Iran: A Modern History*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv19prrqm)). These records do not supply methodologically comparable death tolls, so they cannot support a reliable ranking of the revolutions by total violence.

Institutional change was substantial, but it unfolded on different timelines. In France, the National Assembly abolished feudal privileges in 1789 and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen asserted rights and national sovereignty; the 1791 Constitution established a constitutional monarchy before the monarchy fell and a republic followed. The Directory ended with Napoleon’s coup in 1799, while the Civil Code came in 1804, beyond the conventional revolutionary period ([French National Assembly, “French Revolution”](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/histoire-et-patrimoine/revolution-francaise); [Élysée, “Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789)”](https://www.presidence-de-la-republique.fr/hist/10/ang/instit/text1.html); [Élysée, “The Constitution of 3 September 1791”](https://www.elysee.fr/la-presidence/la-constitution-du-3-septembre-1791); [Cornell Law School, “The French Civil Code”](https://ww3.lawschool.cornell.edu/research/JLPP/upload/Garson-203.pdf)). Russia’s Soviet government issued decrees on peace, land, nationalization, workers’ control, and civil law; the 1918 constitution established the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic, and the USSR was founded in 1922 ([*The Decrees of Soviet Power*, 1917–1918](https://www.marxists.org/history/ussr/government/1917/firstdecreesofsovietpower.pdf); [Oxford Bibliographies, “Russian Revolutions and Civil War, 1917–1921”](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62360/reference-article/554546917)). In Iran, the 1979 constitution combined elected offices with religious leadership and created institutions including the Guardian Council; revolutionary courts and the Revolutionary Guards also became instruments of consolidation. The new constitutional order retained some provisions from the previous constitution, showing that institutional rupture did not mean complete legal discontinuity ([*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Constitution of the Islamic Republic”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/constitution-of-the-islamic-republic/); [Ervand Abrahamian, *Iran: A Modern History*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv19prrqm)).

The long-term legacies likewise combine durable change with exclusion and uneven implementation. The French Revolution weakened inherited privilege and altered property relations, but land redistribution and legal arrangements varied regionally; Republican rights language also helped enslaved people in the Caribbean press claims that contributed to the abolition of slavery in 1794, while women remained excluded from political citizenship ([Cambridge University Press, “Violence and the French Revolution”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-the-age-of-atlantic-revolutions/violence-and-the-french-revolution/19552C72CB66761B541B7FF0C0773C9D); [University of Chicago Press, “The Effects of Land Redistribution: Evidence from the French Revolution”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/713688); [Harvard, “Slave Emancipation and the Limits of Citizenship during the French Revolution”](https://atlantic.fas.harvard.edu/Dubois%20-%20WP%2097032); [Cambridge University Press excerpt on post-revolutionary citizenship and gender](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805216/58782/excerpt/9780521658782_excerpt.pdf)). Russia produced a party-state that reorganized class and state power, but later Soviet rule also brought political purges, religious persecution, censorship, and deportations; these later policies should not be collapsed into the events of 1917 ([Library of Congress, “Revelations from the Russian Archives”](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/archives/intn.html)). Iran established a religious-political order alongside elected institutions, while women’s educational gains coexisted with legal constraints and limited political participation; scholars also disagree about the revolution’s longer-run economic effects and about the significance of class differences ([*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Constitution of the Islamic Republic”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/constitution-of-the-islamic-republic/); [Aga Khan University, “Revolutions and Women’s Rights: The Iranian Revolution in Comparative Perspective”](https://www.aku.edu/ismc/publications/Documents/AFAOcassionalPaperSeries_Issue1.pdf); [Springer, “Revolutions as Structural Breaks”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10602-025-09471-6)). Across all three, constitutional promises did not ensure equal citizenship or uniform lived outcomes.

## A layered framework explains breakdown better than a single cause

Structural theories explain why regimes become vulnerable but do not, by themselves, explain who takes power. Skocpol’s account links state weakness under international pressure with social structures that enable revolt; it helps illuminate France and Russia, but Iran’s revolution challenged the expectations derived from those cases. Goldstone’s approach adds leadership, ideology, movement identification, elite conflict, and international pressure, while Tilly’s process approach focuses on competing groups making incompatible claims to state authority ([Princeton University Press chapter on Skocpol’s theory](https://assets.press.princeton.edu/chapters/s9_8089.pdf); [“Rentier State and Shi’a Islam in the Iranian Revolution”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/social-revolutions-in-the-modern-world/rentier-state-and-shia-islam-in-the-iranian-revolution/56EE3B4F3B2BF0794C0D79BC78C71B4E); [Annual Review of Political Science, “Toward a Fourth Generation of Revolutionary Theory”](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.polisci.4.1.139); [Library of Congress-hosted chapter on revolutionary situations and multiple sovereignty](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/19/01/85/08/2019018508/2019018508.pdf)). Cultural and ideological accounts are also necessary: explanations focused only on class or the state miss religious and communal solidarities central to Iran ([“Iran’s Islamic Revolution in Comparative Perspective,” *World Politics*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/world-politics/article/abs/irans-islamic-revolution-in-comparative-perspective/ED0DC07E778902E8902075A13BA05CC2)).

A useful comparative sequence is **governing stress, elite or coercive-apparatus fracture, broad mobilization, opposition coordination and shared claims to legitimacy, a contest over authority, then institutional reconstruction**. It is a guide for comparison, not a fixed law or validated prediction formula. Iran especially shows why organization belongs in the explanation: a study comparing the Qom protests of 1975 and 1978 finds that prior opposition organization and growing confidence better explain the later protest’s wider effect than differences in economic conditions or political opportunity alone ([“The Qum Protests and the Coming of the Iranian Revolution,” *Social Science History*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/social-science-history/article/abs/qum-protests-and-the-coming-of-the-iranian-revolution-1975-and-1978/A1EE9BD1496A4DDBAED00C945B05B8CF)). Durable clerical networks connected mosques, religious circles, mourning groups, and bazaar supporters, giving leaders channels for communication and mobilization ([*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Jamʿiyat-e Moʾtalefa-ye Eslāmi”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jamiyat-e-motalefa-i/)). Conversely, Russia shows that the fall of a regime, a competing claim to authority, and the eventual consolidation of a new one are distinct stages ([“The Army and the Revolution,” Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/F6C453E81BB01743DC8105EAB90911E4/9780511615719c3_p64-137_CBO.pdf/the-army-and-the-revolution-1917.pdf)).

The framework should therefore monitor combinations: fiscal, economic, or military stress; visible elite divisions; the breadth and durability of mobilization; opposition capacity to coordinate around an alternative; security-force cohesion or defection; and foreign pressure or intervention. Evidence against an imminent revolutionary situation would include protests remaining episodic, grievances being addressed through functioning institutions, opposition groups staying fragmented, security forces following orders, or elections and negotiations remaining credible outlets. These indicators help assess relative vulnerability, not predict a country’s fate. Kuran’s preference-falsification theory further cautions that public opposition can be concealed until it suddenly becomes visible; research on revolutionary surprises also warns that past upheavals have not yielded reliable prediction of when or where the next will occur ([Timur Kuran, “Sparks and Prairie Fires”](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/preference-falsification/1989-kuran.pdf); [Timur Kuran, “The Inevitability of Future Revolutionary Surprises”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdf/10.1086/230671?download=true)).

## Contemporary scenarios remain conditional, not forecasts

Current unrest illustrates the difference between background pressure and a revolutionary situation. Carnegie’s tracker reported **more than 97 significant antigovernment protests in more than 55 countries** in the twelve months before its September 9, 2026 update, with **33 active protests** at the time; these counts measure protest activity, not imminent regime breakdown ([Carnegie Endowment, “Global Protest Tracker”](https://carnegieendowment.org/features/global-protest-tracker%20)). In France, late-2026 protests expanded across education, living costs, workers, and unions, while negotiations, concessions, policing, and electoral channels remained in play. The cited reporting does not establish a rival authority, security-force fracture, or collapse of state capacity ([Reuters, report published November 5, 2026](https://www.investing.com/news/world-news/france-faces-new-protests-as-macron-seeks-to-prevent-repeat-of-october-unrest-5002765); [Associated Press, report on French protests](https://apnews.com/article/france-protests-unions-students-paris-rioting-94e10fc8a1edbf7951e2c067418a7d33); [Le Monde, “French social unrest: Between concessions and tough talk”](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/politics/article/2026/11/14/french-social-unrest-between-concessions-and-tough-talk-pm-lecornu-struggles-to-end-crisis_6780688_5.html)).

Iran and Myanmar show more severe combinations of pressure and opposition activity, but not all the mechanisms associated with revolutionary regime change. In Iran, protests over currency and living costs spread during December 2025–January 2026 and were met with lethal repression; later accounts describe economic devastation, continued coercion, and a divided opposition, without established high-level defections or a broadly agreed alternative ([RUSI, “Is Iran at a Tipping Point?”](https://www.rusi.org/explore-our-research/publications/commentary/iran-tipping-point-protest-military-escalation-and-regime-survival); [The Atlantic, “What War Has Brought Iran’s Opposition”](https://www.theatlantic.com/international/2026/11/iran-opposition-strategic-options/689510/)). Renewed mass mobilization is possible if economic hardship and a triggering event meet stronger cross-sector organization; continued coercive rule remains plausible if security institutions stay cohesive. In Myanmar, the military and organized resistance groups contest authority across much of the country, but opposition forces remain divided and often lack firm territorial control, while the junta retains air superiority. A negotiated transition would depend on greater coordination and agreement among the National Unity Government, ethnic armed organizations, and other resistance groups; prolonged conflict or fragmentation also remains plausible ([BTI 2026 Myanmar Country Report](https://bti-project.org/en/reports/country-report/MMR)).

Other cases caution against treating protest, electoral change, or state weakness as interchangeable with revolution. In Serbia, a student-led movement won a parliamentary majority in the October 2026 election, and the new parliament convened on November 17 while government formation remained pending: the central question is whether electoral turnover becomes institutional reform, not whether a revolutionary seizure has already occurred ([AP News, “Serbia’s new parliament meets for the first time since students won election”](https://apnews.com/article/6614f11c13762d535c732210592f2011)). Bangladesh’s July 2024 uprising was followed by elections in February 2026 and a more stable political situation in the cited assessment, though garment-worker protests in November show continuing economic contention; those protests alone do not demonstrate a new regime-level coalition ([UK Home Office, “Report of a fact-finding mission Bangladesh: political situation, October 2026”](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/bangladesh-country-policy-and-information-notes/report-of-a-fact-finding-mission-bangladesh-political-situation-october-2026-accessible); [AP News, “Police in Bangladesh clash with garment workers demanding higher minimum wages”](https://apnews.com/article/98c4faa993c5f310685375bfc442cd24)). Haiti faces severe state weakness and gang challenges, but the cited UN evidence describes insecurity and a precarious political transition, not a unified popular movement with a shared program for state reconstruction ([UN Secretary-General, report on the United Nations Integrated Office in Haiti, October 12, 2026](https://dppa.un.org/sites/default/files/2026-10/SG%20October%20Report%20on%20BINUH%20-%20En.pdf)). OECD and ACLED assessments of fragility and conflict can flag places for closer monitoring, but do not directly forecast revolutions ([OECD, *States of Fragility: 2026 update*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/states-of-fragility-2026-update_866ee7f4-en/full-report.html); [ACLED, *Conflict Watchlist 2026*](https://acleddata.com/series/conflict-watchlist-2026)).

Economic shocks, war, and digital communication can alter the prospects for mobilization without fixing the outcome. In Iran, inflation and living costs contributed to protest, but the same crisis has accompanied repression and restricted communications; internet tools may reduce coordination costs, while censorship and surveillance can obstruct sustained organization ([RUSI, “Is Iran at a Tipping Point?”](https://www.rusi.org/explore-our-research/publications/commentary/iran-tipping-point-protest-military-escalation-and-regime-survival); [BTI 2026 Iran Country Report](https://bti-project.org/en/reports/country-report/IRN); [“Modelling temporal dynamics: does internet use fuel anti-government protests?”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13510347.2023.2268019)). A study of campaigns from 1970–2013 found security-force disloyalty in **45% of campaigns** but **13% of campaign-years**, and reported a positive association with campaign success; these figures are not country-specific probabilities and do not establish that defection alone causes a revolution ([“Security force disloyalty in nonviolent and violent campaigns, 1970–2013”](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopres/xjaf020)). Likewise, the NAVCO project covers **389 campaigns from 1945–2013** and reports declining success rates for nonviolent resistance campaigns after 2001, a caution against assuming historical campaign outcomes transfer unchanged to the present ([Harvard Kennedy School, “Updating nonviolent campaigns: Introducing NAVCO 2.1”](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/updating-nonviolent-campaigns-introducing-navco-21)).

## Conclusion

The comparison shows why revolutions should be understood as sequences rather than single explosions: crises open political space, but organized challengers, divisions inside the state, and competing claims to legitimacy determine whether that opening produces regime change and what institutions follow. The same forces that enable revolutionary victory can also narrow participation and authorize coercion, so overthrow and emancipation are not synonymous.

For future assessment, the strongest use of these histories is conditional monitoring, not prediction. A protest wave becomes more consequential when it coincides with sustained cross-group organization, elite fracture, security-force noncompliance, and a credible alternative governing project; without those developments, hardship or unrest alone is insufficient evidence of revolution. The cases offer mechanisms to watch, not a reliable timetable or formula for forecasting which society will undergo upheaval.
