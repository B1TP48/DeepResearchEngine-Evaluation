# Prove What the System Actually Preserves

No available result proves that a self-improving AI will remain aligned with human values indefinitely through unrestricted recursive self-modification; the premise that mathematics can establish this “definitely” is therefore unsupported. The strongest results are conditional: they prove that a specified system preserves a specified property under stated assumptions, not that the property captures human values or that the assumptions hold forever ([“Algorithmic Unverifiability of Safety for Fixed and Recursively Self-Improving Systems”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28639); [“A Rational Defense of Reasonable Reflection”](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol380-lics2026/html/LIPIcs.LICS.2026.1/LIPIcs.LICS.2026.1.html)). Here, **alignment** means conformity to a formal target, which might be an encoded utility, a safety constraint, or a model of human preferences; these targets are not interchangeable ([“Self-Modification of Policy and Utility Function in Rational Agents”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.03142); [“AI Alignment with Changing and Influenceable Reward Functions”](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/carroll24a.html)). **Recursive self-modification** means a system changing its own code, policy, utility, or related components, with successors potentially making further changes ([“Gödel Machines: Self-Referential Universal Problem Solvers Making Provably Optimal Self-Improvements”](https://sferics.idsia.ch/pub/juergen/gm4.pdf); [“Tiling Agents for Self-Modifying AI, and the Löbian Obstacle”](https://intelligence.org/files/TilingAgentsDraft.pdf)). In a step-by-step model, “indefinitely” means that the property holds at every finite stage of an unending sequence, not that a proof predicts every possible real-world future. The defensible answer is **yes for carefully specified invariants within a proved model, but no definite general guarantee of human-value alignment**.

## A guarantee applies only to its specified target

A standard conditional guarantee starts with an invariant \(I\), proves that it holds initially, and proves that every permitted modification preserves it. Induction then establishes \(I\) at every finite stage, provided the update relation covers all permitted changes and the checker enforcing that relation remains within the trusted system ([“A Rational Defense of Reasonable Reflection”](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol380-lics2026/html/LIPIcs.LICS.2026.1/LIPIcs.LICS.2026.1.html)). This can establish a precise claim—such as a program property or a specified safety constraint—but cannot, by itself, establish that the invariant faithfully represents human values.

Proof-gated reflection illustrates the distinction. A 2026 paper describes a gate that checks evidence for proposed changes and gives an example preserving the property that a reflective interpreter remains a conservative extension of its base. Its theorem concerns that software-semantics property, not whether the interpreter’s behavior matches human values ([“A Rational Defense of Reasonable Reflection”](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol380-lics2026/html/LIPIcs.LICS.2026.1/LIPIcs.LICS.2026.1.html)). Gödel machines similarly accept rewrites only when a proof searcher establishes that they are useful according to the machine’s formal utility and axioms. Their global-optimality result assumes a consistent formal system, and the paper notes that useful changes may go unproved and therefore be ignored ([“Gödel Machines”](https://sferics.idsia.ch/pub/juergen/gm4.pdf)).

Results about rational self-modifying agents also depend on what the model assumes. Everitt and colleagues show that, in their framework, an agent can make essentially harmless modifications relative to its current utility when it anticipates the consequences and evaluates future outcomes using that utility. The result does not validate the initial utility as a representation of human values; the authors also caution that it is on-policy and does not rule out changing the percepts supplied to the utility function ([“Self-Modification of Policy and Utility Function in Rational Agents”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.03142)). If the initial utility is wrong, preserving it can prevent correction; if the agent is unaware of modification consequences or optimizes instantaneous utility, harmful changes can become possible ([“Self-Modification of Policy and Utility Function in Rational Agents”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.03142)). A separate tiling-agent analysis constructs an indefinitely approving sequence within a formal trust model, but leaves a fundamental decision criterion for self-modifying probabilistic agents open and does not prove human-value preservation ([“Tiling Agents for Self-Modifying AI, and the Löbian Obstacle”](https://intelligence.org/files/TilingAgentsDraft.pdf)). Its discussion also identifies a Gödel/Löb obstacle to a consistent theory proving its own unrestricted soundness, while offering technical ways around that obstacle only within the model ([“Tiling Agents for Self-Modifying AI, and the Löbian Obstacle”](https://intelligence.org/files/TilingAgentsDraft.pdf)).

## Update mechanisms preserve different properties

Formal work addresses several mechanisms, but their guarantees concern different targets. Certified self-modifying code uses machine-checked reasoning about runtime code changes; the cited framework includes a Coq soundness proof and certified examples, including an x86 operating-system boot loader and MIPS code examples. These are program-correctness results, not evidence that an AI’s goals remain aligned ([“Certified Self-Modifying Code”](https://flint.cs.yale.edu/certikos/publications/smctr.pdf)). Runtime shields instead constrain a learning system’s actions against a specified temporal-logic safety property, using an abstraction of environment dynamics; the shield does not prove that the learner’s objective expresses human values ([“Safe Reinforcement Learning via Shielding”](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/11797/11797-13-15325-1-2-20201228.pdf)). Verification-preserving model updates change an environmental model and controller together with an updated safety proof, and were evaluated on hybrid-system control tasks under stated model and monitoring conditions ([“Verifiably Safe Off-Model Reinforcement Learning”](https://logic.kastel.kit.edu/ap/pub/vpmu.pdf)).

These mechanisms show how some properties can survive defined changes: check each candidate update, maintain an external shield, or update the proof alongside the model. They do not show that arbitrary changes are safe, or that a system cannot alter or bypass its checker, shield, or assumptions. The cited seL4 project provides a bounded example of proof maintenance: its documentation says proofs are updated as changes are merged and reports no functional-correctness defects in verified code since completion of that proof in 2009, while identifying assumptions and unverified components. That is evidence about a verified operating-system kernel, not recursively self-improving AI ([seL4 Proofs](https://sel4.systems/Verification/proofs); [seL4 FAQ](https://sel4.org/About/FAQ.html)).

Not every proposed approach offers a formal preservation theorem. EXPAI advocates education, testing, and intervention for recursive self-improvers, while warning that incomplete assumptions and proof costs can limit proof-based approaches; it proposes a management strategy rather than proving indefinite alignment ([“Growing Recursive Self-Improvers”](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~kris/ftp/AGI16_growing_recursive-self-improvers.pdf)). Moreover, a 2020 analysis finds that some bounded-rational agents in its models can suffer exponentially worsening performance and gradual misalignment; it covers four modeled imperfections, not every AI or update process ([“Performance of Bounded-Rational Agents With the Ability to Self-Modify”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06275)). The evidence therefore supports neither the claim that all self-modification is unsafe nor the claim that a proof gate solves alignment generally.

## Human values are not a settled formal invariant

Formal value models make different choices about what an AI should preserve. Cooperative inverse reinforcement learning treats the human and robot as sharing a reward function that the robot initially does not know; inverse reward design treats a designed reward as evidence about a possibly different underlying objective. These approaches represent uncertainty, but neither establishes that observed feedback or a designed reward fully captures what people value in every context ([“Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.03137); [“Inverse Reward Design”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.02827)). Other proposals represent incomplete orderings or uncertainty over preference orderings rather than forcing a single complete ranking ([“Impossibility and Uncertainty Theorems in AI Value Alignment”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.00064)).

Values may also differ across people or change over time. Arrow’s theorem rules out a social preference aggregation rule satisfying five specified conditions—universal domain, social ordering, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship—when there are more than two alternatives. Its relevance depends on whether that ordinal framework and those conditions fit the problem; relaxing assumptions or using richer inputs can avoid the result ([“Arrow’s Theorem”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/arrows-theorem/index.html); [“Social Choice Theory”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/social-choice/)). Moral Graph Elicitation is one proposal for eliciting contextual values and comparing them; its reported trial involved 500 Americans and three divisive prompts, which does not establish a uniquely correct or universally accepted target ([“What are human values, and how do we align AI to them?”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10636)). Dynamic Reward MDPs explicitly model changing preferences and AI influence on them. The authors compare eight alignment notions and find that, in their framework, they either permit undesirable influence or become overly risk-averse; this is not a theorem about every possible alignment method ([“AI Alignment with Changing and Influenceable Reward Functions”](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/carroll24a.html)).

There is no settled resolution to this modeling challenge. One argument holds that diverse human values arise from structured cognitive processes that could support scalable learning if modeled accurately; a contrasting view emphasizes moral pluralism and the political question of whose values count. These are a theoretical proposal and a normative critique, not proofs that value learning either will or cannot work generally ([“How Complex Are Human Values?”](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/640697699fefd338762bdda6/t/646ed1b6349289280465728c/1684984246781/de%2BFont-Reaulx%2B%28ms%29%2B-%2BHow%2BComplex%2BAre%2BHuman%2BValues.pdf); [“Artificial Intelligence, Values, and Alignment”](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11023-020-09539-2)). Formal corrigibility and shutdown work likewise offers conditional properties—such as obedience to shutdown instructions—based on assumptions about human vigilance, instructions, and the system’s response, rather than unconditional alignment through arbitrary self-modification ([“5 Routes to Control”](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v216/carey23a/carey23a.pdf); [“Corrigibility: Definitions, Algorithms & Implications”](https://openreview.net/references/pdf?id=QfIHz7s1Kv)).

## General certification faces limits, not universal impossibility

A 2026 preprint on unrestricted code transformations reports that deciding whether safety survives certain modifications is undecidable or at least as hard as the halting problem, with persistence over an entire trajectory potentially harder still. The result is explicitly limited to formal classes of systems and properties, and does not show that every self-modifying system is unsafe; the cited record identifies it as a preprint, not a peer-reviewed result ([“Algorithmic Unverifiability of Safety for Fixed and Recursively Self-Improving Systems”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28639)). A separate 2026 preprint proposes a trade-off among soundness, broad coverage, and tractability under its assumptions about verification access, domain coverage, and model expressivity. It likewise supports a conditional limit on certification, not a universal impossibility theorem ([“On the Formal Limits of Alignment Verification”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08761)). Conversely, a peer-reviewed study shows that approximate alignment can be verified with a constant-query test under sufficient conditions in its formal setting; that positive result does not extend automatically to arbitrary agents or all future states ([“Value Alignment Verification”](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/brown21a.html)).

## Conclusion

A rigorous proof can establish indefinite preservation of a formal invariant when the initial state, allowed updates, specification, and trusted enforcement mechanism are all covered by the proof. The remaining gap is not merely technical: a system can preserve its encoded objective perfectly while that objective misstates human values, excludes disagreement, or fails to reflect legitimate changes in preference.

Accordingly, the strongest defensible answer is conditional rather than absolute. Restricting self-modification and re-verifying updates can support meaningful safety guarantees within a model; no cited result establishes that those guarantees amount to human alignment across unrestricted recursive self-modification and every future environment.
