# PAC Learning Converts Samples into Guarantees

Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) learning, introduced by Valiant in 1984, answers a practical question: how many labelled examples are enough to trust a rule on unseen data? The framework promises that, with probability at least **1−δ**, a learner's output has true error no worse than the best error in its chosen class **H** plus **ε** ([Cornell CS6781, Lecture 8](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs6781/2020sp/lectures/08-agnostic-bounds.pdf)). For a finite class, the uniform-convergence bound is **m ≥ ln(2|H|/δ)/(2ε²)** ([CMU 10-315, Lecture 25](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~10315-s20/lectures/10315_Sp20_Lecture_25_Learning_Theory_inked.pdf)); the realizable-case bound is **m ≥ (1/ε)(ln|H| + ln(1/δ))** ([CMU 10-601, PAC Learning](http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~mgormley/courses/10601-s21/slides/lecture25-pac.pdf)). The significance is structural: sample size grows only logarithmically in **|H|** and quadratically in **1/ε**, so accuracy is expensive and confidence is relatively cheap ([McGill COMP-652](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~dprecup/courses/ML/Lectures/ml-lecture23.pdf); [CMU 10-601](http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~mgormley/courses/10601-s21/slides/lecture25-pac.pdf)). Infinite classes require VC dimension instead of raw counting ([UML p. 72](https://issuhub.com/view/index/1331?pageIndex=72)), and the guarantee is worst-case and distribution-free rather than a forecast for modern deep learning ([AAAI 1990](https://aaaipress.org/Papers/AAAI/1990/AAAI90-163.pdf); [Zhang et al., ICLR 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03530)). Valiant's original formulation used a single parameter **h** for both accuracy and confidence and allowed only one-sided error ([Valiant, CACM 1984](http://web.mit.edu/6.435/www/Valiant84.pdf)).

## The PAC promise is about future mistakes, not training scores

The basic objects are simple. Inputs come from a domain **X**; labels come from **Y**; an unknown distribution **D** generates examples; a hypothesis **h** is a candidate rule from a class **H**; and the true error is the probability that **h** disagrees with the target on a fresh draw from **D**, written **L_D(h) = P_{x~D}(h(x) ≠ f(x))** ([CMU 15-251, Lecture 27](https://procaccia.info/courses/15251f15/slides/lec27.pdf)). The empirical or training error is the average loss on the sample **S = (z₁,…,z_m)**, written **L_S(h) = (1/m) Σᵢ ℓ(h,zᵢ)** ([TAU, PAC learning](https://www.tau.ac.il/~rlivni/files/clt/lect2_pac.pdf)). The PAC definition fixes a sample-complexity function **m_H(ε,δ)** and requires that, for every **ε,δ ∈ (0,1)**, every distribution **D**, and every labelling function **f** satisfying the realizable assumption that some **h* ∈ H** has zero true error, running the algorithm on **m ≥ m_H(ε,δ)** i.i.d. examples returns **h** with **L_D(h) ≤ ε** with probability at least **1−δ** ([UML Chapter 3, Definition 3.1](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/understanding-machine-learning/formal-learning-model/94E1E4D2AC86897DF9B330977FF016B0)). The textbook calls **ε** the accuracy parameter and **δ** the confidence parameter: ε controls “approximately correct,” δ controls “probably” ([UML Chapter 3](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/understanding-machine-learning/formal-learning-model/94E1E4D2AC86897DF9B330977FF016B0)).

| Symbol | Plain meaning | Formal expression |
|---|---|---|
| **X, Y, D** | inputs, labels, unknown data source | examples **(x, f(x))** with **x ~ D** ([UML Chapter 3](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/understanding-machine-learning/formal-learning-model/94E1E4D2AC86897DF9B330977FF016B0)) |
| **H** | the list of candidate rules | a set of hypotheses |
| **true error** | chance of a future mistake | **L_D(h) = P(h(x) ≠ f(x))** ([CMU 15-251](https://procaccia.info/courses/15251f15/slides/lec27.pdf)) |
| **empirical error** | average mistake rate on the sample | **L_S(h) = (1/m) Σ ℓ(h,zᵢ)** ([TAU](https://www.tau.ac.il/~rlivni/files/clt/lect2_pac.pdf)) |
| **ε** | allowed true error | accuracy target |
| **δ** | allowed failure probability of the guarantee | confidence target |
| **m** | number of labelled examples | **m_H(ε,δ)** |

Agnostic PAC learning removes the assumption that a perfect rule exists in **H**. It requires that, for every joint distribution over inputs and labels, the learner output **h_A** satisfy **err_D(h_A) ≤ min_{c∈C} err_D(c) + ε** with probability **1−δ** ([Cornell CS6781, Lecture 8](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs6781/2020sp/lectures/08-agnostic-bounds.pdf)). The realizable guarantee is absolute because the best-in-class error is zero; the agnostic guarantee is relative because it promises only “no worse than the best in **H**, plus ε” ([Cornell CS6781](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs6781/2020sp/lectures/08-agnostic-bounds.pdf)). A useful plain story is this: a rule can look perfect on the examples shown, but two different things can still go wrong. The rule may be wrong on many unseen items — that is what **ε** measures — or the sample may have been unlucky and produced a bad rule — that is what **δ** measures. The theorem says enough examples make the second kind of bad luck rare, and when it does not happen, the first kind of error is small ([Princeton COS 511, Lecture 3](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spring13/cos511/scribe_notes/0212.pdf)).

Two warnings matter for beginners. **ε is not the observed training error**; in the agnostic guarantee the benchmark is **min_{h∈H} err(h)**, so **ε** bounds excess true error over the best hypothesis in the class ([TAU](https://www.tau.ac.il/~rlivni/files/clt/lect2_pac.pdf)). **δ is not a probability of error**; it is the probability that the random draw of the sample produces a misleading training set, and the guarantee says nothing about how large the error can be in that bad event ([Cornell CS6781](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs6781/2020sp/lectures/08-agnostic-bounds.pdf)). Zero training error is not by itself evidence of low true error: a bad classifier with true error at least **ε** can still get all **m** training points right with probability up to **(1−ε)^m** ([CMU 10-315](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~10315-s20/lectures/10315_Sp20_Lecture_25_Learning_Theory_inked.pdf)). PAC is distribution-free, but it assumes training and future test items come from the same distribution **D** ([NJU ML lecture 2](http://www.lamda.nju.edu.cn/mlt2023/Slides/chapter_2.pdf)).

## Training error is a sample mean: expectation, Chebyshev, and the law of large numbers

Six probability ideas are enough for the whole argument: a random variable is a number produced by a random experiment; expectation is its probability-weighted average; an indicator variable is a 0/1 variable recording whether an event happened; independence means one outcome does not change another's probability; variance is the average squared distance from the mean; and standard deviation is the square root of variance, in the same units as the data ([Wikipedia, Expected value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_value); [Wikipedia, Variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variance); [Wikipedia, Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_(probability_theory))). A fair six-sided die has expectation **(1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = 3.5**, and many rolls average near 3.5 ([Wikipedia, Law of large numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_large_numbers)). A Bernoulli variable is 1 with probability **p** and 0 with probability **1−p**; its mean is **p** and variance is **p(1−p)** ([Wikipedia, Bernoulli distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_distribution)). For a die roll with **X=1** if the result is 6 and 0 otherwise, **E[X] = 1·(1/6)+0·(5/6)=1/6≈0.167** and **Var(X)=(1/6)(5/6)=5/36≈0.139**, computed from the Bernoulli formulas ([Wikipedia, Bernoulli distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_distribution)).

The 0/1 loss **ℓ(h(x),y)** is Bernoulli: the true error **p = L_D(h) = E[ℓ(h(x),y)] = P(h(x)≠y)**, and the training error is the average of **m** independent such loss variables ([TAU](https://www.tau.ac.il/~rlivni/files/clt/lect2_pac.pdf)). Therefore **E[L_S(h)] = p** and **Var(L_S(h)) = p(1−p)/m**. The law of large numbers makes this precise. Let **X̄_m = (X₁+…+X_m)/m** be a sample mean with true mean **μ** and variance **σ²**. Linearity gives **E[X̄_m]=μ**, and independence gives **Var(X̄_m)=σ²/m** ([Wikipedia, Law of large numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_large_numbers)). Chebyshev's inequality turns that variance into a tail bound: **P(|X̄_m−μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/(mε²)** ([Wikipedia, Law of large numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_large_numbers)). For a 0/1 loss, **σ²=p(1−p)≤1/4**, so requiring the right side to be at most **δ** gives **m ≥ 1/(4ε²δ)**; with **ε=0.1** and **δ=0.05**, this gives **m ≥ 500** for a single fixed hypothesis ([TAU](https://www.tau.ac.il/~rlivni/files/clt/lect2_pac.pdf)). Chebyshev's bound is generally loose and should be read as an illustration of the method, not a recommended sample size ([Wikipedia, Chebyshev's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Chebyshev%27s_inequality&oldid=509452739)).

This explains the slogan “training error approximates true error”: training error is a sample mean, and true error is its expectation. But the statement is per hypothesis, at rate **1/m**, and asymptotic. It says nothing about the largest deviation over an entire class **H**, which is why the union bound is needed ([TAU](https://www.tau.ac.il/~rlivni/files/clt/lect2_pac.pdf)).

## Hoeffding plus the union bound produce the finite-class formula

Hoeffding's inequality gives the sharper finite-sample bound that PAC theory uses. Let **X₁,…,X_n** be independent with **a_i ≤ X_i ≤ b_i**, let **S_n = X₁+…+X_n**, and let **c = Σ(b_i−a_i)²**. Then for every **t>0**, **P(S_n − E[S_n] ≥ t) ≤ exp(−2t²/c)**, and the two-sided form is **P(|S_n − E[S_n]| ≥ t) ≤ 2exp(−2t²/c)** ([Wikipedia, Hoeffding's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoeffding%27s_inequality)). The symbols mean: **n** or **m** is the number of examples; **X_i** is the loss on example **i**, either 0 or 1; **a_i,b_i** are lower and upper bounds on that loss; **S_n** is the sum of losses; **t** is the allowed deviation in sum units; **ε** is the allowed deviation in average units, so **t=mε**; and **exp** means **e^(·)**.

The derivation has four moves. For **s>0**, Markov's inequality on the positive variable **e^{s(S_n−E[S_n])}** gives **P(S_n−E[S_n] ≥ t) ≤ e^{−st} E[e^{s(S_n−E[S_n])}]** ([Sariel Har-Peled, Chernoff notes](https://sarielhp.org/research/papers/notes/16/chernoff/chernoff.pdf)). Independence factorises the expectation into a product over **i**. Hoeffding's lemma bounds each factor: if **X ∈ [a,b]**, then **E[e^{s(X−E[X])}] ≤ exp(s²(b−a)²/8)** ([Wikipedia, Hoeffding's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoeffding%27s_inequality); [Har-Peled](https://sarielhp.org/research/papers/notes/16/chernoff/chernoff.pdf)). That lemma follows from convexity of **e^{sx}**: linear interpolation on **[a,b]**, centring, and Taylor expansion with **φ″(u) ≤ 1/4** give the **1/8** constant ([Har-Peled](https://sarielhp.org/research/papers/notes/16/chernoff/chernoff.pdf)). Multiplying the factors gives **P(S_n−E[S_n] ≥ t) ≤ exp(s²c/8 − st)**. The exponent is minimised at **s = 4t/c**, and substituting gives **exp(−2t²/c)**; applying the same argument to **−(S_n−E[S_n])** and union-bounding the two directions produces the factor 2 ([Har-Peled](https://sarielhp.org/research/papers/notes/16/chernoff/chernoff.pdf); [Wikipedia, Hoeffding's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoeffding%27s_inequality)).

For 0/1 losses averaged over **m** examples, **a_i=0**, **b_i=1**, **c=m**, and **t=mε**. The exponent becomes **−2(mε)²/m = −2mε²**, so **P(|L_S(h)−L_D(h)| ≥ ε) ≤ 2e^{−2mε²}** ([TAU](https://www.tau.ac.il/~rlivni/files/clt/lect2_pac.pdf)). As a sanity check, **m=100** and **ε=0.1** give **2e^{−2}≈0.271**, while **m=200** gives **2e^{−4}≈0.037**, computed from the cited formula ([Wikipedia, Hoeffding's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoeffding%27s_inequality)). Hoeffding is a Chernoff-style bound: both start from Markov applied to **e^{sX}**, but Hoeffding uses only the range of each variable, which makes it distribution-free and often looser than a Chernoff bound that uses the actual variance ([Wikipedia, Chernoff bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernoff_bound); [Wikipedia, Hoeffding's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoeffding%27s_inequality)).

A single-hypothesis bound is not enough, because ERM chooses **h_S = argmin_h L_S(h)** after seeing the very sample that produced the estimates. The relevant bad event is **{∃h∈H : |L_S(h)−L_D(h)| ≥ ε}**, a union of **|H|** events. Boole's inequality, or the union bound, says **P(∪A_i) ≤ ΣP(A_i)** ([Wikipedia, Boole's inequality](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_bound)). Its proof is induction: the base case is **P(A₁)≤P(A₁)**, and the step uses **P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)** with the intersection probability nonnegative ([Wikipedia, Boole's inequality](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_bound)). Applying it to the class gives **P(∃h∈H : |L_S(h)−L_D(h)| ≥ ε) ≤ 2|H|e^{−2mε²}** ([TAU](https://www.tau.ac.il/~rlivni/files/clt/lect2_pac.pdf)). The price of covering the whole class is exactly a factor **|H|**; because that factor multiplies an exponential, it later becomes an additive **ln|H|** inside the sample size.

Set the union-bounded failure probability equal to **δ** and solve:

**2|H|e^{−2mε²} ≤ δ**

**e^{−2mε²} ≤ δ/(2|H|)**

**−2mε² ≤ ln(δ/(2|H|)) = ln δ − ln(2|H|)**

**2mε² ≥ ln(2|H|) − ln δ = ln(2|H|/δ)**

**m ≥ ln(2|H|/δ)/(2ε²)**.

This is the finite-class uniform-convergence bound: with probability at least **1−δ**, every **h∈H** has empirical error within **ε** of true error ([CMU 10-315](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~10315-s20/lectures/10315_Sp20_Lecture_25_Learning_Theory_inked.pdf); [McGill COMP-652](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~dprecup/courses/ML/Lectures/ml-lecture23.pdf)). Solving the same inequality for **ε** instead gives **ε ≤ √(ln(2|H|/δ)/(2m))**, the “how accurate with this much data” form ([CMU 10-315](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~10315-s20/lectures/10315_Sp20_Lecture_25_Learning_Theory_inked.pdf)). For **|H|=1000**, **ε=0.1**, **δ=0.05**, the numerator is **ln(40,000)≈10.5966**, the denominator is **2(0.1)²=0.02**, and **m≥529.8**, so about **530** examples ([CMU 10-315](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~10315-s20/lectures/10315_Sp20_Lecture_25_Learning_Theory_inked.pdf)).

The constants are not all the same because they belong to different theorems. The table below separates them.

| Statement | Sample-size guarantee | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Uniform convergence for finite **H**: every **h** within **ε** | **m ≥ ln(2|H|/δ)/(2ε²)** | [CMU 10-315](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~10315-s20/lectures/10315_Sp20_Lecture_25_Learning_Theory_inked.pdf); [IIT Kharagpur](http://cse.iitkgp.ac.in/~pabitra/course/aml/Lec9-FTP.pdf) |
| Agnostic ERM for finite **C**: excess error over best-in-class at most **ε** | **m ≥ (2/ε²)(ln|C| + ln(2/δ))** | [Cornell CS6781](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs6781/2020sp/lectures/08-agnostic-bounds.pdf) |
| Realizable finite **H**: all consistent **h** have error at most **ε** | **m ≥ (1/ε)(ln|H| + ln(1/δ))** | [CMU 10-601](http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~mgormley/courses/10601-s21/slides/lecture25-pac.pdf); [UML Corollary 3.2](https://prove2.me/collections/understanding-machine-learning?status=completed) |
| A looser agnostic route using uniform convergence at **ε/2** with slack | **m ≥ (8/ε²)ln(2|H|/δ)** | [TAU](https://www.tau.ac.il/~rlivni/files/clt/lect2_pac.pdf) |

The **1/2** in the uniform-convergence bound is the reciprocal of the **2** in Hoeffding's exponent **−2mε²**, which itself comes from the squared range **(b−a)²=1** of a 0/1 loss ([Wikipedia, Hoeffding's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoeffding%27s_inequality)). The **2** in **2|H|/δ** is the two-sided tail, covering deviations in both directions; a one-sided bound would give **|H|/δ** instead ([Wikipedia, Hoeffding's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoeffding%27s_inequality)). The **ln(1/δ)** term is simply what **e^{−2mε²}≤δ** becomes after taking logs; larger confidence enters only logarithmically ([McGill COMP-652](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~dprecup/courses/ML/Lectures/ml-lecture23.pdf)). The realizable case is different because a bad hypothesis is ruled out by a single example with probability **ε**, so the relevant failure probability is **(1−ε)^m ≤ e^{−εm}**, not a two-sided average deviation ([UCSB machine learning notes](https://web.math.ucsb.edu/~atzberg/pmwiki_intranet/uploads/Teaching2018FallMachineLearningMATHCS120/ml_lecture_PAC_Learning_Atzberger.pdf)). Solving **|H|e^{−εm}≤δ** gives **m ≥ (1/ε)(ln|H| + ln(1/δ))**, a **1/ε** rate rather than **1/ε²** ([UCSB](https://web.math.ucsb.edu/~atzberg/pmwiki_intranet/uploads/Teaching2018FallMachineLearningMATHCS120/ml_lecture_PAC_Learning_Atzberger.pdf)). With **|H|=1000**, **ε=0.1**, **δ=0.05**, the realizable formula gives **(6.9078+2.9957)/0.1≈99** examples, computed from that bound ([CMU 10-601](http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~mgormley/courses/10601-s21/slides/lecture25-pac.pdf)); the uniform-convergence bound gives about **530**, and the agnostic ERM bound gives about **2119** ([Cornell CS6781](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs6781/2020sp/lectures/08-agnostic-bounds.pdf)). The differences are statements, not contradictions.

The agnostic guarantee also splits into approximation and estimation error. Write **L_D(h_S)=ε_app+ε_est**, where **ε_app=min_{h∈H}L_D(h)** is the approximation error of the class and **ε_est=L_D(h_S)−ε_app** is the estimation error caused by choosing from noisy empirical estimates ([Pascal Welke, Error Decomposition](http://pwelke.de/SeminarLearningTheory/s03_ErrorDecomposition.html)). The approximation error depends on **H** and **D**, not on sample size; the estimation error is the probabilistic part ([Welke](http://pwelke.de/SeminarLearningTheory/s03_ErrorDecomposition.html)). If the sample is **ε/2-representative**, meaning **|L_S(h)−L_D(h)|≤ε/2** for every **h∈H**, then ERM satisfies **L_D(h_S)≤min_{h∈H}L_D(h)+ε** ([UML Lemma 4.2](https://issuhub.com/view/index/1331?pageIndex=54)). The proof is three inequalities: **L_D(h_S)≤L_S(h_S)+ε/2≤L_S(h*)+ε/2≤L_D(h*)+ε** ([TAU](https://www.tau.ac.il/~rlivni/files/clt/lect2_pac.pdf)). Running uniform convergence at accuracy **ε/2** is what yields the agnostic bound **m=⌈2log(2|H|/δ)/ε²⌉**, a factor of four above the uniform-convergence sample size ([Hanveiga, Theorem 5](https://hanveiga.com/ml_lecture_notes_2022_v0.0.pdf)). If **ε_app** already exceeds **ε**, no sample size helps; the theorem can only buy the estimation term ([Welke](http://pwelke.de/SeminarLearningTheory/s03_ErrorDecomposition.html)).

## Infinite classes need VC dimension, and geometry can beat counting

The finite formula fails when **H** is infinite, because **ln|H|** is infinite and the union bound diverges ([Kearns et al., statistical mechanics of generalization](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/papers/barbados/hkst-statmech.pdf)). Intervals on a line and linear classifiers have uncountably many members, so counting hypotheses is hopeless ([Greiner, PAC Learning notes](https://www2.cs.uh.edu/~ceick/ML/Greiner-PAC-Learning.pdf)). The fix is to count not hypotheses but distinct behaviours on the **m** data points. The growth function, or shatter coefficient, is **Π_H(m)=max_{|S|=m}|{ (h(x₁),…,h(x_m)) : h∈H }|** ([CMU 10-806, Balcan](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~avrim/ML07/lect-09-21-23.pdf); [Princeton COS 511, Lecture 6](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spr08/cos511/scribe_notes/0220.pdf)). A set **S** is shattered if **H** produces all **2^{|S|}** labellings; the VC dimension is the size of the largest shattered set ([CMU 10-806](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~avrim/ML07/lect-09-21-23.pdf)). Half-lines on the line have VC dimension 1; intervals have VC dimension 2; axis-aligned rectangles in the plane have VC dimension 4; axis-parallel hyper-rectangles in **R^n** have VC dimension **2n**; and linear classifiers in **R^d** have VC dimension **d+1** ([CMU 10-806](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~avrim/ML07/lect-09-21-23.pdf); [Oxford CLT notes](http://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/people/varun.kanade/teaching/CLT-MT2022/lectures/CLT.pdf); [Princeton COS 511, Lecture 6](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spr08/cos511/scribe_notes/0220.pdf)).

Sauer's lemma makes the replacement finite. If **d=VCdim(H)**, then **Π_H(m) ≤ Σ_{i=0}^{d} C(m,i) ≤ (em/d)^d** ([Princeton COS 511, Sauer's lemma](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spring19/cos511/scribe_notes/0225.pdf)). The sum counts the ways of choosing at most **d** items from **m**, and the exponential form shows polynomial growth once **d** is finite ([Princeton COS 511, Lecture 6](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spr08/cos511/scribe_notes/0220.pdf)). The union bound can then run over the distinct labellings instead of over **H**; **ln|H|** is replaced by roughly **d ln(m/d)**, which is where the **d log(1/ε)** term in the final VC bounds comes from ([CMU 10-806](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~avrim/ML07/lect-09-21-23.pdf); [CMU 09/19/14 notes](http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~avrim/ML09/lect0919.txt)). For finite classes, **VCdim(H) ≤ log₂|H|**, so the finite-class bound is a special case ([UML p. 72](https://issuhub.com/view/index/1331?pageIndex=72)).

The resulting VC sample complexities are **O((1/ε)(d log(1/ε)+log(1/δ)))** in the realizable case and **O((1/ε²)(d+log(1/δ)))** in the agnostic case, up to absolute constants ([Shalev-Shwartz, MLSS Tübingen](http://mlss.tuebingen.mpg.de/2017/speaker_slides/Shai3.pdf)). A classical explicit realizable bound is **max{(4/ε)log₂(2/δ), (8d/ε)log₂(13/ε)}** ([Calvert, Lemma 7.3.13](http://lagrange.math.siu.edu/calvert/LPBookDraft/Ch7Learning.pdf); [CMU 10-601 Mitchell](http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~tom/10601_fall2012/slides/PAC-learning1-10-11-2012_ANN.pdf)). The fundamental theorem of PAC learning says that for binary classification with the 0–1 loss, six statements are equivalent: uniform convergence; every ERM rule is a successful agnostic PAC learner; agnostic PAC learnability; PAC learnability; every ERM rule is a successful PAC learner; and finite VC dimension ([UML Theorem 6.7, p. 72](https://issuhub.com/view/index/1331?pageIndex=72)). Infinite VC dimension implies not PAC learnable ([IIT Kharagpur](http://cse.iitkgp.ac.in/~pabitra/course/aml/Lec9-FTP.pdf)). The equivalence is the central result of Blumer, Ehrenfeucht, Haussler and Warmuth's 1989 paper ([JACM 1989](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/76359.76371)).

Geometric classes show why infinite **H** can still be learnable. For thresholds, intervals, rectangles and hyper-rectangles, the learner returns the tightest consistent object; the error is a set of **k** “strips” or tails; each strip is forced to have probability mass **ε/k**; the sample misses a given strip with probability **(1−ε/k)^m ≤ e^{−mε/k}**; and a union bound over **k** strips gives **k e^{−mε/k} ≤ δ**, hence **m ≥ (k/ε)ln(k/δ)** ([CMU 15-251](https://procaccia.info/courses/15251f15/slides/lec27.pdf); [Princeton COS 511](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spring13/cos511/scribe_notes/0212.pdf)). The conjunction example is the cleanest finite-class walk-through. Each of **n** Boolean variables can appear positive, negated, or absent, so **|H|=3^n** ([Dietterich](https://www.site.uottawa.ca/~stan/csi5387/Dietterich-PAC.pdf)). With **n=10**, **|H|=3^{10}=59,049**, **ln|H|=10 ln 3≈10.986**, **ln(1/0.05)=ln 20≈2.996**, and **m≥(10.986+2.996)/0.05≈280** ([Dietterich](https://www.site.uottawa.ca/~stan/csi5387/Dietterich-PAC.pdf); [ITK](https://www.itk.ilstu.edu//faculty/mecalif/ITK385/Spring2010/slides/385_2010_02_08.pdf)). Making **δ** ten times smaller adds only about **2.3/ε** examples, while halving **ε** roughly doubles the requirement ([Dietterich](https://www.site.uottawa.ca/~stan/csi5387/Dietterich-PAC.pdf)). A different “bad-literal” proof gives **(2n/ε)ln(2n/δ)**, which is about **2,396** for **n=10**, **ε=δ=0.05**; both are valid upper bounds, and neither is the true minimum ([NJU ML lecture 2](http://www.lamda.nju.edu.cn/mlt2023/Slides/chapter_2.pdf)). The published numbers are sufficient, not necessary: the same source calls them “a gross over-estimate in practice” ([ITK](https://www.itk.ilstu.edu//faculty/mecalif/ITK385/Spring2010/slides/385_2010_02_08.pdf)).

Why must **H** be chosen at all? The no-free-lunch theorem says that for any learning algorithm and any sample size **m<|X|/2**, there is a distribution on which the algorithm fails badly — true error at least **1/8** with probability at least **1/7** — even though some perfect function exists ([UML §5.1](https://vdoc.pub/documents/understanding-machine-learning-from-theory-to-algorithms-dbej70s1des0)). The proof puts **2m** points aside; the learner sees only **m**, and there are **2^{2m}** possible labellings, so whichever answer it gives off-sample, some world punishes it ([UML §5.1](https://vdoc.pub/documents/understanding-machine-learning-from-theory-to-algorithms-dbej70s1des0)). Restricting **H** is therefore logically necessary, not optional. A larger **H** reduces approximation error but increases estimation error; a smaller **H** does the opposite, which is the bias–complexity tradeoff ([IAIO training chapter](https://iaio-official.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/IAIO-Training-Eljakim-Schrijvers.pdf); [Welke](http://pwelke.de/SeminarLearningTheory/s03_ErrorDecomposition.html)).

## What PAC does not promise: computation, worst cases, and deep learning

PAC learning's statistical definition constrains the number of examples, not the running time. A class is efficiently PAC learnable only if the learner's running time is polynomial in the input size **n**, the target description size **size(c)**, **1/ε**, and **1/δ** ([Oxford CLT notes](http://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/people/varun.kanade/teaching/CLT-MT2022/lectures/CLT.pdf)). Computational hardness is a separate obstacle: proper learning of DNF by conjunctions is NP-hard, and allowing a richer output representation can make an otherwise hard problem easy ([Feldman, Hardness of Proper Learning](http://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~vitaly/papers/PittValiant88-e.pdf); [Greiner](https://www2.cs.uh.edu/~ceick/ML/Greiner-PAC-Learning.pdf)). The choice of representation therefore has a dramatic effect on computational complexity, which is why “is class **C** PAC learnable?” must always specify “by which hypothesis class?” ([Feldman](http://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~vitaly/papers/PittValiant88-e.pdf)).

The bounds are worst-case over distributions and samples, so they are not good for predicting learning curves. Even an exact sample-complexity calculation would overestimate typical error on a particular target and distribution; distribution-specific repairs are fragile if the actual distribution differs slightly; and classical bounds may contain overblown constants ([AAAI 1990](https://aaaipress.org/Papers/AAAI/1990/AAAI90-163.pdf)). The union bound is “loose but works,” and Chebyshev-type bounds are generally poor ([CMU 10-315](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~10315-s20/lectures/10315_Sp20_Lecture_25_Learning_Theory_inked.pdf); [Wikipedia, Chebyshev's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Chebyshev%27s_inequality&oldid=509452739)). A sample size below **m(ε,δ)** does not prove training will fail; it says only that the worst-case analysis no longer applies ([Hanveiga, Remark 13](https://hanveiga.com/ml_lecture_notes_2022_v0.0.pdf)). The formulas should be read as qualitative laws — which classes are learnable, and how cost scales with **d**, **ε**, and **δ** — not as literal data-size forecasts ([AAAI 1990](https://aaaipress.org/Papers/AAAI/1990/AAAI90-163.pdf)).

The sharpest scope problem is deep learning. State-of-the-art convolutional networks can fit a random labelling of the training data, and this happens even with explicit regularization and even when true images are replaced by random noise; conventional explanations based on model-family properties or regularization fail to explain why large networks generalize ([Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht & Vinyals, ICLR 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03530)). PAC-style uniform convergence over a fixed hypothesis class cannot be the whole explanation of that behaviour. The fixed-class, i.i.d., worst-case framework answers a different question from the one practitioners ask, even though its qualitative lessons about sample size and class complexity remain foundational ([AAAI 1990](https://aaaipress.org/Papers/AAAI/1990/AAAI90-163.pdf); [Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03530)).

## Conclusion

PAC learning gives a precise meaning to “enough data”: with probability **1−δ**, the learner's true error is within **ε** of the best achievable error in its chosen class, or within **ε** of zero in the realizable case. The engine is a concentration inequality plus a union bound. Hoeffding turns one hypothesis's training error into a sample mean with an exponential tail; the union bound pays a factor **|H|** for searching the class; solving the resulting inequality gives sample sizes that are logarithmic in **|H|** and quadratic in **1/ε**. For infinite classes, VC dimension and Sauer's lemma replace raw counting, and the fundamental theorem identifies finite VC dimension with statistical learnability.

The practical reading is equally important. Restricting the hypothesis class is unavoidable because no-free-lunch theorems forbid universal learning, and the bias–complexity tradeoff is a mathematical necessity rather than an empirical curiosity. The bounds are worst-case, often loose, and indifferent to computation in their statistical form; efficient PAC learning adds a separate polynomial-time requirement, and proper learning can be NP-hard. Most importantly for modern practice, PAC theory does not explain why heavily over-parameterised networks generalise while fitting random labels. It remains the right language for asking which classes are learnable and how sample cost scales, not for reading a precise example count off a formula.
