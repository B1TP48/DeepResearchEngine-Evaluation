# PAC Learning Turns Examples Into Provable Guarantees

Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) learning asks how many labeled examples a computer needs before the rule it learns is reliably close to right. Leslie Valiant introduced the framework in 1984, and [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1968.1972) records it as a theory of learning that needs no explicit programming. This report does not reproduce Valiant's own formal definition from the original paper. For a finite set of candidate rules, the core result is that **m ≥ (1/ε)(ln|H| + ln(1/δ))** labeled examples suffice to guarantee, with probability at least 1 − δ, that the chosen rule has error at most ε ([CSC 665 PAC notes](https://zcc1307.github.io/courses/csc665fa19/notes/pac.pdf)). Here |H| is the number of candidate rules, and ε and δ are the two tolerances explained below. The derivation needs only three ideas: independent events multiply their chances, a failure by any one of many rules is a failure of the whole, and logarithms turn products into sums. Extending the result to infinite families requires a counting measure called VC dimension, and the **sample size then scales as d/ε**, where d is that dimension ([Princeton COS-511 Lecture 3](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~rlivni/cos511/lectures/lect3.pdf)). These guarantees rest on assumptions that practical systems often break, and the bounds are very loose for modern deep networks. The sources are clear on the mathematics but disagree about how much practical guidance the theory gives.

## Four terms define what the guarantee promises

A **hypothesis** is one candidate rule that maps an input, such as an email, to a predicted label, such as spam or not spam. A **hypothesis class** H is the family of rules the learner is allowed to choose from. The **error** of a rule h is the fraction of future inputs it gets wrong, and this true error is written err(h, D), where D is the unknown distribution that generates the data ([CSC 665 PAC notes](https://zcc1307.github.io/courses/csc665fa19/notes/pac.pdf)). The tolerance **ε** (epsilon) is the largest error the learner is allowed to accept. The confidence parameter **δ** (delta) is the chance that the learner fails, so "probably" means success with probability 1 − δ, and "approximately correct" means error at most ε ([CSC 665 PAC notes](https://zcc1307.github.io/courses/csc665fa19/notes/pac.pdf)). The sample size **m** is the number of labeled examples the learner receives. In the **realizable** setting, a rule with zero error exists inside H. In the **agnostic** setting, no such rule is assumed, and the learner must compete with the best rule in H ([Mohri et al., Foundations of Machine Learning, Ch. 2](https://www.jeffreyheinz.net/classes/18S/materials/Mohri%20Rostamizadeh%20Talwalk%202012%20Foundations%20of%20Machine%20Learning%20Chap%202.pdf)).

The first formula concerns one "bad" rule, meaning one whose true error is above ε. Suppose we test it on m examples and it gets every one right. Each example is labeled correctly with probability 1 − err(h). Because the examples are **independent** (one example tells us nothing about another), the chance that all m are right is the product of the individual chances, (1 − err(h))^m. Since err(h) > ε, this is less than (1 − ε)^m. A standard inequality finishes the step: 1 − x ≤ e^(−x) for any x. You can see this from the curves: the straight line 1 − x always sits below the exponential e^(−x). Applying it gives

**P(bad rule looks perfect) ≤ (1 − ε)^m ≤ e^(−εm)** ([CSC 665 PAC notes](https://zcc1307.github.io/courses/csc665fa19/notes/pac.pdf)).

The result is exponential in m. Each extra example multiplies the chance of being fooled by a fixed factor below 1, so a misleading rule is rapidly unlikely to survive a long test.

## Summing over candidate rules turns one guarantee into a family guarantee

A learner usually cannot know in advance which rule is bad, so the guarantee must cover every rule in H at once. The **union bound** says that the chance that at least one of several events happens is at most the sum of their chances: P(A₁ or A₂ or …) ≤ P(A₁) + P(A₂) + …. It holds even when the events overlap, so it is a safe, if sometimes generous, step ([Illinois CS 574 Lecture 24 notes](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs574/fa2025/notes/lec24-ai-converted.pdf)). Applying it to the |H| bad rules gives P(some bad rule looks perfect) ≤ |H| · e^(−εm).

To guarantee a failure chance of at most δ, we require |H| · e^(−εm) ≤ δ. Dividing by |H| gives e^(−εm) ≤ δ/|H|. Taking natural logarithms (which turn products into sums, since ln(ab) = ln a + ln b, and reverse the inequality direction for negative exponents) gives −εm ≤ ln δ − ln|H|. Multiplying by −1/ε and rearranging:

**m ≥ (1/ε)(ln|H| + ln(1/δ))** ([CSC 665 PAC notes](https://zcc1307.github.io/courses/csc665fa19/notes/pac.pdf)).

The term ln|H| is the price of choosing among many rules. Because it is a logarithm, doubling the number of candidate rules adds only a fixed amount to the sample requirement. Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David state the corollary that every finite class is PAC learnable with m ≤ log(|H|/δ)/ε, and this is the same expression written with a single logarithm ([Understanding Machine Learning, Cambridge](https://www.cs.huji.ac.il/~shais/UnderstandingMachineLearning/understanding-machine-learning-theory-algorithms.pdf)).

A concrete case from Mohri et al. shows the formula at work. Conjunctions of at most n Boolean literals (rules such as "age over 30 and member") form a finite class with |H| = 3ⁿ. With n = 10, ε = 0.1 and δ = 0.02, the bound gives **m ≥ 149 examples** ([Mohri et al., Example 2.2](https://www.jeffreyheinz.net/classes/18S/materials/Mohri%20Rostamizadeh%20Talwalk%202012%20Foundations%20of%20Machine%20Learning%20Chap%202.pdf)). Check the arithmetic: 10·ln 3 ≈ 10.99 and ln 50 ≈ 3.91, which sum to about 14.9, and dividing by 0.1 gives 149.

## Counting patterns with VC dimension handles infinite families

Many useful classes are infinite, such as all rectangles on a plane, so ln|H| is undefined. The fix is to count the distinct ways a class can label a set of points. A set of points is **shattered** when the class can produce every possible labeling of them, and the **VC dimension** d is the size of the largest set that can be shattered ([Princeton COS-511 Lecture 3](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~rlivni/cos511/lectures/lect3.pdf)). Axis-aligned rectangles have VC dimension 4, and halfspaces in ℝᵈ have VC dimension d + 1 ([Princeton COS-511 Lecture 3](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~rlivni/cos511/lectures/lect3.pdf)). Thresholds have VC dimension 1 and intervals have VC dimension 2 ([CMU PAC slides](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~mgormley/courses/10601-s17/slides/lecture28-pac.pdf)).

The **growth function** C[m] is the largest number of distinct labelings a class can produce on any m points ([Balcan, CMU 10-806 notes](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~avrim/ML07/lect-09-21-23.pdf)). The **Sauer–Shelah lemma** says that if the VC dimension is d, the number of labelings on m points is at most the sum of binomial coefficients C(m,i) for i from 0 to d, and this sum is at most (em/d)^d for m ≥ d ([TTIC 31290 Lecture 6](https://home.ttic.edu/~avrim/MLforAD25/Lecture6.pdf); [Balcan, CMU 10-806](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~avrim/ML07/lect-09-21-23.pdf)). The sum counts ways to pick up to d of the m positions, and the bound replaces what would be 2^m labelings with a polynomial in m. The proof is an induction using Pascal's identity C(m,i) = C(m−1,i) + C(m−1,i−1), which splits the class by whether its members agree on the last point ([TTIC 31290 Lecture 6](https://home.ttic.edu/~avrim/MLforAD25/Lecture6.pdf)).

The full realizable argument uses a **ghost sample**: a second, independent set of examples used only as a thought experiment. The learner's bad event is compared with an event on the ghost sample, and a random swap of paired examples, decided by a fair coin, leaves the sample distribution unchanged. For a fixed rule, the chance that the swap pattern separates the two halves this way is at most 2^(−εm/2). A union bound over the C[2m] distinct patterns on the combined 2m points then gives the requirement 2·C[2m]·2^(−εm/2) ≤ δ ([Balcan, CMU 10-806](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~avrim/ML07/lect-09-21-23.pdf)). Replacing C[2m] by Sauer–Shelah gives m ≥ (4/ε)[d·ln(2em/d) + ln(2/δ)]. Because m sits inside a logarithm, one further inequality, ln x ≤ αx − ln α − 1, moves it outside. The result is:

**m ≥ (8/ε)[d·ln(16/ε) + ln(2/δ)]** ([Balcan, CMU 10-806, Theorem 2](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~avrim/ML07/lect-09-21-23.pdf)).

This is the form (1/ε)(d·log(1/ε) + log(1/δ)) that the question names. Different sources state the same result with different logarithmic factors. The Princeton lecture gives O((d/ε)·log(1/δ)) for the realizable case ([Princeton COS-511 Lecture 3](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~rlivni/cos511/lectures/lect3.pdf)). A lower bound of Ω(d/ε + (1/ε)·log(1/δ)) applies to any PAC learner, and the log(1/ε) gap in the upper bound was closed by Hanneke's 2016 learner, which matches the lower bound up to constants ([Sample Complexity of Robust Learning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.12054)). The result the framework is best known for is that a class is PAC learnable exactly when its VC dimension is finite. Blumer, Ehrenfeucht, Haussler and Warmuth established this in 1989 in the *Journal of the ACM* ([ACM DL, Blumer et al. 1989](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/76359.76371)).

For the rectangle case, the proof is geometric and does not depend on counting. The learner returns the tightest rectangle around the positive examples. Its error region splits into four strips, each with probability at least ε/4, and missing all four has chance at most 4·e^(−mε/4). Setting this to δ gives **m ≥ (4/ε)·log(4/δ)** ([Mohri et al., Ch. 2](https://www.jeffreyheinz.net/classes/18S/materials/Mohri%20Rostamizadeh%20Talwalk%202012%20Foundations%20of%20Machine%20Learning%20Chap%202.pdf)). The same exponential-to-logarithm rearrangement as before gives the answer. Mohri et al. note that this geometric step does not carry over easily to other classes ([Mohri et al., Ch. 2](https://www.jeffreyheinz.net/classes/18S/materials/Mohri%20Rostamizadeh%20Talwalk%202012%20Foundations%20of%20Machine%20Learning%20Chap%202.pdf)).

## When the right rule is not in the family, the square-root price appears

In practice the true rule often is not in H. The agnostic setting then asks the learner to match the best rule in H, and the guarantee must hold for every rule simultaneously. The tool is **Hoeffding's inequality**. For independent values Z_i between a and b, the average deviates from its mean by more than t with chance at most exp(−2nt²/(b−a)²) for each tail ([CS229 Hoeffding notes, John Duchi](https://cs229.stanford.edu/extra-notes/hoeffding.pdf)). For 0/1 errors, b − a = 1. The two-sided form for one rule is P(|average error − true error| > t) ≤ 2·exp(−2mt²) ([TTIC 31290 Lecture 6](https://home.ttic.edu/~avrim/MLforAD25/Lecture6.pdf)).

To see the logic in plain terms, take a coin. If we flip it m times and count heads, the fraction of heads is an average of independent 0/1 values, and Hoeffding says it rarely strays far from the true bias. For a fixed guess tested on 500 tosses with δ = 0.02, Mohri et al. give a deviation of **about 0.048** between the true and observed rates ([Mohri et al., Example 2.6](https://www.jeffreyheinz.net/classes/18S/materials/Mohri%20Rostamizadeh%20Talwalk%202012%20Foundations%20of%20Machine%20Learning%20Chap%202.pdf)).

Now apply the union bound over all |H| rules. Set the two-sided failure chance to δ: 2|H|·e^(−2mt²) = δ. Then e^(−2mt²) = δ/(2|H|), so 2mt² = ln(2|H|/δ) = ln|H| + ln(2/δ). Solving for the deviation t gives the square-root term:

**R(h) ≤ R̂(h) + √((ln|H| + ln(2/δ))/(2m))**, which holds for every h in H with probability at least 1 − δ. Here R(h) is the true error and R̂(h) is the error on the training sample ([Mohri et al., Thm 2.2](https://www.jeffreyheinz.net/classes/18S/materials/Mohri%20Rostamizadeh%20Talwalk%202012%20Foundations%20of%20Machine%20Learning%20Chap%202.pdf)). The derivation above is an inference from the cited Hoeffding and union-bound statements; no source in the notes writes this exact line as a single step.

To require that the deviation be at most ε, we need (ln|H| + ln(2/δ))/(2m) ≤ ε², which rearranges to **m ≥ (ln|H| + ln(2/δ))/(2ε²)** ([Illinois CS 574 Lecture 24 notes](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs574/fa2025/notes/lec24-ai-converted.pdf)). The denominator 2ε² shows the cost of the agnostic setting. Halving ε roughly quadruples the sample needed, whereas in the realizable case halving ε only doubles it. For a class of VC dimension d, the agnostic sample size is stated as O((d/ε²)·log(1/δ)) ([Princeton COS-511 Lecture 3](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~rlivni/cos511/lectures/lect3.pdf)), while another source gives C₁(d + log(1/δ))/ε² ≤ N ≤ C₂(d + log(1/δ))/ε² ([MIT GradML](https://gradml.mit.edu/supervised/learnability_and_vc/)). These are consistent statements with different logarithmic terms, not a contradiction. The constants also vary by source, because some use deviation ε and others ε/2. The sources differ on these constants, so they should not be merged.

The table below collects the main formulas, their plain meaning, and where each is derived.

| Formula | Plain meaning | Derived in |
|---|---|---|
| P(bad rule looks perfect) ≤ e^(−εm) | A wrong rule rarely survives many correct-looking tests ([CSC 665 PAC notes](https://zcc1307.github.io/courses/csc665fa19/notes/pac.pdf)) | Section 1 |
| m ≥ (1/ε)(ln\|H\| + ln(1/δ)) | Enough examples to cover every rule in a finite family ([CSC 665 PAC notes](https://zcc1307.github.io/courses/csc665fa19/notes/pac.pdf)) | Section 2 |
| m ≥ (4/ε)·log(4/δ) | Rectangle learning, with four error strips ([Mohri et al., Ch. 2](https://www.jeffreyheinz.net/classes/18S/materials/Mohri%20Rostamizadeh%20Talwalk%202012%20Foundations%20of%20Machine%20Learning%20Chap%202.pdf)) | Section 3 |

## Assumptions, limits and disputes

The guarantees hold only under stated assumptions, and the sources agree that practical systems often break them. The two core assumptions are that the target is realizable (a perfect rule exists in the class) and that the data are i.i.d., meaning independent draws from a single distribution ([arXiv 1911.12603](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.12603)). The same paper says these assumptions "are not naturally held in practice" ([arXiv 1911.12603](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.12603)). Realizability is described as "a rather strong assumption in practice," because a hypothesis is usually not fully consistent with the training set ([arXiv 2412.10513](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.10513)). The agnostic extension handles this, but its guarantee is weaker, because the learner is only compared against the best rule in the class ([Medium, "Agnostic PAC learning"](https://medium.com/@majdii.karim/foundational-machine-learning-agnostic-pac-learning-54aadb5b7fce)).

The i.i.d. assumption is the most fragile in deployment. One interactive article on PAC learning shows what happens when linear drift is added to the data, and states the assumption "is frequently incorrect in practice" ([JOVI, Cashman](https://www.journalovi.org/2024-Cashman-PAC-learning-game/)). Under distribution shift, a 2025 paper states that if the shifted distribution is arbitrary, PAC learning is impossible ([arXiv 2510.08335](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08335)). The guarantee therefore covers only the distribution it was trained on. Another paper reports that a PAC-style prediction-set guarantee "fails under label shift" ([arXiv 2310.12964](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.12964)). Note that one GeeksforGeeks page says PAC learning "often assumes that the data distribution is known," which conflicts with the distribution-free definition in [arXiv 2308.12054](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.12054); the primary-source reading is the distribution-free one.

The theory is also contested on two fronts. First, it is mathematically subtle: a 2026 preprint shows that finite VC dimension implies consistent PAC learnability for Borel classes only under a measurability condition, and constructs a Borel class of VC dimension one with a consistent learning rule that is not PAC ([arXiv 2608.30246](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.30246)). Second, its numerical bounds are weak in practice. Classic VC-based bounds for deep networks were reported as "vacuous by orders of magnitudes" on ImageNet-scale problems ([arXiv 1804.05862](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.05862)). In a 2017 study, PAC-Bayes bounds gave test-error bounds of 16–22%, while Chernoff bounds on held-out data gave around 3%, and the authors call their bounds "far from nonvacuous but loose" ([arXiv 1703.11008](https://arxiv.org/html/1703.11008v1); [arXiv 1703.11008 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1703.11008)). These results concern 2017-era networks, and the sources do not report current bounds for today's large models. Zhang et al. (ICLR 2017) found that state-of-the-art convolutional networks easily fit random labels, which makes capacity-based explanations inadequate ([ACM CACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3446776)).

The sources also disagree about the practical value of the framework. A 2025 thesis judges computational learning theory "of limited utility" for estimating how many extra labeled examples a project needs ([arXiv 2610.02610](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.02610)). An arXiv preprint argues that the theory requires training sets "so large, that it would make the learning practically useless" ([arXiv 1807.10681](https://arxiv.org/html/1807.10681)). Wikipedia, by contrast, reports that the theory has led to practical algorithms ([Wikipedia, "Computational learning theory"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_learning_theory)). A Lacuna page claims a sequential procedure can be "up to 27 times more efficient in practice than standard PAC theory suggests," but this rests on the authors' own synthetic benchmarks ([Lacuna, "Practical PAC Learning"](https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/work/practical-pac-learning/wrk_001fc9b0d5a47421d750a8f3f493d71c)). The sources do not resolve how much practice has been shaped by the theory, and none documents a production system that chose its training-set size with a PAC bound.

A further caveat concerns the historical model. A 2026 paper by Hanneke notes that Valiant's 1984 paper is widely credited with introducing PAC learning, but that the model it formulates differs from the PAC model in use today, and that without a polynomial requirement on the sample size the model becomes vacuous ([arXiv 2605.13840](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13840)). Readers who want Valiant's original definition should consult the paper itself.

## Conclusion

The central lesson of PAC learning is that learning from examples can come with a mathematical guarantee, and that the guarantee is governed by a small number of quantities: the tolerance ε, the confidence δ, and a measure of complexity, either ln|H| for a finite family or the VC dimension d for an infinite one. The formulas are derived from three simple ideas: independent examples multiply their chances, a failure by any rule counts as a failure, and logarithms turn exponential decay into a sample count. The agnostic version costs a factor of 1/ε in the sample size, and the square-root term shows why noisy or imperfect targets demand far more data.

The practical message is more cautious. The guarantees are distribution-free and worst-case, so they are often loose, and they apply only when examples are independent, drawn from one distribution, and labeled by a rule the learner can represent. Where those conditions fail, as with drifting data or label shift, the stated error bound is no longer a valid promise. Readers should therefore treat PAC theory as a clear account of why and how learning is possible and of which quantities matter, rather than as a tool for setting exact training-set sizes.
