# States Fall When Money, Elites and Armies Fail

Across 1789, 1917 and 1979 the trigger is never misery alone, and no single variable explains all three. The recurring sequence runs from a state that cannot raise or allocate revenue, through a fractured elite, to mass mobilisation catalysed by a price shock, and it ends when the armed forces stop obeying. France fits hardest on the fiscal side — a **140 million** deficit in 1787 and an **88%** rise in the price of bread in 1789 ([Wikipedia, Causes of the French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causes_of_the_French_Revolution)) — Russia on the coercive side, and Iran hardly at all, because the 1979 revolution "occurred in a country experiencing relative prosperity" and lacked the customary causes such as war defeat or financial crisis ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)). That anomaly is the most theoretically important finding: a cohesive ideological leadership can substitute for fiscal collapse, which is why theories built on France and Russia had to be amended before they could absorb Iran. Durability does not track violence — Russia's civil war killed **7–12 million** ([Wikipedia, Russian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Civil_War)) and produced a state that lasted roughly seven decades, while Iran's faster, less lethal seizure has held **47 years** to 2026 while facing repeated mass protest ([Britannica, Iranian Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/Iranian-Revolution)). The sections below define terms, work through the seven dimensions, and close with a five-variable framework applied to the 2026 landscape.

## "Revolution" has two meanings, and the start dates follow from which one is used

The three dates in the question are conventional regime dates, not the boundaries of the crises that produced them. Britannica dates the French Revolution **1787–1799** and calls 1789 its "first climax" ([Britannica, French Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/French-Revolution)), while Wikipedia's infobox runs **5 May 1789 – 9 November 1799** ([Wikipedia, French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution)). Neither is factually wrong: a process definition, which treats the revolution as the unravelling of the absolutist order, starts with Calonne's Assembly of Notables; an institutional definition, which treats it as the existence of a new sovereign body, starts with the Estates-General. The dispute is live in the specialist literature — one *Journal of Modern History* article argues for "the Revolution of 1786" on the grounds that Calonne's decision to convoke the Notables fixed the rupture ([Journal of Modern History](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/241372)), and an Oxford monograph calls 1787 the "overture" and "first link in an unbroken chain" while still naming the event "the Revolution of 1789" ([OUP Canada](https://oupcanada.com/catalog/9780199585779.html)). A monograph on 1789 locates the break in the transfer of "effective sovereignty … from king to nation" between May and November 1789 ([Age of Revolutions](https://ageofrevolutions.com/2020/05/11/sovereignty-at-stake-in-1789-the-french-revolution-begins/)). For cross-case comparison the institutional date is the defensible choice, because it matches how 1917 and 1979 are dated; the onset of the old regime's crisis should be labelled separately.

The Russian and Iranian cases carry the same ambiguity. Britannica defines the Russian Revolution as **two revolutions in 1917**, February and October ([Britannica, Russian Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/Russian-Revolution)), while a fuller periodisation runs to **25 October 1922** and folds in civil war and dual power ([Wikipedia, Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution)). Iran's infobox span is **7 January 1978 – 11 February 1979** ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)), yet sustained demonstrations "date back to late 1977" ([Oxford Bibliographies](https://www.oxfordbibliographies.com/display/document/obo-9780195390155/obo-9780195390155-0204.xml)), and the Islamic Republic was only consolidated with Khomeini as supreme leader in December 1979 ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)). Read as start dates, 1979 is the weakest of the three.

## Two revolutions of scarcity, one of disappointed prosperity

France's preconditions combine fiscal privilege with subsistence shock. The first two estates were **2%** of the population while "all of the many types of taxes were paid by the third estate" ([Wikipedia, Causes of the French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causes_of_the_French_Revolution)); the bourgeoisie was **7.7%** of the population but took **39.1%** of national income ([Wikipedia, Causes of the French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causes_of_the_French_Revolution)); peasants were **82–88%** of the population but owned **35%** of the land, and in 1789 real wages fell **25%** as bread rose **88%** ([Wikipedia, Causes of the French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causes_of_the_French_Revolution)). Britannica adds that a bad 1788 harvest came "on top of a long period of economic difficulties", that France held **26 million** inhabitants in 1789, and that about half the peasantry "remained poor or landless" ([Britannica, French Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/French-Revolution)). The political turn came when taxation of the privileged was attempted and refused. These price and income series are aggregated in a secondary reference work rather than drawn from primary fiscal accounts, so they indicate scale rather than exact magnitudes.

Russia's profile is agrarian grievance plus compressed industrialisation. Emancipated peasants in 1861 "still resented paying redemption payments", and land inequality is asserted at **1.5% of the population owning 25% of the land** — a sentence the source itself flags as unsourced ([Wikipedia, Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution)). St Petersburg nearly doubled from **1,033,600 to 1,905,600** between 1890 and 1910, a 1904 survey found an average of **16 people** per apartment, and by 1914 **40%** of workers were in factories of 1,000 or more ([Wikipedia, Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution)). Average annual strikes rose from **6** (1862–69) to **176** (1895–1905) ([Wikipedia, Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution)). The February explosion itself was about food: on 23 February 1917 "no fewer than 130,000 men were picketing" Petrograd and "the primary concern of protesters remained the food shortages" ([Britannica, Russian Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/Russian-Revolution)). No grain, requisition or calorie series is available in the sources, so the wartime hunger crisis is documented by behaviour, not by nutrition data.

Iran inverts the pattern. Contemporaries attributed the upheaval to a backlash against forced Westernisation, the 1953 coup, "a rise in expectations created by the 1973 oil revenue windfall", an "overly ambitious economic program" and "anger over a short, sharp economic contraction in 1977–1978" ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)). Inflation reached **50% a year** ([Time, "Nation: Nobody Influences Me!"](https://content.time.com)), and the White Revolution (1963–1979) — land reform, women's suffrage, a literacy corps — "was built especially to weaken those classes that supported the traditional system" ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)). This is relative deprivation rather than absolute misery: state-sponsored modernisation dissolved the clergy's, bazaar's and landlords' social anchors while creating new urban poor and an educated middle class. The one explicit inflation figure comes from contemporaneous journalism; no central-bank or IMF series was available, and income-distribution and migration data are absent.

Ideologically, each framework explained a different grievance. In France the Enlightenment supplied the vocabulary — Montesquieu, Voltaire, Rousseau spread through "societies of thought", Masonic lodges and reading rooms — though Britannica concedes it is "uncertain … whether revolution would have come without the added presence of a political crisis" ([Britannica, French Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/French-Revolution)). Censer traces a shift in fashion: contemporaries believed "radical ideas" were "at the bottom of this upheaval", later "upstaged by social explanations", with natural law re-emerging as the justification for radicalisation ([Censer, Journal of Social History](https://academic.oup.com)). Furet is reported as holding that Enlightenment influence "only played a part after the Revolution had begun" ([Wikipedia, Causes of the French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causes_of_the_French_Revolution)). In Russia, Bolshevism "considers itself to be a continuation and development of Marxism" ([Brill, Bolshevism](https://brill.com)) and the party "regarded the class of factory workers as the 'bearers of the future'" ([HathiTrust](https://babel.hathitrust.org)). In Iran the framework was Shi'a Islamist: an Islamic republic "based on the concept of Velâyat-e Faqih" ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)), mobilising through "the theodicy of suffering, the martyrdom of Imam Husayn, and Shiite millenarianism" ([Parsa, Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/4/article/262220/)), with Khomeini cast as Husayn against the Shah-as-Yazid ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)). But Britannica is explicit that the anti-Shah groups "did not have … a cohesive political ideology or vision" ([Britannica, Iranian Revolution: Quick Facts](https://www.britannica.com/facts/Iranian-Revolution)).

## Broad coalitions make these revolutions; narrow cores inherit them

None of the three was a single-organisation revolution, and in each the body that mobilised was broader than the body that ruled afterwards. In France the Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly on **17 June 1789**, and from 25 September 1788 an average of **25** new political pamphlets appeared each week ([Wikipedia, French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution)). The Jacobin Club built a nationwide network of affiliated societies — "more than a thousand" by one account ([Le Club des Amis de la Constitution](https://www.parisrevolutionnaire.org)), about **6,000** by another ([Club des Jacobins](https://www.parisrevolutionnaire.org)) — while by August 1792 the sans-culottes dominated all **forty-eight** Paris sections ([The Sections of Paris](https://revolution.chnm.org)), and the Montagnards "used the insurrectionary organs created by the sans-culottes for their own purposes" ([The Making of an Insurrection](https://books.google.com.sg)). Power passed to a Robespierre-dominated Committee of Public Safety after the June 1793 insurrection ([Wikipedia, French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution)).

Russia shows the same narrowing in compressed form. The Petrograd Soviet passed to Bolshevik control by September 1917, after which Trotsky directed the October insurrection ([May to October 1917](https://www.taylorfrancis.com)); the party's Central Committee voted **10–2** on 10 October to make armed uprising "the order of the day" ([The October Revolution](https://soviethistory.msu.edu)), and the Military Revolutionary Committee, created on 12 (25) October ([День Октябрьской революции](https://www.prlib.ru)), served as the command centre. Iran's mobilisation was the broadest of all: "using mosques as safe gathering places and led by a segment of the clergy" ([Social Origins of the Iranian Revolution](https://books.google.com.sg)), with the bazaar "a crucial component of the social movement" ([Collective Action with and without Islam](https://scholar.google.co.th)), clerical populists coordinating Tehran's strike committees into a **Komiteh-e Asnaf** ([They – more than any other group](https://orient.sci.am)), students active from 1977 ([Along with the ulama](https://www.archive.org)), and a rolling strike from Abadan's oil workers that reached **1.5 million** workers by late 1978 ([Workers' Iran](https://www.workers-iran.org)). Khomeini's Revolutionary Council was formed on **12 January 1979** to manage the transition ([Al Arabiya](https://english.alarabiya.net)) — and the clergy, not the bazaar or the left, took the state.

Turning points follow the same logic of widening then narrowing. France's hinges are the Bastille, 10 August 1792, the Terror's institutionalisation and Thermidor, with about **16,000** sentenced and executed by the Revolutionary Tribunal ([Wikipedia, French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution)). Russia's decisive turn is not February but the Kornilov affair, "the seminal political event of the summer of 1917" ([The Kornilov affair](https://www.taylorfrancis.com)). Iran's are the Cinema Rex fire of **19 August 1978**, which killed around **400** and which much of the public blamed on SAVAK ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)), and Black Friday on **8 September 1978**, when martial law was declared and security forces killed at least **100** in Jaleh Square ([Timeline of the Iranian revolution](https://www.dailymail.com)).

## Foreign patrons and disloyal soldiers decide who wins

Every regime oscillated between concession and repression and depended on external patrons with their own stakes. Louis XVI "often backed down when faced with opposition from conservative elements within the nobility" ([Wikipedia, French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution)); Leopold II and Frederick William II issued the Declaration of Pillnitz in August 1791 "in consultation with emigrant French nobles" ([Foreign Intervention](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-worldhistory/chapter/22-4-3-foreign-intervention/)), and the July 1792 Brunswick Manifesto, drafted by the émigré Prince de Condé, threatened Paris with martial law — and "helped further spur the increasingly radical French Revolution" ([Foreign Intervention](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-worldhistory/chapter/22-4-3-foreign-intervention/)). Britain subsidised the coalition, with Prussia supplying **62,400** men against **50,000 pounds sterling per month** ([Sur le continent, le zèle n'était pas aussi grand](https://www.dominiopublico.gov.br)). French officers emigrated throughout 1791–92, weakening the army and deepening patriot suspicion of it ([The New Cambridge Modern History](https://www.archive.org)); Marat's paper described "the almost total desertion of the officers" ([Marat, L'Ami du peuple](https://babel.hathitrust.org)).

The Provisional Government had no counter-revolutionary instrument of its own. It suppressed the July Days and arrested Bolshevik leaders, forcing Lenin to flee to Finland ([The Petrograd Soviet](https://www.bbc.co.uk/bitesize)), then fell back on the army: Kornilov, backed by banking and industrial leaders ([Kornilov Affair](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/kornilov-affair/)), ordered the Savage Division against Petrograd on 27 August; Red Guards, the garrison and railway workers halted the trains, and Kerensky had to release Bolsheviks from prison to help defeat him ([Kornilov Affair](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/kornilov-affair/)). Germany's role was direct: Berlin brokered Lenin's wartime journey "to enable Lenin to foment revolution and take Russia off the front" ([Reviews: Lenin on the Train](https://polarisweb.arlingtonlibrary.org)), and in 1921 Eduard Bernstein claimed Germany delivered more than **50,000,000 marks** to Lenin ([Tells How Germans Gave Lenin a Start](https://www.nytimes.com)) — a thesis whose scale and purpose remain disputed ([Октябрьская революция](https://ru.wikipedia.org)). No Allied intervention in the civil war, and no Comintern network, is documented in the sources available.

Iran's international dimension is longer-run and inverted. The **1953** CIA–MI6 coup against Mossadegh reinstated Mohammad Reza Pahlavi as an absolute monarch and "significantly increased US influence over Iran" ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)); SAVAK operated "with CIA assistance" ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)); and Carter's human-rights policy encouraged Iranians to "post open letters and petitions" ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)) while Western support waned as the Shah pushed OPEC prices up ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)). Coercive loyalty collapsed in sequence: martial law on 8 September 1978 ([Timeline of the Iranian revolution](https://www.dailymail.com)), military rule under General Azhari on 6 November 1978 ([The Shah's Departure](https://permanent.access.gpo.gov)), around **1,000 soldiers defecting each day** by January 1979 ([Dahl, PRIO Policy Brief 18/2016](https://cdn.cloud.prio.org/files/842c02be-8551-4444-8a44-bd4636ed6688/Dahl+-+Military+Defection+PRIO+Policy+Brief+18-2016.pdf)), and the army's revocation of allegiance on **11 February 1979** ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)). Dahl's cross-national finding gives this its weight: "non-violent campaigns that generate military defection are 46 times more likely to succeed than those that do not" ([PRIO Policy Brief 18/2016](https://cdn.cloud.prio.org/files/842c02be-8551-4444-8a44-bd4636ed6688/Dahl+-+Military+Defection+PRIO+Policy+Brief+18-2016.pdf)).

## Institutional legacies diverge more than the revolutions did

France produced constitutionalism plus administrative centralisation. The Constitution of 1791 created a unicameral Legislative Assembly with the Declaration of the Rights of Man as its preamble, after a motion for bicameralism was defeated **849–89** on 10 September 1789 ([Wikipedia, French Constitution of 1791](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Constitution_of_1791)), and the Revolution produced "three consecutive Constitutions in 1791, 1793 and 1795" ([Université Paris-Panthéon-Assas](https://rda.u-paris2.fr)). The sessions of **4–5 August 1789** abolished feudalism ([Wikipedia, French Constitution of 1791](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Constitution_of_1791)); the Church was reduced to "a bureau controlled by the government" ([Wikipedia, Influence of the French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influence_of_the_French_Revolution)); and the Napoleonic Code of **21 March 1804**, still extant with revisions, entrenched civil equality and became the model for most continental European and Latin American civil codes ([Britannica, Napoleonic Code](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Napoleonic-Code)). It also "strengthened property rights and patriarchal authority" and "rolled back some gains women had made during the Revolution" ([Britannica, Napoleonic Code](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Napoleonic-Code)). Tocqueville's point stands: administrative centralisation ran continuously from the Old Regime into the revolutionary years ([Wikipedia, Historiography of the French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography_of_the_French_Revolution)).

Russia built a party-state. The Bolshevik Party was renamed the Russian Communist Party, and "all politics and attitudes that were not strictly RCP were suppressed" on the premise that opposition was "counterrevolutionary" ([Wikipedia, History of Soviet Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Soviet_Russia_and_the_Soviet_Union_(1917%E2%80%931927))); the Cheka became the forerunner of the KGB ([Britannica, Cheka](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Cheka)). Collectivisation moved from **7.5%** of peasant households in October 1929 to **52.7%** by February 1930 ([Wikipedia, Collectivization in the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectivization_in_the_Soviet_Union)), and most historians treat industrialisation as "the decisive transformative event in Soviet history" ([Wikipedia, Industrialization in the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrialization_in_the_Soviet_Union)). The USSR dissolved into **15** independent states by **26 December 1991** ([Wikipedia, Dissolution of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union)).

Iran built dual sovereignty. Its constitution, adopted by referendum on **2–3 December 1979**, "combines elected institutions … with unelected institutions, including the Supreme Leader and Guardian Council" ([Wikipedia, Constitution of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Iran)) — although the preliminary draft resembled the French Fifth Republic and "made no reference to velayat-e faqih", which is itself a trace of coalition politics ([Wikipedia, Constitution of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Iran)). The IRGC's founding date is reported inconsistently: **April 1979** by Britannica ([Britannica, IRGC](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Islamic-Revolutionary-Guard-Corps)) and **5 May 1979**, with 6,000 initially enlisted, elsewhere ([Wikipedia, Aftermath of the Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aftermath_of_the_Iranian_Revolution)). The Cultural Revolution of **1980–1983** closed universities for three years, purged thousands of students and lecturers, mandated the hijab from age **6**, and drove out teachers and technocrats ([Wikipedia, Cultural Revolution in Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_Revolution_in_Iran)); the revolution "resulted in a massive exile that characterizes a large portion of Iranian diaspora" ([Wikipedia, Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution)). Iran's 2026 GDP is estimated at **$311.95 billion** nominal with unemployment at **21.9%** (2025), against an article flagged as needing updating ([Wikipedia, Economy of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Iran)).

## A five-variable framework flags elite succession, not mass uprising, as the 2026 pattern

The comparative literature supplies the components but not the synthesis. Skocpol's *States and Social Revolutions* builds on "France, Russia and China", making states "potentially autonomous from class controls" the unit of analysis ([Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/states-and-social-revolutions/)), combining "international pressures, peasants' insurrections, and the presence of a marginal elite" into "state incapacitation" ([TRACE, University of Tennessee](https://trace.tennessee.edu/)). She conceded that Iran "does not fit the model … very closely", citing three anomalies: rapid modernisation, a **300,000**-strong army rendered ineffective without foreign defeat, and a revolution "deliberately 'made' by a mass-based social movement" ([Understanding Society](https://undsoc.org/2014/02/11/skocpol-on-the-1979-revolution-in-iran/)). Tilly ties revolutions to state formation and defines the revolutionary situation as "multiple sovereignty" ([Bailey, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/)), but no application of his model to Iran surfaced. Goldstone's demographic-structural model assigns weight to "population changes, youth bulges, urbanization, elite divisions, and fiscal crises" ([University of Hertfordshire](https://library.herts.ac.uk/)) and now names "three recurring stressors": rising government debt, overproduction of elites, and mass grievance rooted in inequality — all three, he says, present today ([ECPS interview, 21 Oct 2025](https://www.populismstudies.org/professor-goldstone-the-worlds-descent-into-authoritarianism-may-trigger-a-revolutionary-movement-2/)). Brinton's stage model fits Iran poorly ([Journal of Islamic Revolution Studies](https://enghelab.maaref.ac.ir/)), and Shakibi's three-monarchy comparison argues the structural tradition misses what mattered most: the rulers' own "unwillingness to employ force at the proper moment" ([Wiley review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6563.2008.00233_75.x)).

The framework that best reconciles the three cases has five variables in sequence: fiscal and revenue stress; elite fracture or overproduction; a mobilisation catalyst in the form of a food or currency shock amplified by youth and urban concentration; the loyalty switch of the coercive apparatus; and the ideological and organisational capacity to convert a winning coalition into a governing one. The first four determine whether the regime falls; the fifth determines what replaces it. Iran 1979 shows that a strong fifth variable can compensate for a weak first, while Iran 2026 shows the reverse: protests "erupted on 28 December 2025 following a sharp currency collapse" ([Amnesty International](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/01/iran-deaths-and-injuries-rise-amid-renewed-cycle-of-protest/)) and became "nationwide upheaval" within three weeks ([UN DPPA briefing, 15 Jan 2026](https://dppa.un.org/en/asg-martha-pobee-briefs-security-council-nationwide-upheaval-and-human-rights-concerns-iran)), but the regime retained its coercive core. The evidence base favours price over general inflation as a predictor: food inflation "turns out to be a fairly reliable predictor of the beginning of the unfolding of revolutionary processes" while general inflation does not ([HSE/RAS, Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/14650123)).

Applied forward, the framework yields three scenario types rather than one. A **France-type fiscal-legitimacy collapse** fits low-income, food-import-dependent states with delegitimised elites and youth bulges; Bloomberg Economics' machine-learning unrest index, trained on **22 million** data points, ranks Nepal, Madagascar, Ethiopia, the Central African Republic, Angola, Guatemala, the Republic of the Congo and Malaysia highest for 2026, with the United States, Indonesia and Israel also rising ([Times Now](https://www.timesnownews.com/world/gen-z-uprisings-global-instability-hotspot-predictions-for-civil-unrest-in-2026-report-article-153351544)); its youth misery gauge puts Zimbabwe at a **152%** index rise and Argentina at **103%** ([Times Now](https://www.timesnownews.com/world/gen-z-uprisings-global-instability-hotspot-predictions-for-civil-unrest-in-2026-report-article-153351544)). A **Russia-type elite-succession crisis** fits personalist regimes where the leader is the arbitration mechanism: the NEST Centre told a UK parliamentary committee in October 2026 that Russia is "under greater pressure than at any time since Vladimir Putin's ascent", with three scenarios — natural regime death (most likely), palace coup, and popular uprising (least likely, and if it came, most likely from the radicalised pro-war community) ([PolicyMogul / NEST](https://policymogul.com/committee-publication/39891/new-eurasian-strategies-centre-rus0005-)). A **1979-type cross-class religious movement** has no clear 2026 analogue in the sources. Iran itself fits none of the three cleanly: after the U.S.–Israeli assassination of Khamenei and senior leaders, "the most likely outcome is that some residual version of Iran's revolutionary regime will remain intact, albeit more bloodied, battered, and vulnerable than at almost any point since 1979" ([Maloney, Foreign Affairs, 3 March 2026](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/middle-east/how-long-can-iranian-regime-hold-iran)), with casualty claims of at least **7,000** demonstrators from HRANA that conflict with lower figures reported elsewhere.

## Conclusion

Two conclusions follow for how revolutions should be understood. First, grievance is cheap and loyalty is decisive: the variable that separates regimes that survive a price shock from those that fall is whether soldiers and security services keep firing, which is why France's officer emigration, Kornilov's botched putsch and Iran's desertions matter more than any pamphlet or sermon. Second, ideology is best modelled as the answer to "who inherits", not "whether the regime falls" — it decides whether a broad coalition is consolidated into a party-state, a constitutional order or a clerical guardianship, and therefore also whether the outcome is a seven-decade regime, a decade of constitutional churn, or a 47-year theocracy still contested in 2026.

The forward-looking implication is deflationary about 1789 and 1917 analogies. Where the framework's first four variables are firing and the fifth is absent, the expected outcome is not revolution but a fragile or reconstituted authoritarianism; where the fifth is present but the fourth holds, the expected outcome is a surviving regime with recurring mass protest, which is the best available description of Iran as of November 2026. Genuine predictive confidence would require validated thresholds for food inflation, debt and elite fragmentation, and no source establishes any — the literature reports directional and conditional effects only, which limits all forecasting here to ranking and scenario-building rather than probability.
