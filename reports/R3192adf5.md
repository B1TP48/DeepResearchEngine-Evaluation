# Testing Responsible AI Principles Across Two Legal Cases

Predictive policing and AI contract analysis both show real but conditional benefits, and in both cases the risks depend on evaluation design, human verification, and who can contest outputs. For predictive policing, the independent evidence of crime reduction is thin and mixed: the strongest randomized trial with a positive result had authors with a financial stake in the vendor, and a randomized trial of another deployment found no significant effect on property crime ([RAND, RR-531](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR531.html); [CrimeSolutions](https://crimesolutions.ojp.gov/ratedprograms/predictive-policing-model-los-angeles-calif)). Bias is structural rather than incidental: models trained on police-recorded data can create feedback loops, and the fairness metrics used to judge them cannot all be satisfied at once ([Lum and Isaac, Significance](https://rss.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1740-9713.2016.00960.x); [Chouldechova, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.07524)). For contract analysis, the speed gains are well documented, but accuracy is uneven: legal research tools hallucinated in **17% to 33%** of answers in one preregistered study ([Magesh et al., JELS](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jels.12413)). Courts and bar regulators have placed responsibility on the lawyer who signs, not the vendor. The EU AI Act treats the two domains very differently: it bans only person-based crime prediction built on profiling, while contract review falls outside its named high-risk categories on the reading retrieved here ([artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/prohibited-ai-practices-under-the-ai-act/)). Across both cases, responsible use requires independent evaluation before deployment, documented error rates, human verification with real authority to override, and a route for affected people to contest outputs.

## Feedback loops and contested fairness in predictive policing

The central bias problem in place-based forecasting is not the model's stated features but its training data. Lum and Isaac showed that a PredPol-style model trained on unadjusted police records creates a loop in which flagged areas attract more patrols, which generate records that appear to confirm the prediction; they state that the conclusion applies to any predictive policing algorithm using such records ([Lum and Isaac, Significance](https://rss.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1740-9713.2016.00960.x)). An urn-model replication reports that the loop converges on sending police only to the highest-recorded neighbourhood even when arrests track the true crime rate ([arXiv:1706.09847](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.09847v2)). This is a simulation, and the sources found no peer-reviewed study measuring such bias in a current US or EU hotspot system, so the mechanism is well supported but not yet isolated in a field deployment.

Person-based risk tools raise a parallel problem that has been contested for a decade. ProPublica reported that COMPAS labelled Black defendants as high-risk who did not reoffend at almost twice the rate of white defendants, and that white defendants were more often mislabelled as low-risk ([ProPublica, "Machine Bias"](https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing)). The developer, Northpointe, argued that ProPublica's statistics were faulty ([Flores, Bechtel and Lowenkamp rejoinder](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/306032039_False_Positives_False_Negatives_and_False_Analyses_A_Rejoinder_to_Machine_Bias_There's_Software_Used_Across_the_Country_to_Predict_Future_Criminals_And_it's_Biased_Against_Blacks)). The dispute turns on definitions. Chouldechova showed that when recidivism prevalence differs across groups, the fairness criteria cannot all be satisfied together, so a tool can be calibrated yet produce higher false positive rates for the higher-prevalence group ([Chouldechova, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.07524)). Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan establish similar inherent trade-offs ([Kleinberg et al., ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/308327297_Inherent_Trade-Offs_in_the_Fair_Determination_of_Risk_Scores)). The practical implication is that a headline finding such as "twice as likely" depends on which error metric is chosen, and auditors must specify the metric before testing.

The simplicity result is the most striking challenge to the proprietary-complexity defence. Dressel and Farid reported COMPAS accuracy of **67.0%** for white defendants and **63.8%** for Black defendants, and found that the same accuracy could be reached with a two-feature linear classifier ([Dressel and Farid, Science Advances](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5777393/)). If a simple model matches a complex proprietary one, the opacity buys little demonstrable validity.

Public trust is the other dimension. A 2026 Dutch survey experiment with 877 citizens found that disclosing external oversight of police predictive algorithms significantly increased trust, while details about the legislation did not ([Nieuwenhuizen et al., Public Administration](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/padm.70034?af=R)). That suggests credible independent oversight matters more to trust than legal publicity. The evidence cuts unevenly, however. A preregistered Chicago experiment on a police "Sentiment Dashboard" (a community-feedback tool, not a predictive system) found trust rose among non-Black respondents and fell among Black respondents, especially when the dashboard was framed as monitoring ([PMC, "When trust building looks like surveillance"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13378587/)). Transparency may therefore reduce trust in communities that already experience monitoring as targeting. No peer-reviewed study was found that measures community trust before and after deployment of a predictive tool.

## Crime benefits, costs and cancellations in the policing evidence

The independent evidence on whether predictive policing reduces crime is thin and mostly null. The Shreveport trial was randomized, but RAND reports no statistically significant change in property crime between districts using predictive maps and control districts, and attributes this partly to low statistical power ([RAND, RR-531](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR531.html)). The Los Angeles trials produced the only positive crime result: patrols directed by the ETAS model led to an average **7.4%** reduction in crime volume relative to analyst-drawn hotspots, and NIJ CrimeSolutions rates the program "Promising" ([CrimeSolutions](https://crimesolutions.ojp.gov/ratedprograms/predictive-policing-model-los-angeles-calif)). Several of that study's authors held a financial stake in PredPol, which tempers the result ([R Street](https://www.rstreet.org/commentary/what-works-in-american-policing-part-6-predictive-policing-promise-pitfalls-and-the-limits-of-algorithmic-forecasting/)). Person-based systems performed worst. The Chicago Strategic Subject List was associated with no reduction in shootings or homicides and with increased arrests ([Saunders, Hunt and Hollywood, via ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/306071857_Predictions_put_into_practice_a_quasi-experimental_evaluation_of_Chicago's_predictive_policing_pilot)). The trial results disagree in direction, which probably reflects different models and implementations rather than a single verdict on the category.

Accuracy in deployment can be far lower than vendors suggest. The Markup examined Geolitica predictions for Plainfield, New Jersey, in 2018 and found that, restricted to locations police did not visit, **34** of **336** crime reports that followed a prediction matched its timeframe, location and crime type, an accuracy rate of about 10% ([The Markup](https://themarkup.org/show-your-work/2023/10/02/how-we-assessed-the-accuracy-of-predictive-policing-software)). Officers visited only **129** of the prediction locations, so predictions were rarely turned into patrol time ([The Markup, prediction bias](https://themarkup.org/prediction-bias/2023/10/02/predictive-policing-software-terrible-at-predicting-crimes)). The Plainfield study covers one city in one year and records only reported crime, so it cannot be generalized, but it shows the gap between a model's output and operational use.

The economic case is correspondingly small. RAND reports that treatment groups spent 6% to 10% less than control groups to achieve similar crime reduction, with savings mainly from lower officer overtime ([RAND, RR-531](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR531.html)). The dollar estimate of roughly **$13,000 to $20,000** less than status quo operations depends on standard pay and vehicle-cost assumptions ([Journalist's Resource](https://journalistsresource.org/criminal-justice/predictive-policing-randomized-controlled-trial/); [NIJ/RAND PDF](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/grants/248883.pdf)). A secondary review concludes that the cost savings were not accompanied by a significant decrease in crime ([Hardyns et al., 2017](https://creatingfutureus.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/HardynsEtAl-2017-PredictivePolicingAsNewTool.pdf)). Litigation, staffing and decommissioning costs were not measured in any independent source found. Chicago's dispute with the Sun-Times forced release of arrest-based database records from 2012 to 2016 ([Chicago Magazine, 2017](https://www.chicagomag.com/city-life/august-2017/chicago-police-strategic-subject-list/)), but no dollar figure was found.

The social costs are most visible in who is listed. An Upturn analysis found that **126,904** people on Chicago's list had never been arrested or been a victim of a crime ([Upturn](https://www.upturn.org/work/how-strategic-is-chicagos-strategic-subjects-list/)). The Chicago Inspector General found that the list relied heavily on arrest records even where arrests had not led to conviction ([Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/predictive-policing-explained)). Most programme closures followed audits and advocacy rather than controlled harm findings. The LAPD ended its LASER program in 2019 after an inspector general audit found problems with how individuals were selected and retained ([Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/predictive-policing-explained)), and the inspector general reported difficulty in isolating the software's effect ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/politicians-move-to-limit-predictive-policing-after-years-of-controversial-failures/)). Santa Cruz banned predictive policing in 2020 and allowed future use only on findings that the technology is scientifically validated and "will not perpetuate bias" ([Tenth Amendment Center reprint of city release](https://blog.tenthamendmentcenter.com/2020/07/santa-cruz-california-bans-facial-recognition-and-predictive-policing-technology/)). The vendor's CEO supported that ordinance ([GovTech](https://insider.govtech.com/california/news/city-is-first-in-nation-to-ban-police-predictive-technology.html)), a reminder that cancellation and procurement decisions are shaped by reputation and cost as well as evidence. The date of Chicago's list shelving is also contested: the Brennan Center gives January 2020, while the R Street summary places the Chicago OIG criticism in 2019. The primary Chicago OIG report would resolve the conflict.

## Accuracy, confidentiality and accountability in contract analysis

Contract tools share the hallucination problem of legal research tools, and the best peer-reviewed evidence is about research, not drafting. Magesh and colleagues tested over 200 queries across commercial tools in a preregistered study published in the *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*: Lexis+ AI and Westlaw's tools each hallucinated between **17% and 33%** of the time, and the authors state that vendor "hallucination-free" claims are overstated ([Magesh et al., JELS](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jels.12413)). Grounding reduces but does not eliminate hallucination, so these figures are a floor for contract work rather than a measured contract error rate. The main contract benchmark, the Vals Legal AI Report (February 2025), shows lawyers at **79.7%** on redlining against **65.0%** for the best AI tool, while AI matched or beat lawyers on extraction ([ContractSafe, citing Vals](https://www.contractsafe.com/blog/ai-contract-review-software)). The Vals benchmark has been criticized: its evaluator was an LLM, some averages rest on few questions, and LexisNexis withdrew before publication ([Tokuma Labs](https://tokumalabs.com/insights/reviewing-the-review/)). Three of four generative tools also missed a standard Most Favored Nation clause ([Artificial Lawyer](https://www.artificiallawyer.com/2025/03/12/missed-mfn-gate-and-ai-contract-review/)). The vendor-run LawGeex study, which reported **94%** accuracy against **85%** for twenty lawyers on five NDAs, is not peer reviewed and has not been replicated ([PR Newswire, LawGeex](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/artificial-intelligence-more-accurate-than-lawyers-for-reviewing-contracts-new-study-reveals-300603781.html)).

Bias and disparity evidence for contract tools is largely absent. Vendor and practitioner sources argue that tools trained on US agreements may underperform on English-law or non-English contracts ([Beings](https://beings.com/can-you-use-ai-for-contract-analysis/)), and one vendor reports drops of 15 to 25 percentage points on non-English documents without citing a source ([Alice Labs](https://alicelabs.ai/en/insights/ai-contract-analysis)). These are plausible, unmeasured concerns. No peer-reviewed study was found that compares contract tool accuracy across languages or legal systems, or that examines demographic fairness in contract tools.

Confidentiality is the most concrete legal constraint. In the US, ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 2024) applies existing Model Rules to generative AI. It requires lawyers to weigh disclosure risk and safeguards before inputting client information, and to obtain informed consent before using self-learning tools whose outputs could disclose client information ([Rulex](https://rulex.org/united-states/guides/aba-formal-opinion-512)). It states that boilerplate in engagement letters is not sufficient consent ([Legal AI Governance](https://legalaigovernance.com/resources/aba-opinion-512/)). The opinion is advisory, and enforcement depends on state bars ([Makelaw Substack](https://makelaw.substack.com/p/into-the-cybersecurity-breach)). The EU position is stricter by default. The CCBE guide of 2 October 2025 is non-binding but says lawyers may not input personal, confidential or client-related information into GenAI tools ([eucrim](https://eucrim.eu/news/ccbe-issues-guidance-on-generative-ai-use-in-legal-practice/)), and a Belgian practitioner guide notes that GDPR Article 28 requires a written processor agreement that consumer AI tools do not offer ([Brieflee](https://www.brieflee.be/blog/professional-secrecy-ai-guide-belgian-lawyers)). No source documents how specific contract vendors use client data for training.

Accountability has been assigned consistently to lawyers. In *Mata v. Avianca* (S.D.N.Y. 2023), the court imposed sanctions for filing fabricated ChatGPT citations ([Wikipedia, Mata v. Avianca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mata_v._Avianca,_Inc.)); the sanctions were for failing to verify and to correct after the problem was raised, not for using AI as such ([Legal Agent](https://legalagent.co.jp/en/legal-lab/ai-news/mata-v-avianca-ai-fake-citations/)). Standing orders are now common. Judge Starr of the Northern District of Texas issued one of the earliest AI certification orders in May 2023 ([Trace](https://trace.law/kb/court-ai-disclosure-orders)), and one tracker counts **113** active court orders on AI use by spring 2026 ([Case Read](https://www.caseread.ai/blog/do-lawyers-have-to-disclose-ai-use-to-courts)). No court or regulator was found that held a vendor liable for an AI error. Contract-review errors that never reach a court have no reported sanction in the sources reviewed.

## Economic and labour effects of AI contract review

Efficiency gains are the best-supported finding. In a 2024 randomized trial, GPT-4 access produced large, consistent speed gains for law students, but only slight and inconsistent improvements in quality ([Choi, Monahan and Schwarcz, Minnesota Law Review](https://scholarship.law.umn.edu/minnlrev/vol109/iss1/3/)). The 2025 Schwarcz et al. trial of six realistic assignments found large productivity gains on five of six tasks, but its one transactional task, NDA drafting, showed no quality improvement ([LawNext](https://www.lawnext.com/2025/03/another-new-study-of-legal-ai-shows-some-models-can-significantly-improve-work-quality-and-efficiency.html)). The grounded tool produced roughly as many factual errors as no-AI work ([BABL AI](https://babl.ai/study-shows-legal-ai-tools-can-improve-work-quality-speed/)). No randomized study of AI on negotiated, high-value commercial contract review was located. The efficiency case for contract work therefore rests on speed and extraction, not demonstrated drafting quality.

Labour evidence is mostly projection. The BLS projects **863,700** lawyer jobs in 2025 growing 5% over 2025–35, and states that some routine legal work may be automated, but that this is "not expected to reduce overall demand for lawyers" ([BLS OOH, Lawyers](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/legal/lawyers.htm)). For paralegals, the BLS projects **404,900** jobs in 2025 and 0% change over the decade ([BLS OOH, Paralegals](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/legal/paralegals-and-legal-assistants.htm)). An earlier BLS projection with a different base put paralegal growth at 1.2% and said AI could greatly enhance productivity in document review ([BLS TED](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/ai-impacts-in-bls-employment-projections.htm)). The 2025–35 figure should be preferred. Exposure studies rank legal work highly because document-heavy tasks overlap with language model capability ([Eloundou et al., Science](https://www.science.org/stoken/author-tokens/ST-2100/full)), but exposure measures task overlap, not job loss. Thomson Reuters' 2026 survey says AI "may reduce demand for some junior roles" while raising demand for hybrid tech-legal roles ([Thomson Reuters Institute](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/institute/reports/future-of-professionals-law-firms-paper-2026)). The reasonable reading is task restructuring, with junior document-production work most at risk, though no measured post-adoption employment data was found.

Billing pressure is the clearest economic effect, but the figures are vendor-produced. Clio reports that 75% of solos and 65% of small firms use flat fees, while 84% still use hourly rates ([Clio blog](https://www.clio.com/blog/solo-small-law-firms-highlights-2025-legal-trends/)). It estimates that lawyers who keep traditional billing could lose up to $27,000 a year, and that 74% of billable work could be automated ([Clio press release](https://www.clio.com/about/press/legal-trends-solo-small-law-firms-2025/); [Clio blog](https://www.clio.com/blog/death-of-the-billable-hour-legal-ai/)). These are vendor estimates, not measured losses. Access-to-justice evidence is equally limited. A field study of 91 legal-aid professionals reported that 90% saw increased productivity and that concierge-supported participants did significantly better than controls ([Chien and Kim, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4733061); [Loyola Law Review](https://digitalcommons.lmu.edu/llr/vol57/iss4/2/)). No study traced productivity into lower client fees, and small-business contract costs were not measured.

## Frameworks: what they require and where they conflict

The EU AI Act is the only retrieved framework that combines prohibitions, high-risk classification and legal duties. Article 5(1)(d) prohibits AI that assesses or predicts the risk of a person committing a criminal offence **solely** on profiling or personality traits. The exception applies only where AI supports a human assessment already based on objective and verifiable facts directly linked to a criminal activity ([Bundesnetzagentur, prohibited practices](https://www.bundesnetzagentur.de/EN/Areas/Digitalisation/AI/08_ProhibitedPractices/start.html)). Commission guidance states that place-based predictive policing falls outside this ban, but that a location score used to profile an individual, such as a resident, can be caught ([artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/prohibited-ai-practices-under-the-ai-act/)). Annex III places law enforcement risk assessment, including recidivism prediction, and the administration of justice in the high-risk category ([Bundesnetzagentur, high-risk](https://www.bundesnetzagentur.de/EN/Areas/Digitalisation/AI/09_HighRisk/start.html); [euaiact.com](https://www.euaiact.com/blog/eu-ai-act-high-risk-classification)). Contract review tools are not addressed in any retrieved source. Unless used by judicial authorities, they appear to fall outside Annex III, which is an inference rather than a finding.

The timeline has shifted. The Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) was published on 24 July 2026 and deferred the Annex III high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027, with the stated reason that harmonised standards are not finished ([regulation-ai.eu](https://www.regulation-ai.eu/en/omnibus/); [Cooley](https://cdp.cooley.com/digital-ai-omnibus-delays-key-deadlines-introduces-new-rules/)). One source lists a 2030 date for public-authority systems, which the other sources do not mention, so it is unconfirmed. Readers should check the Official Journal before relying on dates.

UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation and NIST's AI RMF are voluntary but usable. UNESCO says AI systems should be auditable and traceable ([UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics)) and must not displace ultimate human responsibility and accountability ([UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/en/recommendation)). NIST organises risk management into GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE, and says systematic documentation increases transparency and accountability ([NIST AI 100-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/nist.ai.100-1.pdf)). The two frameworks treat human oversight differently: the AI Act makes human assessment a legal condition for an exception, UNESCO a principle of accountability, and NIST a risk-management practice. The primary text of the OECD principles and IEEE Ethically Aligned Design was not retrieved, so this report makes no claim about them.

Legal-ethics guidance converges on existing duties. The UK SRA says AI has no separate legal personality, so solicitors remain accountable for outputs, and client information should enter AI systems only with appropriate contractual and technical safeguards ([SRA](https://www.sra.org.uk/solicitors/guidance/misuse-ai/)). US federal policy has moved the other way: Biden's Executive Order 14110 was revoked, and a December 2025 order preempts state AI laws, but it leaves state government use of AI, including law enforcement, to state authority ([Snell & Wilmer](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/white-house-releases-national-policy-framework-for-artificial-intelligence/)). The sources differ on the date of the 2025 revocation order, reported as 20 January ([Beginners in AI](https://beginnersinai.org/us-ai-policy/)) and 23 January ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/guides/ai-regulation-in-the-us)). No source retrieved addresses state law on pretrial or recidivism tools, or the prosecutor and judge duties that would govern criminal-justice AI.

## Conclusion

The two cases share one lesson: the legal system's principles are only as strong as their enforcement. Predictive policing has the more serious harms, because a biased model shapes who is watched, stopped and listed, and because the best evidence of crime benefit is both thin and conflicted. The EU's profiling ban and the Chicago and LAPD audits show that regulation and oversight can work, but both came after deployment. A responsible deployer should require an independent, pre-specified evaluation with a named error metric, publish the audit, and test whether any benefit survives an honest comparison with conventional hotspot policing.

Contract analysis carries less direct risk to liberty but more uncertainty about quality. Its benefits are real in speed and extraction, but hallucination, missed clauses and unmeasured performance in non-English or civil-law contracts mean that the verifying lawyer carries the risk. The labour effect is more likely to change how junior work is organised than to remove legal jobs outright, though the evidence is projection rather than measurement. Several gaps remain across both cases: no field study measures how a US or EU predictive tool changes neighbourhood arrests, no independent benchmark measures contract tools across jurisdictions, and no source addresses how prosecutors or judges should apply the professional duties. Closing these gaps is the most useful next step for responsible AI in law.
